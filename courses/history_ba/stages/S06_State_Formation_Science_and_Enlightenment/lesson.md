# S06_State_Formation_Science_and_Enlightenment - Lesson: Absolutism and Louis XIV; the Scientific Revolution; the early modern Atlantic slave trade

## Goal
The learner explains absolutism and state formation under Louis XIV, the Scientific Revolution and its intellectual consequences, and the scale and structure of the early modern Atlantic slave trade.

## Syllabus items taught here
- L5.SF.1 - Absolutism and state formation: Louis XIV's France and the theory of divine-right monarchy
- L5.SF.2 - The Scientific Revolution: Copernicus, Galileo and Newton, and its intellectual consequences for early Enlightenment thought
- L5.SF.3 - The early modern Atlantic slave trade and Europe's expanding global connections

## How to teach this
Ask: what does it take to turn a kingdom of quarrelsome, semi-independent nobles into a centralised state that answers to one man? This course builds directly on GCSE History and A-level History (Edexcel 9HI0, Tudor England/German Reformation/Tudor rebellion), which the learner has already completed: do not re-teach Tudor political or religious history, and use the learner's existing source-criticism and essay-planning skills as a floor, not a ceiling. Degree-level history goes further in the ways the QAA benchmark statement specifies: engaging critically with historiography (not just facts, but how and why historians have disagreed), constructing the learner's own historical questions rather than only answering set ones, synthesising large and sometimes conflicting bodies of evidence, and sustaining a longer, more theoretically aware argument. Push the learner to name historians and schools of interpretation, not just events, and to interrogate sources for genre, purpose and silence, not only content. Historical facts (events, dates, figures) do not change with time; historiographical debates are living scholarship and should be taught as ongoing argument, not settled fact.

#### L5.SF.1 Absolutism and state formation: Louis XIV's France and the theory of divine-right monarchy
**Absolutism and Louis XIV.** Louis XIV of France (r.1643-1715, personal rule from 1661) is the paradigm case of early modern absolutism: government theoretically unconstrained by any institution (no functioning Estates-General was called between 1614 and 1789), justified by the theory of divine-right monarchy (the king answerable to God alone). Louis's early reign was shaped by the Fronde (a series of noble and parliamentary rebellions, 1648-53) that nearly toppled the monarchy while he was a child - an experience historians see as decisive in his later determination to control the nobility directly, notably by requiring leading nobles to attend him at the vast, deliberately overwhelming palace of Versailles (construction from 1661), where court ritual and royal patronage substituted for independent noble power. Louis centralised administration (through intendants, royally appointed provincial officials bypassing local nobility) and pursued religious uniformity, revoking the Edict of Nantes (1598, which had granted toleration to Protestant Huguenots) with the Edict of Fontainebleau (1685), provoking a significant Huguenot exodus. Historians debate how far Louis's 'absolutism' was really as complete in practice as court propaganda claimed, given the practical limits of pre-modern communication and local power.

#### L5.SF.2 The Scientific Revolution: Copernicus, Galileo and Newton, and its intellectual consequences for early Enlightenment thought
**The Scientific Revolution.** Nicolaus Copernicus's *De revolutionibus orbium coelestium* (1543) proposed a heliocentric (sun-centred) model of the solar system, challenging the geocentric Ptolemaic/Aristotelian model that had underpinned both astronomy and much theology for centuries. Galileo Galilei's telescopic observations (from 1609) provided strong empirical support for heliocentrism; his conflict with the Catholic Church culminated in his trial by the Roman Inquisition (1633), forced recantation, and house arrest - a famous case study in the relationship (and tension) between new science and religious authority. Isaac Newton's *Philosophiae Naturalis Principia Mathematica* (1687) synthesised earlier work (including Kepler's laws of planetary motion) into a unified mathematical account of motion and universal gravitation, providing a model of the universe as governed by discoverable, mathematical natural laws. This shift - from authority-based to observation- and mathematics-based knowledge - is widely credited by historians as a crucial intellectual precondition for the Enlightenment's confidence that reason and empirical method could be applied to society, politics and religion as well as to nature.

#### L5.SF.3 The early modern Atlantic slave trade and Europe's expanding global connections
**The early modern Atlantic slave trade.** From the sixteenth century, European powers (initially Portugal and Spain, later heavily the Dutch, British and French) built a transatlantic system transporting enslaved Africans to the Americas, historians' modern estimates putting the total at approximately 12.5 million people forcibly embarked over the trade's several centuries, with a substantial proportion dying during the 'Middle Passage' (the transatlantic crossing) due to horrific conditions. The trade formed one leg of a 'triangular trade': manufactured goods from Europe to West Africa, enslaved people from West Africa to the Americas, and plantation goods (sugar, tobacco, later cotton) from the Americas back to Europe. It underpinned the plantation economies (most intensively sugar in the Caribbean and Brazil) that generated enormous wealth for European trading nations and, later, the Southern colonies/states of British North America. This forced global connection is essential context for both the Haitian Revolution (S03) and for understanding the economic underpinnings of the European state power and later industrial wealth studied later in this stage.
