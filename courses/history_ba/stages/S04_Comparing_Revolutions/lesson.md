# S04_Comparing_Revolutions - Lesson: Theories of revolution; the Russian Revolution of 1917 as a comparative case; synthesis

## Goal
The learner compares structural and contingent theories of revolution (Skocpol; the Marxist-revisionist debate), explains the causes and immediate consequences of the 1917 Russian Revolutions as a comparative case, and writes a comparative essay synthesising revolutions across periods.

## Syllabus items taught here
- L4.CR.1 - Comparing revolutions: structural/social-scientific theories (Skocpol) versus political/contingent explanations, and the Marxist-revisionist debate on 1789
- L4.CR.2 - The Russian Revolution of 1917 as a comparative case and a bridge to Stage 3: February and October, causes and immediate consequences
- L4.CR.3 - Synthesis: what, if anything, makes a revolution 'revolutionary'? Writing a comparative essay across periods

## How to teach this
Ask: do revolutions happen because deep social structures make them inevitable, or because of what particular people decided to do on particular days? This course builds directly on GCSE History and A-level History (Edexcel 9HI0, Tudor England/German Reformation/Tudor rebellion), which the learner has already completed: do not re-teach Tudor political or religious history, and use the learner's existing source-criticism and essay-planning skills as a floor, not a ceiling. Degree-level history goes further in the ways the QAA benchmark statement specifies: engaging critically with historiography (not just facts, but how and why historians have disagreed), constructing the learner's own historical questions rather than only answering set ones, synthesising large and sometimes conflicting bodies of evidence, and sustaining a longer, more theoretically aware argument. Push the learner to name historians and schools of interpretation, not just events, and to interrogate sources for genre, purpose and silence, not only content. Historical facts (events, dates, figures) do not change with time; historiographical debates are living scholarship and should be taught as ongoing argument, not settled fact.

#### L4.CR.1 Comparing revolutions: structural/social-scientific theories (Skocpol) versus political/contingent explanations, and the Marxist-revisionist debate on 1789
**Comparing revolutions: structure versus contingency.** Theda Skocpol's *States and Social Revolutions* (1979) offered an influential structural theory, comparing France (1789), Russia (1917) and China (1949) to argue that successful social revolutions occur where a state's international/military pressures combine with an agrarian structure that allows peasant revolt against a weakened, administratively overstretched old regime - revolutions, on this view, are not simply 'made' by revolutionaries but happen when structural conditions align. Critics argue this understates contingency, leadership and ideology, and can look teleological in hindsight (fitting only the cases that succeeded). This mirrors the Marxist-revisionist debate over the French Revolution specifically (S03): Marxist historians read 1789 through underlying class/economic structure, while revisionists (Cobban, Furet) emphasise political contingency, ideas and the specific decisions of specific actors. Comparative history of this kind is valuable precisely because it tests whether an explanation that works for one revolution generalises, or whether each revolution needs its own, more particular explanation.

#### L4.CR.2 The Russian Revolution of 1917 as a comparative case and a bridge to Stage 3: February and October, causes and immediate consequences
**The Russian Revolution of 1917 as a comparative case.** Long-term strains (a rigid autocracy under Tsar Nicholas II, an overwhelmingly agrarian peasant economy alongside a small but concentrated and radicalising industrial working class, and a state stretched to breaking point by the First World War) fit Skocpol's structural model closely. The February Revolution (March 1917 by the Western calendar) began with bread riots and strikes in Petrograd, spread rapidly amid army mutiny, and forced Nicholas II's abdication, ending three centuries of Romanov rule; a Provisional Government shared uneasy power with the Petrograd Soviet ('dual power'). The Provisional Government's decision to continue the unpopular war, and its failure to resolve land hunger, let the Bolsheviks under Vladimir Lenin (who returned from exile in April 1917 with his radical 'April Theses': 'Peace, Land, Bread', and all power to the Soviets) grow rapidly in support. The October Revolution (25 October/7 November 1917) was a comparatively small, swift Bolshevik seizure of power in Petrograd, not a mass uprising - a contrast worth weighing against Skocpol's structural framing, since the actual seizure of power depended heavily on Bolshevik organisation and timing, not just background conditions.

#### L4.CR.3 Synthesis: what, if anything, makes a revolution 'revolutionary'? Writing a comparative essay across periods
**Synthesis: what makes a revolution 'revolutionary'?** Comparing the English Civil War/Glorious Revolution, the American, French and Haitian Revolutions, and 1917 Russia raises a genuinely comparative question: is a 'revolution' defined by violence, by the overthrow of a ruler, by a change in the underlying social/economic order, or by a lasting change in ideas about legitimate authority? By these different tests, the revolutions studied here rank very differently - 1688-89 was nearly bloodless but constitutionally transformative; Haiti overthrew both a ruler and an entire economic/social order (slavery) but is historiographically underweighted; the French Revolution combined violent rupture with a lasting shift in political ideas (popular sovereignty, rights) that outlived its own instability. Writing a genuinely comparative essay means selecting a clear basis for comparison (causes, methods, or outcomes), using specific, accurate evidence from each case rather than generalising, and reaching a judgement about what, if anything, the cases have in common - the central skill this course now expects going into Stage 2 and Stage 3.
