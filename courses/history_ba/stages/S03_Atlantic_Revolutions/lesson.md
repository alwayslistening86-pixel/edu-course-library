# S03_Atlantic_Revolutions - Lesson: The American, French and Haitian Revolutions

## Goal
The learner explains the causes and course of the American, French and Haitian Revolutions, and the significance of the Haitian Revolution's long marginalisation in older historiography.

## Syllabus items taught here
- L4.AR.1 - The American Revolution: causes (taxation, representation, Enlightenment ideas) and the Declaration of Independence, 1765-83
- L4.AR.2 - The French Revolution: causes, the fall of the monarchy, and the Terror, 1789-94
- L4.AR.3 - The Haitian Revolution: Toussaint Louverture, the only successful large-scale slave revolt, and its marginalisation in older historiography, 1791-1804

## How to teach this
Ask: why do most people know the French Revolution's date but not the Haitian Revolution's, even though Haiti's was the only one to abolish slavery and win full independence for the people who made it? This course builds directly on GCSE History and A-level History (Edexcel 9HI0, Tudor England/German Reformation/Tudor rebellion), which the learner has already completed: do not re-teach Tudor political or religious history, and use the learner's existing source-criticism and essay-planning skills as a floor, not a ceiling. Degree-level history goes further in the ways the QAA benchmark statement specifies: engaging critically with historiography (not just facts, but how and why historians have disagreed), constructing the learner's own historical questions rather than only answering set ones, synthesising large and sometimes conflicting bodies of evidence, and sustaining a longer, more theoretically aware argument. Push the learner to name historians and schools of interpretation, not just events, and to interrogate sources for genre, purpose and silence, not only content. Historical facts (events, dates, figures) do not change with time; historiographical debates are living scholarship and should be taught as ongoing argument, not settled fact.

#### L4.AR.1 The American Revolution: causes (taxation, representation, Enlightenment ideas) and the Declaration of Independence, 1765-83
**The American Revolution, 1765-83.** Britain's attempt to raise revenue from its thirteen American colonies after the costly Seven Years' War (1756-63) produced escalating conflict: the Stamp Act (1765, a direct tax on printed material) provoked the rallying cry 'no taxation without representation' (colonists were not represented in Westminster); the Townshend Acts (1767) and the Boston Tea Party (1773, a protest against the Tea Act, dumping British tea into Boston harbour) deepened the crisis; Britain's punitive 'Intolerable Acts' (1774) pushed the colonies toward united resistance (the First Continental Congress, 1774). War began in 1775 (Lexington and Concord); the Second Continental Congress adopted the Declaration of Independence (4 July 1776), drafted principally by Thomas Jefferson and drawing on Enlightenment natural-rights ideas (notably John Locke's argument that legitimate government rests on the consent of the governed). French support (from 1778) proved decisive militarily; British defeat at Yorktown (1781) led to the Treaty of Paris (1783) recognising American independence. Historians debate how far the Revolution was a genuinely radical break (a new republic built on Enlightenment principle) versus a conservative movement to preserve existing colonial liberties against new taxation.

#### L4.AR.2 The French Revolution: causes, the fall of the monarchy, and the Terror, 1789-94
**The French Revolution, 1789-94.** France's near-bankruptcy (worsened by its support for the American Revolution), a rigid social/legal structure of privileged Estates (clergy, nobility, and the Third Estate - everyone else), and poor harvests (1788) produced crisis. Louis XVI summoned the Estates-General (May 1789) for the first time since 1614; when the Third Estate's demands were resisted, its deputies declared themselves a National Assembly and swore the Tennis Court Oath (June 1789) not to disband until France had a constitution. Popular Parisian action - the storming of the Bastille (14 July 1789, since commemorated as Bastille Day) - and rural unrest ('the Great Fear') forced concessions: the abolition of feudal privileges and the Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen (August 1789). The monarchy's position collapsed further after Louis's failed flight to Varennes (1791); the monarchy was abolished and a republic declared (September 1792); Louis XVI was tried and executed (January 1793). Facing foreign war and internal counter-revolution, the Committee of Public Safety under Maximilien Robespierre pursued the Terror (1793-94), executing perceived enemies of the Revolution (including, eventually, Robespierre himself, in the Thermidorian Reaction of July 1794).

#### L4.AR.3 The Haitian Revolution: Toussaint Louverture, the only successful large-scale slave revolt, and its marginalisation in older historiography, 1791-1804
**The Haitian Revolution, 1791-1804.** In the French colony of Saint-Domingue, the most profitable slave colony in the Americas, enslaved Africans launched a massive uprising in August 1791, inspired partly by the French Revolution's own language of rights (which French revolutionaries did not initially extend to enslaved people) and partly by existing traditions of resistance. Under the leadership of Toussaint Louverture, a former slave and skilled general/administrator, the rebellion evolved into a sustained war that defeated French, Spanish and British forces in turn; Louverture was captured and died in French custody (1803), but his successor Jean-Jacques Dessalines completed the struggle, declaring Haiti's independence on 1 January 1804 - the only successful large-scale slave revolt in history to produce an independent state, and the second independent republic in the Americas after the United States. For much of the twentieth century, mainstream Western historiography marginalised the Haitian Revolution relative to the American and French Revolutions, a pattern the historian C.L.R. James's *The Black Jacobins* (1938) challenged directly by placing it at the centre of Atlantic revolutionary history; more recent historians (e.g. Laurent Dubois) have continued to recover its full significance, including its influence on later abolitionist movements and the fear it provoked among slaveholding powers.
