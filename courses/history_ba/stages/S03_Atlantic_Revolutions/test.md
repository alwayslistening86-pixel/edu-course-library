## Test items
1. Explain why the Haitian Revolution was historiographically marginalised for much of the twentieth century, and how this has changed. [6 marks]
2. 'The French Revolution of 1789 was a bourgeois revolution driven by class conflict.' Assess the validity of this view. [30 marks]

## Answer key (for the tutor only)
1. [6] B1 Western historiography for much of the 20th century centred the American and French Revolutions as the paradigm 'Age of Revolutions'; B1 racial assumptions and the discomfort of former colonial/slaveholding powers with a successful slave-led revolution contributed to its neglect; B1 C.L.R. James's The Black Jacobins (1938) directly challenged this marginalisation, placing Haiti at the centre of Atlantic revolutionary history; B1 later historians (e.g. Laurent Dubois) have continued to recover the Revolution's significance and its links to abolitionism; B1 the change reflects a broader historiographical shift (decolonising/global history) toward recovering non-European agency; B1 credit recognition that the Revolution's outcome (full abolition and independence) was more radical than either the American or French Revolutions achieved for enslaved people.
2. [30] Mark out of 30 using this course's undergraduate-history generic levels, adapting the alevel_history-style levels convention to honours standard and to the QAA benchmark's description of graduate historical skill: Level 1 (1-6) largely descriptive/narrative, little independent analysis, no real engagement with historiography; Level 2 (7-12) some analysis and awareness that historians disagree, but underdeveloped, uneven use of evidence; Level 3 (13-18) a structured argument that explains a range of relevant factors, engages with named historians or schools of interpretation, and reaches a judgement, though depth or balance may be uneven; Level 4 (19-24) a well-organised, evidenced argument that weighs competing historiographical positions explicitly and reaches a supported judgement, showing real synthesis of complex material; Level 5 (25-30) a sophisticated, wide-ranging argument that formulates its own sharp historical question, synthesises extensive and sometimes conflicting evidence, engages critically and precisely with historiography, and sustains a fully substantiated, original judgement throughout. Credit: the Marxist case (Lefebvre, Soboul) - the Third Estate's rising bourgeoisie resisting aristocratic/clerical privilege, the Declaration of the Rights of Man asserting legal equality that served bourgeois economic interests; the revisionist challenge (Cobban, Furet) - questioning whether the Third Estate's leadership was straightforwardly 'bourgeois' in a Marxist economic sense, and stressing political/ideological rather than class causation; a strong answer engages explicitly with this historiographical debate by name rather than only narrating events.
