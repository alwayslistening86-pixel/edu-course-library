# S08_Empire_Ireland_and_Suffrage - Lesson: The Victorian Empire; the Irish Famine and nationalism; the women's suffrage movement

## Goal
The learner explains the expansion and ideology of the Victorian British Empire and its critics, the causes and consequences of the Great Irish Famine and the growth of Irish nationalism, and the causes, methods and progress of the women's suffrage movement to 1914.

## Syllabus items taught here
- L5.EI.1 - The Victorian British Empire: expansion, imperial ideology and its critics
- L5.EI.2 - The Great Irish Famine, 1845-52, and the growth of Irish nationalism
- L5.EI.3 - The women's suffrage movement to 1914: suffragists (NUWSS) and suffragettes (WSPU)

## How to teach this
Ask: how did a small island end up ruling roughly a quarter of the world's population and land by 1900 - and who paid the price? This course builds directly on GCSE History and A-level History (Edexcel 9HI0, Tudor England/German Reformation/Tudor rebellion), which the learner has already completed: do not re-teach Tudor political or religious history, and use the learner's existing source-criticism and essay-planning skills as a floor, not a ceiling. Degree-level history goes further in the ways the QAA benchmark statement specifies: engaging critically with historiography (not just facts, but how and why historians have disagreed), constructing the learner's own historical questions rather than only answering set ones, synthesising large and sometimes conflicting bodies of evidence, and sustaining a longer, more theoretically aware argument. Push the learner to name historians and schools of interpretation, not just events, and to interrogate sources for genre, purpose and silence, not only content. Historical facts (events, dates, figures) do not change with time; historiographical debates are living scholarship and should be taught as ongoing argument, not settled fact.

#### L5.EI.1 The Victorian British Empire: expansion, imperial ideology and its critics
**The Victorian British Empire.** British imperial expansion accelerated across the nineteenth century, from the formalisation of Crown rule over India (the British Raj, established 1858, after the Indian Rebellion of 1857 ended the East India Company's direct governance) to the 'Scramble for Africa' (the rapid, competitive European partition of the continent from the 1880s, formalised diplomatically at the Berlin Conference, 1884-85). Imperial ideology combined economic motives (raw materials, markets, investment opportunities) with a self-justifying rhetoric of racial and cultural superiority - a 'civilising mission' often expressed through Social Darwinist ideas applying evolutionary competition to races and nations. Critics existed even at the time: the economist J.A. Hobson's *Imperialism: A Study* (1902) argued imperialism served narrow financial/capitalist interests at the expense of the wider national interest, an analysis later influential on Marxist theories of imperialism (notably Lenin's). By 1900 the British Empire covered roughly a quarter of the world's land area and population, making its ideology, administration and resistance to it central to understanding modern British and global history.

#### L5.EI.2 The Great Irish Famine, 1845-52, and the growth of Irish nationalism
**The Great Irish Famine, 1845-52.** A potato blight (Phytophthora infestans) destroyed successive harvests of the crop that a large proportion of Ireland's rural poor depended on almost exclusively for subsistence, given the structure of landholding under British/Anglo-Irish landlordism. The resulting famine killed roughly one million people and drove roughly one to two million more to emigrate (heavily to the United States, transforming Irish-American political culture for generations); Ireland's population fell by perhaps a quarter within a decade. The British government's response is heavily criticised by historians: adherence to laissez-faire economic doctrine (limiting direct state relief on principle) and the conduct of officials such as Charles Trevelyan (who saw the famine partly in providentialist terms, as a natural check on Irish over-population) worsened the death toll; grain continued to be exported from Ireland during the famine under the protection of the law, a fact that became a lasting grievance. The Famine's demographic and political shock accelerated Irish nationalism, from the Young Ireland movement of the 1840s to the later, constitutional Home Rule movement led by Charles Stewart Parnell from the 1870s-80s.

#### L5.EI.3 The women's suffrage movement to 1914: suffragists (NUWSS) and suffragettes (WSPU)
**The women's suffrage movement to 1914.** Organised campaigning for women's votes in Britain intensified from the late nineteenth century. The National Union of Women's Suffrage Societies (NUWSS), founded 1897 and led by Millicent Fawcett, pursued 'constitutional' methods - petitioning, lobbying, public meetings - believing patient, lawful pressure would eventually succeed. The Women's Social and Political Union (WSPU), founded 1903 by Emmeline Pankhurst, grew impatient with slow progress and adopted militant tactics ('Deeds not Words'): disrupting political meetings, window-smashing, arson, and hunger strikes when imprisoned (met by the government's controversial force-feeding, and later the 'Cat and Mouse Act', 1913, which released and re-arrested hunger-striking prisoners). Emily Davison's death after stepping in front of the King's horse at the 1913 Derby became a defining, still-debated moment (whether intended as martyrdom or protest gone wrong). The First World War interrupted the campaign; partial suffrage for women over 30 (with property qualifications) came in 1918, and equal voting rights with men (from 21) only in 1928 - historians still debate how far the vote in 1918 was a reward for wartime service versus the culmination of decades of pre-war campaigning by both NUWSS and WSPU.
