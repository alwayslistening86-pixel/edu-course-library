# S10_Interwar_Crisis_Fascism_and_Stalinism - Lesson: Weimar and the rise of Nazism; Italian Fascism; Stalinism in the 1930s

## Goal
The learner explains the instability of the Weimar Republic and the rise of Nazism, the consolidation of Italian Fascism under Mussolini and the historiographical debate over its nature, and the Stalinist Soviet Union of the 1930s.

## Syllabus items taught here
- L6.IF.1 - The instability of the Weimar Republic and the rise of Nazism, 1919-33
- L6.IF.2 - Italian Fascism under Mussolini: ideology, consolidation of power and historiographical debate
- L6.IF.3 - Stalinism in the 1930s: collectivisation, the Five-Year Plans and the Great Terror

## How to teach this
Ask: what does it take for a democracy to vote itself out of existence? This course builds directly on GCSE History and A-level History (Edexcel 9HI0, Tudor England/German Reformation/Tudor rebellion), which the learner has already completed: do not re-teach Tudor political or religious history, and use the learner's existing source-criticism and essay-planning skills as a floor, not a ceiling. Degree-level history goes further in the ways the QAA benchmark statement specifies: engaging critically with historiography (not just facts, but how and why historians have disagreed), constructing the learner's own historical questions rather than only answering set ones, synthesising large and sometimes conflicting bodies of evidence, and sustaining a longer, more theoretically aware argument. Push the learner to name historians and schools of interpretation, not just events, and to interrogate sources for genre, purpose and silence, not only content. Historical facts (events, dates, figures) do not change with time; historiographical debates are living scholarship and should be taught as ongoing argument, not settled fact.

#### L6.IF.1 The instability of the Weimar Republic and the rise of Nazism, 1919-33
**Weimar instability and the rise of Nazism, 1919-33.** The Weimar Republic, Germany's new democracy established in 1919, faced crisis almost from birth: the humiliation and economic burden of Versailles, political extremism from both left and right, and catastrophic hyperinflation (1923, worsened by French/Belgian occupation of the Ruhr after Germany defaulted on reparations - by late 1923 the currency was effectively worthless), which wiped out savings and discredited the Republic among the middle class. A relatively stable period followed under Gustav Stresemann (Chancellor then Foreign Minister, mid-1920s), who stabilised the currency, negotiated reduced reparations (the Dawes Plan) and improved Germany's international standing (the Locarno Treaties, League membership) - though this recovery depended heavily on American loans. Adolf Hitler's National Socialist party, which had attempted a failed coup (the Beer Hall Putsch, Munich, 1923, after which Hitler wrote *Mein Kampf* in prison), was a marginal force through the mid-1920s but grew explosively after the Great Depression (from 1929) devastated the German economy and discredited mainstream parties; the Nazis became the largest party in the Reichstag by 1932. Hitler was appointed Chancellor (30 January 1933) through conservative elite miscalculation (believing they could control him); the Reichstag Fire (February 1933) provided a pretext for suspending civil liberties, and the Enabling Act (March 1933) allowed Hitler to rule by decree, ending Weimar democracy within weeks.

#### L6.IF.2 Italian Fascism under Mussolini: ideology, consolidation of power and historiographical debate
**Italian Fascism under Mussolini.** Post-war Italy faced its own crisis of confidence ('mutilated victory' resentment over unfulfilled territorial promises), economic disruption and fear of socialist revolution; Benito Mussolini's Fascist movement, combining ultranationalism, anti-communism and paramilitary violence (the Blackshirts), exploited this. The March on Rome (October 1922), a show of force rather than a real coup, led King Victor Emmanuel III to appoint Mussolini Prime Minister rather than risk civil conflict. Mussolini then dismantled democratic constraint step by step: the Acerbo Law (1923) guaranteed a two-thirds majority to any party winning at least 25% of the vote, the murder of the socialist deputy Giacomo Matteotti (1924, after he denounced Fascist electoral violence) provoked the opposition's Aventine Secession (a boycott of parliament that, rather than stopping Mussolini, let him consolidate power unopposed), and by 1925-26 Mussolini had established an outright dictatorship. Historians have long debated Fascism's nature: whether it constituted 'totalitarianism' comparable to Nazism or Stalinism (a category associated with Hannah Arendt), or a distinct, more improvised and less totally controlling regime; Renzo De Felice's influential, controversial 'consensus thesis' (from the 1960s-70s) argued Fascism enjoyed genuine broad popular consent for much of the 1930s, challenging simpler accounts of the regime as pure coercion.

#### L6.IF.3 Stalinism in the 1930s: collectivisation, the Five-Year Plans and the Great Terror
**Stalinism in the 1930s.** Following Lenin's death (1924) and Stalin's defeat of rivals (notably Trotsky, exiled 1929, later assassinated 1940), Stalin launched a programme of rapid, state-directed transformation. The First Five-Year Plan (1928-32) forced rapid industrialisation through central planning, with brutal human cost but real industrial growth. Collectivisation of agriculture, forcing peasants into state/collective farms and crushing resistance from wealthier peasants ('kulaks', a category often applied arbitrarily), caused catastrophic famine (1932-33), with particularly severe, and historically and politically contested, mortality in Ukraine (often termed the Holodomor, debated among historians as to whether it constituted a deliberate, targeted policy against Ukrainians specifically or a broader, still catastrophic, consequence of collectivisation policy across Soviet grain regions). The Great Terror (1936-38) saw mass arrest, imprisonment (the Gulag labour-camp system) and execution, including show trials of former senior Bolsheviks (Zinoviev, Kamenev, later Bukharin) forced to confess to fabricated conspiracies, alongside a much wider, less publicised purge reaching deep into the party, military and ordinary population - historians estimate the Terror's victims in the hundreds of thousands executed and millions imprisoned, though exact figures remain debated given archival access issues even after the Soviet collapse.
