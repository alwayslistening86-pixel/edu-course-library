## Test items
1. Explain why the 1832 Reform Act is sometimes described as both a significant and a limited reform. [6 marks]
2. 'Chartism failed.' Assess the validity of this view. [30 marks]

## Answer key (for the tutor only)
1. [6] B1 significant: abolished many rotten boroughs and redistributed seats to growing industrial towns; B1 significant: extended the vote to much of the male middle class (roughly one in five adult men); B1 limited: the working class remained almost entirely excluded; B1 limited: no women could vote; B1 this exclusion directly fed working-class grievance, feeding into Chartism from 1838; B1 credit reference to later Reform Acts (1867, 1884) as evidence reform was a gradual, incomplete process, not settled in 1832.
2. [30] Mark out of 30 using this course's undergraduate-history generic levels, adapting the alevel_history-style levels convention to honours standard and to the QAA benchmark's description of graduate historical skill: Level 1 (1-6) largely descriptive/narrative, little independent analysis, no real engagement with historiography; Level 2 (7-12) some analysis and awareness that historians disagree, but underdeveloped, uneven use of evidence; Level 3 (13-18) a structured argument that explains a range of relevant factors, engages with named historians or schools of interpretation, and reaches a judgement, though depth or balance may be uneven; Level 4 (19-24) a well-organised, evidenced argument that weighs competing historiographical positions explicitly and reaches a supported judgement, showing real synthesis of complex material; Level 5 (25-30) a sophisticated, wide-ranging argument that formulates its own sharp historical question, synthesises extensive and sometimes conflicting evidence, engages critically and precisely with historiography, and sustains a fully substantiated, original judgement throughout. Credit: the case for failure - none of the six points of the Charter was achieved at the time, all three mass petitions (1839, 1842, 1848) were rejected, the movement had collapsed as an organised force by the late 1840s; the case against - five of the six points were eventually achieved by later reform, Chartism built lasting traditions of working-class political organisation, and its scale (over a million signatures) demonstrated working-class political consciousness E.P. Thompson argued was being 'made' in this period; a strong answer weighs immediate political failure against longer-term historical significance rather than treating them as the same question.
