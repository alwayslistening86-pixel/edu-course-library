# S07_Industrialising_Britain - Lesson: The Industrial Revolution; Chartism; the Reform Acts

## Goal
The learner explains the origins and effects of Britain's Industrial Revolution, the Chartist movement, and the sequence and significance of the nineteenth-century Reform Acts.

## Syllabus items taught here
- L5.IB.1 - The origins and effects of Britain's Industrial Revolution, c.1780-1850
- L5.IB.2 - Chartism and working-class political movements in industrialising Britain
- L5.IB.3 - The Reform Acts of 1832, 1867 and 1884-85 and the gradual extension of the franchise

## How to teach this
Ask: Britain industrialised first in the world - so who got to vote in the country that led the industrial age, and who didn't? This course builds directly on GCSE History and A-level History (Edexcel 9HI0, Tudor England/German Reformation/Tudor rebellion), which the learner has already completed: do not re-teach Tudor political or religious history, and use the learner's existing source-criticism and essay-planning skills as a floor, not a ceiling. Degree-level history goes further in the ways the QAA benchmark statement specifies: engaging critically with historiography (not just facts, but how and why historians have disagreed), constructing the learner's own historical questions rather than only answering set ones, synthesising large and sometimes conflicting bodies of evidence, and sustaining a longer, more theoretically aware argument. Push the learner to name historians and schools of interpretation, not just events, and to interrogate sources for genre, purpose and silence, not only content. Historical facts (events, dates, figures) do not change with time; historiographical debates are living scholarship and should be taught as ongoing argument, not settled fact.

#### L5.IB.1 The origins and effects of Britain's Industrial Revolution, c.1780-1850
**The Industrial Revolution, c.1780-1850.** Britain was the first country to industrialise, driven by a combination of factors historians still debate the relative weight of: accessible coal and iron deposits; capital accumulated partly from trade (including, controversially, profits connected to the slave-based Atlantic economy studied in S06); a relatively flexible social structure and legal/financial institutions (secure property rights, a developing banking system); agricultural improvement freeing up labour; and technological innovation, notably James Watt's improved steam engine (patented 1769, commercialised through the 1770s-80s), which powered factories independent of water-power locations and, later, railways. The factory system concentrated textile production (initially cotton, driven partly by cheap raw cotton reliant on slave-grown supply from the American South) in large mills, drawing workers, including many women and children, into new, often harsh urban-industrial conditions. E.P. Thompson's *The Making of the English Working Class* (1963) argued this period saw the making of a self-conscious working class through shared experience and organisation, not merely a passive economic side-effect - a highly influential, though also contested, historiographical claim.

#### L5.IB.2 Chartism and working-class political movements in industrialising Britain
**Chartism, 1838-48.** As industrialisation reshaped British society without extending political rights to most working people, Chartism became Britain's first mass working-class political movement, organised around the People's Charter (1838) and its six demands: universal manhood suffrage, the secret ballot, no property qualification for MPs, payment of MPs, equal electoral districts, and annual parliaments. Mass petitions to Parliament, bearing over a million signatures each, were presented in 1839, 1842 and 1848, and were rejected each time; the movement combined 'moral force' Chartists (favouring petition and persuasion) and 'physical force' Chartists (willing to countenance violence, as in the Newport Rising, 1839). Chartism's immediate political failure (none of its six points was won at the time) should be weighed against its longer significance: it built durable traditions of working-class political organisation, and five of its six demands (all but annual parliaments) were eventually achieved by later reforms, making it an important precursor rather than simply a failure.

#### L5.IB.3 The Reform Acts of 1832, 1867 and 1884-85 and the gradual extension of the franchise
**The Reform Acts and the extension of the franchise.** The Great Reform Act (1832) was the first major nineteenth-century step: it abolished many 'rotten boroughs' (seats with tiny or no real electorates, often controlled by a single patron) and redistributed seats to growing industrial towns, and extended the vote to the male middle class (roughly one in five adult men could now vote) - a significant but partial reform that left the working class and all women excluded, one of the grievances that fed directly into Chartism. The Second Reform Act (1867), passed under a Conservative government (Disraeli), extended the vote to many urban working-class men. The Third Reform Act (1884) extended a similar franchise to the counties (rural working men), and the accompanying Redistribution Act (1885) further rationalised constituencies. By 1884, roughly two in three adult men could vote - still no women, and still excluded the poorest men - showing reform as a gradual, contested, incomplete process rather than a single decisive democratic breakthrough.
