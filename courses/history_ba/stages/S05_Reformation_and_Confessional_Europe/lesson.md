# S05_Reformation_and_Confessional_Europe - Lesson: The Catholic Reformation; the Thirty Years' War; print culture and the witch trials

## Goal
The learner explains the Catholic (Counter-)Reformation's institutional and spiritual response to Protestantism, the causes and consequences of the Thirty Years' War, and the role of print culture in the early modern European witch trials.

## Syllabus items taught here
- L5.RE.1 - The Catholic (Counter-)Reformation: the Council of Trent (1545-63), the Jesuits, and Teresa of Avila
- L5.RE.2 - Confessional conflict and the Thirty Years' War, 1618-48, and the Peace of Westphalia
- L5.RE.3 - Print culture and the witch trials of early modern Europe: the Malleus Maleficarum and the gendering of persecution

## How to teach this
Ask: your A-level covered Luther's Reformation in Germany - so what was the Catholic Church doing about it everywhere else in Europe? This course builds directly on GCSE History and A-level History (Edexcel 9HI0, Tudor England/German Reformation/Tudor rebellion), which the learner has already completed: do not re-teach Tudor political or religious history, and use the learner's existing source-criticism and essay-planning skills as a floor, not a ceiling. Degree-level history goes further in the ways the QAA benchmark statement specifies: engaging critically with historiography (not just facts, but how and why historians have disagreed), constructing the learner's own historical questions rather than only answering set ones, synthesising large and sometimes conflicting bodies of evidence, and sustaining a longer, more theoretically aware argument. Push the learner to name historians and schools of interpretation, not just events, and to interrogate sources for genre, purpose and silence, not only content. Historical facts (events, dates, figures) do not change with time; historiographical debates are living scholarship and should be taught as ongoing argument, not settled fact.

#### L5.RE.1 The Catholic (Counter-)Reformation: the Council of Trent (1545-63), the Jesuits, and Teresa of Avila
**The Catholic (Counter-)Reformation.** Facing the Protestant challenge, the Catholic Church responded with the Council of Trent (meeting in sessions 1545-63), which reaffirmed core Catholic doctrine Protestants rejected (transubstantiation, the necessity of good works alongside faith, the authority of Church tradition alongside scripture), reformed clerical discipline (requiring bishops to reside in their dioceses, establishing seminaries for better-trained priests), and standardised liturgy and doctrine church-wide. The Society of Jesus (the Jesuits), founded by Ignatius of Loyola in 1540, became the Counter-Reformation's most effective agents: rigorously trained, they combined missionary work (in Asia and the Americas as well as Protestant Europe), influential education (Jesuit colleges spread across Catholic Europe), and doctrinal defence. Spiritually, figures such as Teresa of Avila (a Spanish Carmelite nun and mystic, author of *The Interior Castle*, who also reformed her order towards stricter observance) exemplified a renewed, intensely personal Catholic spirituality alongside institutional reform - the Counter-Reformation was as much a genuine spiritual revival as a defensive reaction.

#### L5.RE.2 Confessional conflict and the Thirty Years' War, 1618-48, and the Peace of Westphalia
**Confessional conflict and the Thirty Years' War, 1618-48.** Religious division combined with dynastic and political rivalry (chiefly Habsburg power versus its rivals) to produce Europe's most destructive early modern conflict. It began with the Defenestration of Prague (1618), when Bohemian Protestants threw two Catholic Habsburg officials from a castle window, triggering a revolt against Habsburg (Catholic) rule; the war escalated through successive phases drawing in Denmark, Sweden (under Gustavus Adolphus, a major Protestant military leader) and eventually Catholic France, which intervened against its Habsburg rivals despite France's own Catholicism - showing that by the war's later phases, dynastic/state interest could override confessional solidarity. The war caused catastrophic mortality and destruction across Central Europe, especially the German lands. It ended with the Peace of Westphalia (1648), which extended the earlier principle of *cuius regio, eius religio* (the ruler's religion determines the state's religion, from the 1555 Peace of Augsburg) to include Calvinism, and is widely regarded by historians as a foundational moment for the modern principle of state sovereignty and non-interference.

#### L5.RE.3 Print culture and the witch trials of early modern Europe: the Malleus Maleficarum and the gendering of persecution
**Print culture and the witch trials.** The printing press, which had spread Reformation ideas rapidly (as the A-level's study of Luther showed), also spread panic: the *Malleus Maleficarum* ('Hammer of Witches', 1487, by the German inquisitor Heinrich Kramer) was among the first books to circulate widely in print, codifying beliefs about witchcraft and arguing for its vigorous prosecution, and went through many editions. The intense phase of European witch persecution ran mainly from the later sixteenth to the mid-seventeenth century - overlapping with, not preceding, the Reformation and confessional conflict - with historians estimating roughly 40,000-60,000 executions across Europe, the great majority of the accused (estimates commonly cited around 75-80%) being women. Historians debate why: some (e.g. earlier feminist historiography) emphasise persecution as a mechanism of patriarchal control over women who defied social norms (the elderly, the unmarried, healers/midwives); others (e.g. Diane Purkiss and more recent social historians) stress additional factors - local community tension, religious anxiety heightened by confessional conflict, and legal/judicial change (the spread of inquisitorial procedure and torture) - cautioning against any single-cause explanation.
