# S12_Cold_War_Europe_and_1989 - Lesson: The origins of the Cold War; Hungary 1956 and Prague 1968; the collapse of Communism, 1989

## Goal
The learner explains the origins of the Cold War and the division of Europe, the 1956 Hungarian Uprising and the 1968 Prague Spring as crises within the Soviet bloc, and the causes and course of Communism's collapse in 1989 and German reunification.

## Syllabus items taught here
- L6.CW.1 - The origins of the Cold War and the division of Europe, 1945-49
- L6.CW.2 - Crises within the Soviet bloc: Hungary 1956 and the Prague Spring 1968
- L6.CW.3 - The collapse of Communism, 1989, and German reunification: synoptic essay across the specialist period

## How to teach this
Ask: two wartime allies, the USA and the USSR - so what turned them into the two poles of a divided continent within just a few years of 1945? This course builds directly on GCSE History and A-level History (Edexcel 9HI0, Tudor England/German Reformation/Tudor rebellion), which the learner has already completed: do not re-teach Tudor political or religious history, and use the learner's existing source-criticism and essay-planning skills as a floor, not a ceiling. Degree-level history goes further in the ways the QAA benchmark statement specifies: engaging critically with historiography (not just facts, but how and why historians have disagreed), constructing the learner's own historical questions rather than only answering set ones, synthesising large and sometimes conflicting bodies of evidence, and sustaining a longer, more theoretically aware argument. Push the learner to name historians and schools of interpretation, not just events, and to interrogate sources for genre, purpose and silence, not only content. Historical facts (events, dates, figures) do not change with time; historiographical debates are living scholarship and should be taught as ongoing argument, not settled fact.

#### L6.CW.1 The origins of the Cold War and the division of Europe, 1945-49
**The origins of the Cold War, 1945-49.** Wartime Allied cooperation (formalised at conferences including Yalta and Potsdam, 1945) broke down quickly as ideological rivalry (capitalist democracy versus Soviet communism), mutual suspicion, and disagreement over the fate of liberated/occupied Europe hardened into confrontation. Winston Churchill's 'Iron Curtain' speech (Fulton, Missouri, March 1946) publicly named the division of Europe into Soviet-dominated Eastern states and a Western sphere. The Truman Doctrine (1947) committed the USA to containing communist expansion; the Marshall Plan (1947-48) provided large-scale US economic aid to rebuild Western Europe, partly to prevent economic desperation from aiding communist movements, and the USSR barred Eastern-bloc states from participating. The Berlin Blockade (1948-49), the Soviet attempt to force the Western Allies out of West Berlin by cutting land access, was defeated by the Berlin Airlift, a sustained Western air-supply operation, and hardened the division of Germany into the Federal Republic (West) and the German Democratic Republic (East), both founded 1949. The Western military alliance NATO (1949) and the later Soviet-bloc Warsaw Pact (1955) institutionalised the continent's division into two armed camps for the following four decades.

#### L6.CW.2 Crises within the Soviet bloc: Hungary 1956 and the Prague Spring 1968
**Crises within the Soviet bloc: Hungary 1956 and Prague 1968.** Soviet control over Eastern Europe faced periodic internal challenge. The Hungarian Uprising (October-November 1956) began as student and worker protest, escalated into a genuine national uprising against Soviet-backed rule, briefly installed a reformist government under Imre Nagy (who announced Hungary's withdrawal from the Warsaw Pact) - a step too far for Moscow, which crushed the uprising with Soviet tanks, killing thousands and prompting mass emigration; Nagy was later executed. The Prague Spring (1968) saw Czechoslovak leader Alexander Dubček pursue a programme of liberalising reform ('socialism with a human face': greater freedom of speech, media and movement, while remaining nominally within the Communist system) that similarly alarmed Moscow; the Warsaw Pact invasion of Czechoslovakia (August 1968) crushed the reforms. The Soviet response was formalised as the Brezhnev Doctrine, which explicitly asserted the USSR's right to intervene in any Warsaw Pact state where socialism was, in Soviet judgement, under threat - a doctrine that defined the limits of Eastern-bloc autonomy until its effective abandonment under Gorbachev in the late 1980s (directly enabling 1989, see L6.CW.3).

#### L6.CW.3 The collapse of Communism, 1989, and German reunification: synoptic essay across the specialist period
**The collapse of Communism, 1989, and German reunification: synoptic essay across the specialist period.** By the late 1980s, Mikhail Gorbachev's reforms in the USSR itself (glasnost, openness, and perestroika, economic restructuring) and his renunciation of the Brezhnev Doctrine (making clear the USSR would not use force to prop up Eastern-bloc regimes) removed the coercive backing that had underpinned Soviet control since 1945-49. Reform and mass protest spread rapidly across the bloc in 1989: Poland's Solidarity movement negotiated a transition to democracy; Hungary opened its border with Austria, letting East Germans escape westward; mass protests in East Germany culminated in the fall of the Berlin Wall (9 November 1989), an event now treated as a symbolic hinge of the entire twentieth century; Czechoslovakia's largely peaceful 'Velvet Revolution' removed its Communist government within weeks; Romania's transition was violent, ending in the execution of the dictator Nicolae Ceaușescu (December 1989). Germany was formally reunified on 3 October 1990, and the USSR itself dissolved in 1991. This synoptic stage asks the learner to draw together the whole specialist period (1914-89): from the First World War's destruction of the old European order, through revolution and dictatorship, to total war and genocide, to a Cold War division whose sudden, largely peaceful collapse in 1989 remains one of the most contested turning points for historians to explain.
