# S01_Historical_Method_and_Historiography - Lesson: What is history; degree-level source criticism; historiographical schools

## Goal
The learner explains the central debate over historical objectivity (Ranke, Carr, the postmodern challenge), applies degree-level source criticism (genre, provenance, purpose, silence), and identifies the major historiographical schools and what each brings to reading the past.

## Syllabus items taught here
- L4.HM.1 - What is history? Ranke, E.H. Carr and the postmodern challenge to historical objectivity
- L4.HM.2 - Degree-level source criticism: genre, provenance, purpose, silence and the limits of the archive
- L4.HM.3 - Historiographical schools: Whig history, Marxist history, the Annales school/social history, the cultural and gender turns

## How to teach this
Ask: if two honest historians read the same sources and reach opposite conclusions, is one of them simply wrong? This course builds directly on GCSE History and A-level History (Edexcel 9HI0, Tudor England/German Reformation/Tudor rebellion), which the learner has already completed: do not re-teach Tudor political or religious history, and use the learner's existing source-criticism and essay-planning skills as a floor, not a ceiling. Degree-level history goes further in the ways the QAA benchmark statement specifies: engaging critically with historiography (not just facts, but how and why historians have disagreed), constructing the learner's own historical questions rather than only answering set ones, synthesising large and sometimes conflicting bodies of evidence, and sustaining a longer, more theoretically aware argument. Push the learner to name historians and schools of interpretation, not just events, and to interrogate sources for genre, purpose and silence, not only content. Historical facts (events, dates, figures) do not change with time; historiographical debates are living scholarship and should be taught as ongoing argument, not settled fact.

#### L4.HM.1 What is history? Ranke, E.H. Carr and the postmodern challenge to historical objectivity
**What is history?** Leopold von Ranke (19th century) argued history should show the past 'wie es eigentlich gewesen' (as it essentially/actually was) - a foundational claim that rigorous archival research could recover objective historical truth, underpinning the professionalisation of history as a discipline. E.H. Carr's *What Is History?* (1961) challenged naive objectivism: for Carr, facts do not speak for themselves - historians select and interpret them, so 'study the historian before you study the facts', and history is 'an unending dialogue between the past and the present'. Postmodern critics (notably Hayden White, *Metahistory*, 1973) went further, arguing historical narratives are shaped by literary/rhetorical 'emplotment' (romance, tragedy, comedy, satire) as much as by evidence, raising the question of whether historical writing can ever be simply a neutral report of 'what happened'. Degree-level history sits between these poles: it takes evidence and archival rigour seriously (against pure relativism) while accepting that selection, interpretation and narrative form are unavoidable (against naive Rankean objectivism).

#### L4.HM.2 Degree-level source criticism: genre, provenance, purpose, silence and the limits of the archive
**Degree-level source criticism.** GCSE and A-level source work already asks for provenance, content and inference; a history degree pushes further into: *genre* (is this a private letter, a state proclamation, a memoir written decades later, a statistical return? - each has different conventions and reliability); *purpose* (what was the source trying to do - persuade, record, justify, entertain? - not just who wrote it); *perspective* (whose voice is this, and whose is it not - a source can be honest and still one-sided); and *silence* (what does the archive not contain, and why - the loss or suppression of evidence, especially from marginalised groups, e.g. enslaved people, the illiterate poor, women, shapes what history can be written at all, a problem historians call the 'archival silence' or 'archive of exclusion'). A single source is never sufficient: corroboration across independent sources, and explicit awareness of a source's limits, are what separate degree-level use of evidence from simple summary.

#### L4.HM.3 Historiographical schools: Whig history, Marxist history, the Annales school/social history, the cultural and gender turns
**Historiographical schools.** *Whig history* (a term coined critically by Herbert Butterfield, *The Whig Interpretation of History*, 1931): narrates the past as inevitable progress towards present liberty/enlightenment - now widely criticised as teleological, but still an influence to recognise and resist. *Marxist history*: reads change through class conflict and economic/social structure (e.g. E.P. Thompson's *The Making of the English Working Class*, 1963, recovering working-class agency; Georges Lefebvre and Albert Soboul on the French Revolution as bourgeois revolution). *The Annales school* (founded by Marc Bloch and Lucien Febvre in France, 1929): shifted focus from political events to long-term social, economic and geographical structures ('la longue durée', a phrase from Fernand Braudel) and the history of ordinary life ('histoire totale'). *The cultural turn* (from the 1970s-80s): focuses on meaning, language, representation and mentalities rather than only material structures. *Gender history* (building on and going beyond 'women's history'): examines how gender as a category of analysis shaped power, roles and experience across all periods, not as an add-on topic. A single historical event is often read very differently by each school - which is itself the point of studying historiography.
