## Test items
1. Explain the difference between the Annales school and Marxist history as approaches to the past. [6 marks]
2. 'Objective historical truth is impossible; all history is interpretation.' Assess the validity of this view, drawing on the historians and debates you have studied. [30 marks]

## Answer key (for the tutor only)
1. [6] B1 Marxist history centres class conflict and economic/social structure as the driver of change; B1 Annales history centres long-term social, economic and geographical structures and the texture of everyday life ('la longue durée', 'histoire totale') more than political events or class struggle specifically; B1 both reject a purely political, 'great men' narrative of history; B1 Annales was founded by Bloch and Febvre (1929), associated later with Braudel; B1 Marxist history is associated with historians such as Thompson, Lefebvre and Soboul; B1 both differ from Whig history's assumption of inevitable progress.
2. [30] Mark out of 30 using this course's undergraduate-history generic levels, adapting the alevel_history-style levels convention to honours standard and to the QAA benchmark's description of graduate historical skill: Level 1 (1-6) largely descriptive/narrative, little independent analysis, no real engagement with historiography; Level 2 (7-12) some analysis and awareness that historians disagree, but underdeveloped, uneven use of evidence; Level 3 (13-18) a structured argument that explains a range of relevant factors, engages with named historians or schools of interpretation, and reaches a judgement, though depth or balance may be uneven; Level 4 (19-24) a well-organised, evidenced argument that weighs competing historiographical positions explicitly and reaches a supported judgement, showing real synthesis of complex material; Level 5 (25-30) a sophisticated, wide-ranging argument that formulates its own sharp historical question, synthesises extensive and sometimes conflicting evidence, engages critically and precisely with historiography, and sustains a fully substantiated, original judgement throughout. Credit: the case for interpretation - Carr's argument that historians select facts, White's argument that narrative form shapes meaning, the existence of rival, evidence-based but incompatible historiographical schools (Marxist vs Whig vs Annales); the case against pure relativism - the discipline's shared standards of evidence, corroboration and archival rigour (Ranke's legacy), the fact that some claims can be shown false by evidence regardless of interpretive framework; a strong answer avoids simply asserting one pole and instead shows where the discipline actually sits between them.
