## Test items
1. Explain why the execution of Charles I in 1649 was constitutionally unprecedented. [6 marks]
2. 'The Glorious Revolution of 1688-89 achieved what the Civil War of the 1640s had failed to achieve.' Assess the validity of this view. [30 marks]

## Answer key (for the tutor only)
1. [6] B1 no existing law allowed a king to be tried by his own subjects; B1 the trial and Rump Parliament that authorised it had itself been produced by Pride's Purge, an act of military coercion, not ordinary legitimate process; B1 kingship was believed (by Charles and most contemporaries) to derive from God, not from the consent of subjects who could then remove it; B1 the Commonwealth that followed had to construct a wholly new basis of legitimacy (parliamentary/republican sovereignty) rather than relying on precedent; B1 many contemporaries, including some who had opposed Charles, were horrified, showing the act went beyond what most of the political nation wanted; B1 no European kingdom had yet publicly tried and executed its own monarch by due legal process, making England's act unprecedented even by wider European standards.
2. [30] Mark out of 30 using this course's undergraduate-history generic levels, adapting the alevel_history-style levels convention to honours standard and to the QAA benchmark's description of graduate historical skill: Level 1 (1-6) largely descriptive/narrative, little independent analysis, no real engagement with historiography; Level 2 (7-12) some analysis and awareness that historians disagree, but underdeveloped, uneven use of evidence; Level 3 (13-18) a structured argument that explains a range of relevant factors, engages with named historians or schools of interpretation, and reaches a judgement, though depth or balance may be uneven; Level 4 (19-24) a well-organised, evidenced argument that weighs competing historiographical positions explicitly and reaches a supported judgement, showing real synthesis of complex material; Level 5 (25-30) a sophisticated, wide-ranging argument that formulates its own sharp historical question, synthesises extensive and sometimes conflicting evidence, engages critically and precisely with historiography, and sustains a fully substantiated, original judgement throughout. Credit: 1688-89 achieved a durable, legally entrenched constitutional monarchy (Bill of Rights) without the trauma of civil war/regicide, and did so with elite/parliamentary consensus rather than military coercion; against this - the 1640s war had already established, by force, that a king could be defied and even executed, a precedent 1688-89 relied on even while avoiding repeating it, and the Civil War's ideological ferment (radical groups such as the Levellers) raised questions about popular sovereignty that 1688-89 did not resolve; a strong answer weighs continuity between the two crises against 1688-89's distinctive, lasting constitutional settlement.
