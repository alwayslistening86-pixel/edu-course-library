# S02_The_English_Revolution - Lesson: Causes of the English Civil War; regicide and republic; Restoration and the Glorious Revolution

## Goal
The learner explains the constitutional, religious and financial causes of the English Civil War (1625-42), the trial and execution of Charles I and the Cromwellian republic, and the Restoration and Glorious Revolution's constitutional settlement.

## Syllabus items taught here
- L4.ER.1 - Causes of the English Civil War: constitutional, religious and financial conflict between Charles I and Parliament, 1625-42
- L4.ER.2 - Regicide and republic: the trial and execution of Charles I (1649) and the Cromwellian Commonwealth/Protectorate
- L4.ER.3 - Restoration (1660) and the Glorious Revolution (1688-89): the Bill of Rights and constitutional monarchy

## How to teach this
Ask: how does a king end up on trial for treason against his own kingdom? This course builds directly on GCSE History and A-level History (Edexcel 9HI0, Tudor England/German Reformation/Tudor rebellion), which the learner has already completed: do not re-teach Tudor political or religious history, and use the learner's existing source-criticism and essay-planning skills as a floor, not a ceiling. Degree-level history goes further in the ways the QAA benchmark statement specifies: engaging critically with historiography (not just facts, but how and why historians have disagreed), constructing the learner's own historical questions rather than only answering set ones, synthesising large and sometimes conflicting bodies of evidence, and sustaining a longer, more theoretically aware argument. Push the learner to name historians and schools of interpretation, not just events, and to interrogate sources for genre, purpose and silence, not only content. Historical facts (events, dates, figures) do not change with time; historiographical debates are living scholarship and should be taught as ongoing argument, not settled fact.

#### L4.ER.1 Causes of the English Civil War: constitutional, religious and financial conflict between Charles I and Parliament, 1625-42
**Causes of the English Civil War, 1625-42.** Charles I's belief in the divine right of kings clashed repeatedly with Parliament over money and religion. After dissolving Parliament in 1629, Charles ruled without it for eleven years ('Personal Rule', 1629-40), raising revenue through disputed prerogative means, notably Ship Money (a levy historically raised only from coastal counties in wartime, extended nationwide in peacetime - challenged in court by John Hampden, 1637). Religiously, Archbishop William Laud's reforms (more ceremony, 'beauty of holiness', seen by critics as crypto-Catholic) alienated Puritans; imposing a new Prayer Book on Scotland (1637) triggered the Bishops' Wars (1639-40), forcing Charles to recall Parliament for funds. The Long Parliament (from November 1640) pressed reforms Charles resisted; the Grand Remonstrance (1641), a long list of grievances, passed narrowly, showing Parliament itself was divided; Charles's attempt to arrest five MPs in the Commons (January 1642) fatally destroyed trust, and war began in August 1642. Historians debate the balance of constitutional, religious and financial causes, and whether the war was 'inevitable' by any particular point (a long-running 'Whig' narrative of inevitable constitutional progress versus revisionist historians such as Conrad Russell, who stressed short-term contingency and mismanagement).

#### L4.ER.2 Regicide and republic: the trial and execution of Charles I (1649) and the Cromwellian Commonwealth/Protectorate
**Regicide and republic.** After Royalist defeat (Naseby, 1645) and a failed Second Civil War (1648), the Army, radicalised and distrustful of continued negotiation, purged Parliament ('Pride's Purge', December 1648) leaving a 'Rump Parliament' that put Charles I on trial for treason against the kingdom - a constitutionally unprecedented act, since a king could not, in existing law, be tried by his own subjects. Charles was executed on 30 January 1649. England was declared a Commonwealth (republic) under the Rump Parliament, though real power increasingly lay with the Army and Oliver Cromwell, who dismissed the Rump (1653) and ruled as Lord Protector under the Instrument of Government (1653) and later the Humble Petition and Advice (1657). Cromwell's Protectorate combined religious toleration for Protestants (though not Catholics or, initially, Anglicans) with periods of quasi-military rule (the Major-Generals, 1655-57). His death (1658) and his son Richard Cromwell's weak, brief succession left the republic without a stable settlement.

#### L4.ER.3 Restoration (1660) and the Glorious Revolution (1688-89): the Bill of Rights and constitutional monarchy
**Restoration and the Glorious Revolution.** Facing political collapse, General Monck helped engineer the recall of Charles II (Restoration, 1660), restoring monarchy, Lords and the Anglican Church, though on terms that assumed some constitutional lessons had been learned (Charles II ruled with, not without, Parliament). Tension over royal power and religion resurfaced under James II (r.1685-88), a Catholic convert whose pro-Catholic appointments and a male Catholic heir (born 1688) alarmed the political nation. Protestant nobles invited William of Orange (married to James's Protestant daughter Mary) to invade; James fled, and Parliament declared the throne vacant (the 'Glorious Revolution', 1688-89, notable for its near-bloodlessness in England, though not in Ireland or Scotland). William III and Mary II accepted the crown conditional on the Bill of Rights (1689), which barred Catholics from the throne, required regular Parliaments, and prohibited suspending laws or raising taxes/a standing army without Parliament's consent - establishing, in law, the constitutional monarchy that the 1640s war had failed to settle by force.
