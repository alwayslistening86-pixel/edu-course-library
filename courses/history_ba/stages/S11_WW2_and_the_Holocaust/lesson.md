# S11_WW2_and_the_Holocaust - Lesson: The Second World War in Europe; the Holocaust and its historiography; total war and civilian experience

## Goal
The learner explains the causes and course of the Second World War in Europe, the intentionalist/functionalist historiographical debate over the Holocaust, and the nature of total war and civilian/resistance experience.

## Syllabus items taught here
- L6.WH.1 - The causes and course of the Second World War in Europe, 1939-45
- L6.WH.2 - The Holocaust: intentionalist versus functionalist historiography (Browning, Goldhagen, Kershaw)
- L6.WH.3 - Total war and civilian experience: the Blitz, occupation and resistance in Europe

## How to teach this
Ask: was the Holocaust the execution of a plan Hitler had held since the 1920s, or something that escalated step by step once war made ever-more-extreme 'solutions' seem possible? This course builds directly on GCSE History and A-level History (Edexcel 9HI0, Tudor England/German Reformation/Tudor rebellion), which the learner has already completed: do not re-teach Tudor political or religious history, and use the learner's existing source-criticism and essay-planning skills as a floor, not a ceiling. Degree-level history goes further in the ways the QAA benchmark statement specifies: engaging critically with historiography (not just facts, but how and why historians have disagreed), constructing the learner's own historical questions rather than only answering set ones, synthesising large and sometimes conflicting bodies of evidence, and sustaining a longer, more theoretically aware argument. Push the learner to name historians and schools of interpretation, not just events, and to interrogate sources for genre, purpose and silence, not only content. Historical facts (events, dates, figures) do not change with time; historiographical debates are living scholarship and should be taught as ongoing argument, not settled fact.

#### L6.WH.1 The causes and course of the Second World War in Europe, 1939-45
**The Second World War in Europe, 1939-45.** Hitler's expansionist aims (remilitarising the Rhineland 1936, annexing Austria 1938, the Munich Agreement's appeasement of German claims on Czechoslovak Sudetenland 1938, then occupying the rest of Czechoslovakia 1939) culminated in the invasion of Poland (1 September 1939), triggering British and French declarations of war, made possible by the cynical Nazi-Soviet Pact (August 1939) that secretly divided Eastern Europe between Germany and the USSR. Germany's 'Blitzkrieg' tactics achieved rapid early victories, including the fall of France (June 1940); Britain fought on alone after the Battle of Britain (summer 1940) prevented a German invasion. Operation Barbarossa (June 1941), Hitler's invasion of the USSR, opened the war's largest and most costly front, breaking the Nazi-Soviet Pact and eventually turning decisively against Germany at Stalingrad (1942-43), a battle widely seen as the war's Eastern turning point. The Western Allies opened a second major front with the Normandy landings (D-Day, 6 June 1944); the war in Europe ended with Germany's surrender (VE Day, 8 May 1945), following Hitler's suicide amid the fall of Berlin.

#### L6.WH.2 The Holocaust: intentionalist versus functionalist historiography (Browning, Goldhagen, Kershaw)
**The Holocaust: intentionalist versus functionalist historiography.** The Nazi regime's systematic murder of approximately six million European Jews, alongside Roma, disabled people, political prisoners and others deemed enemies, escalated from discrimination (the Nuremberg Laws, 1935) and violence (Kristallnacht, 1938) toward genocide once war began, culminating in the industrialised killing of the extermination camps (Auschwitz-Birkenau chief among them) and coordinated at the administrative level by the Wannsee Conference (January 1942). Historians have long debated its causation: 'intentionalist' historians (e.g. Lucy Dawidowicz) argue Hitler held a genocidal intention toward Jews from early in his career, with the war providing opportunity rather than cause; 'functionalist' historians (e.g. Hans Mommsen, Martin Broszat) argue the 'Final Solution' emerged cumulatively from wartime radicalisation, bureaucratic improvisation and competition among Nazi agencies, rather than from a single long-held master plan. Ian Kershaw's influential synthesis, 'working towards the Führer', suggests officials radicalised policy on their own initiative, anticipating what they believed Hitler wanted, bridging the two positions. Christopher Browning's *Ordinary Men* (1992), a study of Reserve Police Battalion 101, examined how situational and social pressures led ordinary, non-fanatical men to participate in mass killing; Daniel Goldhagen's *Hitler's Willing Executioners* (1996) controversially argued a uniquely virulent German 'eliminationist antisemitism' explained perpetrators' willingness, a thesis most historians have since heavily criticised as overstated and insufficiently comparative.

#### L6.WH.3 Total war and civilian experience: the Blitz, occupation and resistance in Europe
**Total war and civilian/resistance experience.** The Second World War in Europe was a genuinely 'total war', mobilising entire societies and deliberately targeting civilian populations and morale. The Blitz (the German bombing campaign against British cities, chiefly 1940-41) killed over 40,000 British civilians and tested (with mixed but broadly resilient results) civilian morale under sustained aerial attack. Occupied Europe experienced widely varying regimes of control and collaboration: France's Vichy regime (1940-44) collaborated with Germany while nominally governing the unoccupied south, provoking a spectrum of responses from collaboration to resistance; organised resistance movements (the French Maquis and others across occupied Europe) conducted sabotage, intelligence-gathering and support for Allied operations at severe personal risk, though historians caution against romanticising resistance's scale, since active resisters were a minority and collaboration, accommodation and simple survival were more common civilian experiences. This period's blend of ideological extremity, industrialised violence and civilian/resistance experience sets up the Cold War's division of the very Europe this destruction had reshaped (S12).
