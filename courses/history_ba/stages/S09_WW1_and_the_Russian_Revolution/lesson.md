# S09_WW1_and_the_Russian_Revolution - Lesson: The First World War; the Russian Revolutions of 1917 in depth; the Paris Peace Settlement

## Goal
The learner explains the causes and course of the First World War, the Russian Revolutions of 1917 in depth (building on S04's comparative treatment), and the Paris Peace Settlement's terms and consequences.

## Syllabus items taught here
- L6.WW.1 - The causes and course of the First World War, 1914-18
- L6.WW.2 - The Russian Revolutions of 1917 in depth and the Civil War, 1917-21
- L6.WW.3 - The Paris Peace Settlement (1919) and its consequences for interwar Europe

## How to teach this
Ask: one assassination in Sarajevo in June 1914 - how did that become a war that killed roughly 17 million people? This course builds directly on GCSE History and A-level History (Edexcel 9HI0, Tudor England/German Reformation/Tudor rebellion), which the learner has already completed: do not re-teach Tudor political or religious history, and use the learner's existing source-criticism and essay-planning skills as a floor, not a ceiling. Degree-level history goes further in the ways the QAA benchmark statement specifies: engaging critically with historiography (not just facts, but how and why historians have disagreed), constructing the learner's own historical questions rather than only answering set ones, synthesising large and sometimes conflicting bodies of evidence, and sustaining a longer, more theoretically aware argument. Push the learner to name historians and schools of interpretation, not just events, and to interrogate sources for genre, purpose and silence, not only content. Historical facts (events, dates, figures) do not change with time; historiographical debates are living scholarship and should be taught as ongoing argument, not settled fact.

#### L6.WW.1 The causes and course of the First World War, 1914-18
**Causes and course of the First World War, 1914-18.** Long-term causes historians commonly group as MAIN: Militarism (an arms race, especially Anglo-German naval rivalry), Alliances (the Triple Alliance of Germany/Austria-Hungary/Italy versus the Triple Entente of France/Russia/Britain, which turned a regional crisis into a continental one), Imperialism (colonial rivalry and tension), and Nationalism (including Slavic nationalism in the Balkans, and German/French tension over Alsace-Lorraine, lost by France in 1871). The immediate trigger was the assassination of Archduke Franz Ferdinand of Austria-Hungary in Sarajevo (28 June 1914) by a Bosnian Serb nationalist; Austria-Hungary's ultimatum to Serbia, backed by Germany's 'blank cheque', triggered a rapid chain of alliance-bound mobilisations (the 'July Crisis') that produced general European war by early August 1914. The war on the Western Front became defined by static trench warfare and enormous casualties for minimal territorial gain (the Somme, 1916, over a million casualties combined; Verdun, 1916); the United States entered in 1917 after unrestricted German submarine warfare; the war ended with the Armistice of 11 November 1918, after German military collapse and domestic unrest.

#### L6.WW.2 The Russian Revolutions of 1917 in depth and the Civil War, 1917-21
**The Russian Revolutions of 1917, in depth.** Building on S04's comparative introduction: the February Revolution (March 1917) began with bread shortages and strikes in Petrograd, escalated when the garrison mutinied rather than suppress protesters, and forced Nicholas II's abdication (ending Romanov rule since 1613). The Provisional Government (led eventually by Alexander Kerensky) shared uneasy 'dual power' with the Petrograd Soviet, and its fatal decision to continue Russia's unpopular participation in the war, alongside its failure to address peasant land hunger, steadily eroded its authority. Lenin's return from exile (April 1917) and his 'April Theses' ('Peace, Land, Bread'; all power to the Soviets) sharpened Bolshevik opposition; a failed government crackdown after the 'July Days', followed by the failed right-wing Kornilov Affair (August 1917, which the Bolsheviks helped resist, boosting their credibility), left the Provisional Government fatally weakened. The Bolsheviks seized power in Petrograd on 25 October/7 November 1917 (the October Revolution), a swift, organised action rather than a mass uprising. The new Bolshevik government took Russia out of the war (Treaty of Brest-Litovsk, March 1918, ceding vast territory) and faced a brutal Civil War (1917-21) against the anti-Bolshevik 'Whites' and foreign intervention forces, won by the Red Army under Trotsky's organisation, accompanied by the harsh economic policy of 'War Communism'.

#### L6.WW.3 The Paris Peace Settlement (1919) and its consequences for interwar Europe
**The Paris Peace Settlement, 1919, and its consequences.** The victorious Allied powers (chiefly Britain's Lloyd George, France's Clemenceau and the USA's Woodrow Wilson, whose competing priorities - Wilson's idealistic Fourteen Points versus Clemenceau's demand for French security and punitive terms - shaped fraught negotiations) produced the Treaty of Versailles (signed with Germany, 1919). Its terms included the 'War Guilt' clause (Article 231, assigning Germany and its allies responsibility for the war, the legal basis for reparations), substantial reparations payments, territorial losses (Alsace-Lorraine returned to France; the Polish Corridor separating East Prussia from the rest of Germany), and severe military restrictions (the army capped at 100,000, no air force, a demilitarised Rhineland). The Settlement also established the League of Nations, Wilson's central idealistic project, though the US Senate's refusal to ratify US membership critically weakened it from the outset. Contemporaries and historians have long debated the settlement's wisdom: the economist John Maynard Keynes's *The Economic Consequences of the Peace* (1919) argued the reparations were economically ruinous and would destabilise Europe, a view later historians (e.g. revisionist responses defending Versailles as more measured than the 'Carthaginian peace' myth suggests) have contested; the settlement's perceived harshness became, regardless, a central grievance Nazi propaganda later exploited (S10).
