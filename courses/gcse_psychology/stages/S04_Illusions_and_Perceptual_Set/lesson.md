# S04_Illusions_and_Perceptual_Set - Lesson: Visual illusions and factors affecting perception

## Goal
The learner explains the main types of visual illusion (ambiguity, misinterpreted depth cues, fiction, size constancy) with named examples, and explains how perceptual set (culture, motivation, emotion, expectation) affects perception, citing the Gilchrist and Nesberg, and Bruner and Minturn studies.

## Syllabus items taught here
- 2.3 - Visual illusions: explanations (ambiguity, misinterpreted depth cues, fiction, size constancy) and named examples
- 2.4 - Factors affecting perception: perceptual set, culture, motivation and emotion (Gilchrist and Nesberg; Bruner and Minturn)

## How to teach this
Describe the Müller-Lyer illusion (two equal-length lines, one with arrow-heads pointing inward, one with arrow-heads pointing outward) and ask the learner which line looks longer -- most people say the one with outward-pointing arrow-heads, even though both are the same length. Every named study or theory in this course (e.g. Murdock, Bartlett, Piaget, McGarrigle and Donaldson, Hughes, Dweck, Gibson, Gregory, Gilchrist and Nesberg, Bruner and Minturn, Asch, Milgram, Adorno, Piliavin, Von Frisch, Darwin, Hebb, Penfield, Tulving, Wiles, Kaij) is a genuine, correctly attributed piece of psychology, checked against real psychological knowledge when this course was built -- present it as real research, never as illustrative fiction, and if the learner misremembers who did a study or what it found, correct this explicitly. AQA GCSE Psychology has a substantial Research Methods strand (3.1.4, including 3.1.4.1 Data Handling): sampling methods, experimental design, types of data, and descriptive statistics (mean, median, mode, range) are examined with real calculations, not just definitions -- every numerical worked example in this course was computed and checked with Python when the course was built, and the learner should always be made to show their working for a calculation, since AQA awards credit for correct method as well as a correct final answer. Diagrams this course cannot draw (e.g. the multi-store model boxes, visual illusions, brain lobes, a neuron, a scatter diagram) are described precisely in words -- have the learner sketch or describe each one back, since being able to label a diagram accurately is itself examined.

#### 2.3 Visual illusions: explanations (ambiguity, misinterpreted depth cues, fiction, size constancy) and named examples
A **visual illusion** is a mismatch between how something is physically measured and how it is actually perceived. Illusions are explained in several ways: **ambiguity** (the image can be interpreted in more than one way, so perception can flip between interpretations, e.g. **Rubin's vase**, seen as either a white vase or two facing black profiles, and the **Necker cube**, a wireframe cube whose front face can flip between two positions); **misinterpreted depth cues** (monocular depth cues are applied to a flat, 2D figure where they do not actually indicate real depth, distorting the perceived size of parts of the image, e.g. the **Ponzo illusion**, where two identical horizontal lines are placed across a pair of converging lines that look like railway tracks receding into the distance, making the upper line look longer because linear perspective is misapplied; and the **Müller-Lyer illusion**, where two equal-length lines with different arrow-head/fin directions at their ends are perceived as different lengths); **fiction** (the brain perceives contours or shapes that are not actually physically present in the image, e.g. the **Kanizsa triangle**, where the arrangement of three 'Pac-Man'-like shapes creates the strong impression of a bright white triangle with edges that do not exist); and **size constancy miscued** (size constancy is applied inappropriately to a 2D scene, distorting perceived size, e.g. the **Ames Room**, a distorted room built to look rectangular from one fixed viewing point, which makes two people of very different actual size, standing in different corners, appear to be roughly the same size, or one appear to grow/shrink dramatically as they walk across it).

#### 2.4 Factors affecting perception: perceptual set, culture, motivation and emotion (Gilchrist and Nesberg; Bruner and Minturn)
**Perceptual set** is a readiness or predisposition to perceive certain things in a certain way, based on expectation, rather than perceiving purely objectively -- it means that what a person perceives can be influenced by factors beyond the raw sensory stimulus, including **culture** (growing up in a particular visual/cultural environment shapes which depth cues and interpretations feel natural), **motivation** (current needs or drives can bias perception towards need-relevant stimuli), **emotion** and **expectation** (what a person expects to see, based on context or instruction, biases what they actually perceive). **Gilchrist and Nesberg (1952)** demonstrated the effect of motivation: participants who had been food-deprived (hungry) rated pictures of food as brighter/more vivid than participants who had recently eaten, showing perception of the same stimulus was biased by the internal motivational state (hunger) of the observer. **Bruner and Minturn (1955)** demonstrated the effect of expectation/context: participants were shown an ambiguous figure that could be read either as the letter 'B' or the number '13', depending only on whether it was presented within a sequence of letters or a sequence of numbers -- the surrounding context created an expectation (a perceptual set) that changed how the identical ambiguous figure was perceived.
