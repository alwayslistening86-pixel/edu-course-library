# S17_Brain_Structure_and_Neuropsychology - Lesson: Brain structure, localisation of function and neuropsychology

## Goal
The learner names the lobes of the brain and their localised functions, citing Penfield's interpretive cortex study, and explains cognitive neuroscience, brain-scanning techniques, Tulving's memory study, and the effects of neurological damage on motor ability.

## Syllabus items taught here
- 7.3 - Brain structure and function: the lobes and cerebellum, and localisation of function (Penfield's interpretive cortex study)
- 7.4 - Neuropsychology: cognitive neuroscience, brain-scanning techniques (CT, PET, fMRI), Tulving's memory study, and effects of neurological damage

## How to teach this
Ask the learner to touch the back of their head, the top, the side above their ear, and the very front -- each of these roughly overlies a different lobe of the brain with a different main job. Every named study or theory in this course (e.g. Murdock, Bartlett, Piaget, McGarrigle and Donaldson, Hughes, Dweck, Gibson, Gregory, Gilchrist and Nesberg, Bruner and Minturn, Asch, Milgram, Adorno, Piliavin, Von Frisch, Darwin, Hebb, Penfield, Tulving, Wiles, Kaij) is a genuine, correctly attributed piece of psychology, checked against real psychological knowledge when this course was built -- present it as real research, never as illustrative fiction, and if the learner misremembers who did a study or what it found, correct this explicitly. AQA GCSE Psychology has a substantial Research Methods strand (3.1.4, including 3.1.4.1 Data Handling): sampling methods, experimental design, types of data, and descriptive statistics (mean, median, mode, range) are examined with real calculations, not just definitions -- every numerical worked example in this course was computed and checked with Python when the course was built, and the learner should always be made to show their working for a calculation, since AQA awards credit for correct method as well as a correct final answer. Diagrams this course cannot draw (e.g. the multi-store model boxes, visual illusions, brain lobes, a neuron, a scatter diagram) are described precisely in words -- have the learner sketch or describe each one back, since being able to label a diagram accurately is itself examined.

#### 7.3 Brain structure and function: the lobes and cerebellum, and localisation of function (Penfield's interpretive cortex study)
The brain's outer layer, the **cortex**, is divided into four **lobes**, each associated with particular functions (**localisation of function**): the **frontal lobe** (at the front, involved in movement/motor control, planning, decision-making and aspects of personality), the **parietal lobe** (towards the top/back, involved in processing somatosensory information -- touch, temperature, pain, and spatial awareness), the **temporal lobe** (at the sides, involved in processing auditory/hearing information and, in the left temporal lobe for most people, aspects of language comprehension), and the **occipital lobe** (at the very back, primarily responsible for processing visual information). The **cerebellum**, at the base of the brain, coordinates fine movement and balance. Direct evidence for localisation of function comes from **Penfield's** research, in which the brains of conscious patients undergoing surgery were directly electrically stimulated: stimulating the **motor cortex** produced specific, localised movements, stimulating the **somatosensory cortex** produced localised sensations, and stimulating parts of the temporal lobe (which Penfield termed the **'interpretive cortex'**) sometimes triggered vivid, detailed memory-like experiences or flashbacks in the patient, supporting the idea that specific psychological functions, including aspects of memory, are localised to specific brain regions rather than spread evenly across the whole brain.

#### 7.4 Neuropsychology: cognitive neuroscience, brain-scanning techniques (CT, PET, fMRI), Tulving's memory study, and effects of neurological damage
**Neuropsychology** studies the relationship between brain structure/function and behaviour/cognition; the modern field of **cognitive neuroscience** uses brain-imaging techniques to connect specific brain activity directly to specific mental processes as they happen. Brain-scanning techniques include: **CT (computed tomography)** scans (use X-rays taken from many angles to build a detailed structural image of the brain, useful for showing physical damage such as a tumour or bleed, but not ongoing brain activity); **PET (positron emission tomography)** scans (track a radioactive tracer to show which brain areas are most metabolically active, i.e. using the most energy, while a person carries out a task, showing brain function/activity); and **fMRI (functional magnetic resonance imaging)** scans (measure changes in blood oxygen levels to show which brain areas are more active during a task, without radiation, and with good spatial detail of ongoing brain function). **Tulving's** research used brain scanning (PET) to investigate memory, providing evidence that different types of long-term memory (e.g. episodic and semantic memory) are associated with distinct, at least partly separate patterns of brain activity, supporting the idea that memory itself is not handled by a single, uniform brain area. Studying the effects of **neurological damage** (e.g. from a stroke, injury or tumour) on **motor abilities** provides further evidence for localisation: damage to the motor cortex or related areas can produce very specific patterns of weakness, paralysis or loss of coordinated movement corresponding closely to the particular body area controlled by the damaged brain region, rather than a uniform, general loss of all motor function.
