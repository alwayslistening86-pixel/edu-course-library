# S10_Data_Handling_and_Statistics - Lesson: Data handling and descriptive statistics

## Goal
The learner distinguishes quantitative from qualitative and primary from secondary data, and calculates and interprets the mean, median, mode and range of a data set, and describes tables/charts and the normal distribution.

## Syllabus items taught here
- 4.6 - Data handling: quantitative/qualitative and primary/secondary data; computation; descriptive statistics (mean, median, mode, range); tables, charts and the normal distribution

## How to teach this
Give the learner a short list of numbers (e.g. test scores from a small class) and ask them to find the 'typical' score in three different ways before you name mean, median and mode -- they will likely already have an intuition for at least one. Every named study or theory in this course (e.g. Murdock, Bartlett, Piaget, McGarrigle and Donaldson, Hughes, Dweck, Gibson, Gregory, Gilchrist and Nesberg, Bruner and Minturn, Asch, Milgram, Adorno, Piliavin, Von Frisch, Darwin, Hebb, Penfield, Tulving, Wiles, Kaij) is a genuine, correctly attributed piece of psychology, checked against real psychological knowledge when this course was built -- present it as real research, never as illustrative fiction, and if the learner misremembers who did a study or what it found, correct this explicitly. AQA GCSE Psychology has a substantial Research Methods strand (3.1.4, including 3.1.4.1 Data Handling): sampling methods, experimental design, types of data, and descriptive statistics (mean, median, mode, range) are examined with real calculations, not just definitions -- every numerical worked example in this course was computed and checked with Python when the course was built, and the learner should always be made to show their working for a calculation, since AQA awards credit for correct method as well as a correct final answer. Diagrams this course cannot draw (e.g. the multi-store model boxes, visual illusions, brain lobes, a neuron, a scatter diagram) are described precisely in words -- have the learner sketch or describe each one back, since being able to label a diagram accurately is itself examined.

#### 4.6 Data handling: quantitative/qualitative and primary/secondary data; computation; descriptive statistics (mean, median, mode, range); tables, charts and the normal distribution
Data collected in psychological research is of different types. **Quantitative data** is numerical (e.g. reaction time in milliseconds, number of words recalled) -- easy to analyse statistically and compare objectively, but can lack the depth/detail of qualitative data. **Qualitative data** is non-numerical, descriptive data (e.g. a participant's own words in an interview) -- rich in detail and meaning, but harder to analyse objectively and compare across participants. Data is also classed by its source: **primary data** is collected first-hand by the researcher for the specific purpose of their own study (e.g. running an experiment themselves); **secondary data** is data that already existed, collected by someone else for a different original purpose, and reused by the researcher (e.g. official crime statistics, or data from a previous published study). Basic **computation** skills used in psychology include working with decimals, standard form, ratios, fractions and percentages, and calculating an **arithmetic mean** to an appropriate number of **significant figures**. Three key **descriptive statistics** summarise a data set with a single 'typical' value or spread: the **mean** (add up all the values and divide by how many values there are -- uses every value, so is sensitive to extreme/outlier values); the **median** (arrange all values in order and take the middle value -- if there is an even number of values, average the two middle ones -- not affected by extreme values); the **mode** (the value that occurs most often -- useful for data that is not numerical, and a data set can have more than one mode, called bimodal/multimodal, or no mode at all if every value is different); and the **range** (the highest value minus the lowest value, a simple measure of spread). *Worked example:* eight participants recall these numbers of words: 4, 5, 5, 6, 6, 7, 8, 9. **Mean** = (4+5+5+6+6+7+8+9) / 8 = 50 / 8 = **6.25**. **Median**: already ordered, with 8 values the middle two are the 4th and 5th values (6 and 6), so median = (6+6)/2 = **6**. **Mode**: both 5 and 6 occur twice, more than any other value, so this data set is **bimodal**, with modes of 5 and 6. **Range** = 9 - 4 = **5**. Data can be displayed and interpreted using **frequency tables** (a simple count of how often each value/category occurs), **bar charts** (for categorical/discrete data, with gaps between bars), **histograms** (for continuous data, with no gaps between bars, and bar area representing frequency), and **scatter diagrams** (for showing a correlation between two co-variables, one plotted on each axis). Finally, many psychological variables (e.g. IQ scores) approximate a **normal distribution** when plotted as a frequency curve: a symmetrical, bell-shaped curve where the mean, median and mode are all equal and fall at the centre/peak of the curve, with values becoming progressively less frequent the further they are from the centre in either direction.
