## How to teach this
Ask the learner whether they think an ordinary, otherwise kind person could be persuaded by an authority figure to seriously hurt a stranger -- Milgram's research is famous precisely because the answer, for many participants, was yes. Every named study or theory in this course (e.g. Murdock, Bartlett, Piaget, McGarrigle and Donaldson, Hughes, Dweck, Gibson, Gregory, Gilchrist and Nesberg, Bruner and Minturn, Asch, Milgram, Adorno, Piliavin, Von Frisch, Darwin, Hebb, Penfield, Tulving, Wiles, Kaij) is a genuine, correctly attributed piece of psychology, checked against real psychological knowledge when this course was built -- present it as real research, never as illustrative fiction, and if the learner misremembers who did a study or what it found, correct this explicitly. AQA GCSE Psychology has a substantial Research Methods strand (3.1.4, including 3.1.4.1 Data Handling): sampling methods, experimental design, types of data, and descriptive statistics (mean, median, mode, range) are examined with real calculations, not just definitions -- every numerical worked example in this course was computed and checked with Python when the course was built, and the learner should always be made to show their working for a calculation, since AQA awards credit for correct method as well as a correct final answer. Diagrams this course cannot draw (e.g. the multi-store model boxes, visual illusions, brain lobes, a neuron, a scatter diagram) are described precisely in words -- have the learner sketch or describe each one back, since being able to label a diagram accurately is itself examined.

#### 5.2 Obedience: Milgram's agency theory (agency, authority, culture, proximity) and Adorno's authoritarian personality theory
**Obedience** is complying with a direct instruction from a figure of authority. **Milgram's agency theory** explains obedience through the concept of **agency**: a person can act in an **autonomous state** (behaving according to their own free will and conscience, feeling personally responsible for their actions) or shift into an **agentic state** (seeing themselves as an agent carrying out another person's wishes/orders, and so no longer feeling personally responsible for the consequences -- responsibility is instead attributed to the person giving the orders). Several factors make the shift into, and maintenance of, an agentic state more likely: the **authority** of the person giving instructions (legitimate, socially recognised authority, e.g. a scientist in a lab coat, increases obedience); **culture** (upbringing in a culture that strongly emphasises respect for and obedience to authority figures increases obedience); and **proximity** (obedience decreases as the physical or psychological distance between the person and either the authority figure or the victim of their actions increases -- obedience is highest when the authority figure is close by giving direct instructions, and when the victim is more distant/less visible). A separate, complementary explanation is **Adorno's authoritarian personality theory**, which proposes that some people have a personality type, often developed through a strict, harshly disciplinarian upbringing, that makes them especially likely to be obedient towards authority and hostile/prejudiced towards those perceived as of lower status -- this personality type was measured using an attitude scale (the F-scale), and Adorno argued it reflected a general disposition, not just a response to a particular situation, that predisposed a person towards greater obedience.
