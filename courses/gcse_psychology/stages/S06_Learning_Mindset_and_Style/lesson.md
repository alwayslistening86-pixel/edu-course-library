# S06_Learning_Mindset_and_Style - Lesson: Effects of learning on development: mindset and learning styles

## Goal
The learner explains Dweck's mindset theory (fixed vs growth mindset), the role of praise and self-efficacy beliefs, and describes the idea of learning styles alongside Willingham's evidence-based criticism of matching teaching to a claimed learning style.

## Syllabus items taught here
- 3.3 - Effects of learning on development: Dweck's mindset theory, praise and self-efficacy; learning styles and Willingham's critique

## How to teach this
Ask the learner how they feel when they get something wrong: do they think 'I'm just not good at this', or 'I haven't got it yet, but I can improve with practice'? Dweck's theory is about exactly that difference. Every named study or theory in this course (e.g. Murdock, Bartlett, Piaget, McGarrigle and Donaldson, Hughes, Dweck, Gibson, Gregory, Gilchrist and Nesberg, Bruner and Minturn, Asch, Milgram, Adorno, Piliavin, Von Frisch, Darwin, Hebb, Penfield, Tulving, Wiles, Kaij) is a genuine, correctly attributed piece of psychology, checked against real psychological knowledge when this course was built -- present it as real research, never as illustrative fiction, and if the learner misremembers who did a study or what it found, correct this explicitly. AQA GCSE Psychology has a substantial Research Methods strand (3.1.4, including 3.1.4.1 Data Handling): sampling methods, experimental design, types of data, and descriptive statistics (mean, median, mode, range) are examined with real calculations, not just definitions -- every numerical worked example in this course was computed and checked with Python when the course was built, and the learner should always be made to show their working for a calculation, since AQA awards credit for correct method as well as a correct final answer. Diagrams this course cannot draw (e.g. the multi-store model boxes, visual illusions, brain lobes, a neuron, a scatter diagram) are described precisely in words -- have the learner sketch or describe each one back, since being able to label a diagram accurately is itself examined.

#### 3.3 Effects of learning on development: Dweck's mindset theory, praise and self-efficacy; learning styles and Willingham's critique
**Dweck's mindset theory** proposes that people hold implicit beliefs about the nature of ability, which affect how they respond to challenge and failure. A **fixed mindset** is the belief that intelligence/ability is a fixed trait that cannot be meaningfully changed -- people with a fixed mindset tend to avoid challenges (fearing failure will reveal a lack of ability) and give up more easily after setbacks. A **growth mindset** is the belief that intelligence/ability can be developed through effort, good strategies and learning from mistakes -- people with a growth mindset tend to seek out challenges and persist for longer after setbacks, since effort and difficulty are seen as part of learning, not evidence of low ability. The **type of praise** a learner receives can shape which mindset develops: praising a specific **outcome/ability** ('you're so clever') tends to encourage a fixed mindset and can make children more reluctant to attempt harder tasks afterwards for fear of losing that label, whereas praising **effort and process** ('you worked really hard and tried a different strategy') tends to encourage a growth mindset and greater persistence. This links to **self-efficacy** -- a person's belief in their own capability to succeed at a task -- which a growth mindset and effort-focused praise both tend to strengthen. Separately, the idea of **learning styles** proposes that individuals have a preferred way of taking in information, e.g. **verbalisers** (who learn best through words/text) versus **visualisers** (who learn best through images/diagrams), and that matching teaching methods to a learner's preferred style should improve learning. However, **Willingham's learning theory** reviewed the evidence for this claim and argued it is not well supported: while people do have preferences for how information is presented, well-controlled studies do not show that matching teaching style to a person's claimed 'learning style' actually improves learning outcomes compared with using the method best suited to the content itself (e.g. diagrams for spatial content, words for verbal content, regardless of the individual learner's stated preference).
