# S13_Prosocial_and_Crowd_Behaviour - Lesson: Prosocial behaviour and crowd/collective behaviour

## Goal
The learner explains social and dispositional factors affecting bystander intervention, citing Piliavin's subway study, and explains prosocial/antisocial crowd behaviour through social loafing, deindividuation, culture and dispositional factors.

## Syllabus items taught here
- 5.3 - Prosocial behaviour: bystander intervention, social and dispositional factors (Piliavin's subway study)
- 5.4 - Crowd and collective behaviour: prosocial and antisocial crowd behaviour, social loafing, deindividuation, and dispositional factors

## How to teach this
Ask the learner: if they saw someone collapse in a busy street, would they be more or less likely to stop and help if there were twenty other people also walking past? Bystander research suggests, surprisingly, less likely. Every named study or theory in this course (e.g. Murdock, Bartlett, Piaget, McGarrigle and Donaldson, Hughes, Dweck, Gibson, Gregory, Gilchrist and Nesberg, Bruner and Minturn, Asch, Milgram, Adorno, Piliavin, Von Frisch, Darwin, Hebb, Penfield, Tulving, Wiles, Kaij) is a genuine, correctly attributed piece of psychology, checked against real psychological knowledge when this course was built -- present it as real research, never as illustrative fiction, and if the learner misremembers who did a study or what it found, correct this explicitly. AQA GCSE Psychology has a substantial Research Methods strand (3.1.4, including 3.1.4.1 Data Handling): sampling methods, experimental design, types of data, and descriptive statistics (mean, median, mode, range) are examined with real calculations, not just definitions -- every numerical worked example in this course was computed and checked with Python when the course was built, and the learner should always be made to show their working for a calculation, since AQA awards credit for correct method as well as a correct final answer. Diagrams this course cannot draw (e.g. the multi-store model boxes, visual illusions, brain lobes, a neuron, a scatter diagram) are described precisely in words -- have the learner sketch or describe each one back, since being able to label a diagram accurately is itself examined.

#### 5.3 Prosocial behaviour: bystander intervention, social and dispositional factors (Piliavin's subway study)
**Prosocial behaviour** is voluntary behaviour intended to benefit others, such as **bystander intervention** -- helping a stranger in an emergency. Whether a bystander intervenes is affected by **social factors**: the **presence of others** (a person is often less likely to help when other bystanders are present, since responsibility for helping is spread across the group -- diffusion of responsibility -- and each individual feels less personally obliged to act) and the **cost of helping** (physical risk, embarrassment, time, or unpleasantness involved in helping reduces the likelihood of intervention). **Dispositional factors** also matter: **similarity to the victim** (people are generally more likely to help someone they perceive as similar to themselves) and **expertise** (someone with relevant skills, e.g. first-aid training, is more likely to intervene, being more confident they can actually help). **Piliavin et al.'s (1969)** field study staged apparent medical emergencies on New York subway trains: an actor collapsed, appearing either "ill" (carrying a cane) or "drunk" (smelling of alcohol), and real passengers' helping behaviour was observed. Helping was faster and more frequent for the apparently ill victim than the apparently drunk victim, and helping was reduced when the cost of helping was made higher (e.g. when the collapsed person appeared to be bleeding), supporting the roles of both a victim characteristic (perceived cause of the emergency) and the cost of helping in bystander intervention.

#### 5.4 Crowd and collective behaviour: prosocial and antisocial crowd behaviour, social loafing, deindividuation, and dispositional factors
**Crowd behaviour** can be **prosocial** (a crowd behaving helpfully, cooperatively or supportively, e.g. cooperating calmly during an evacuation) or **antisocial** (a crowd behaving destructively or aggressively, e.g. rioting or looting), and several factors help explain which occurs. **Social factors** include **social loafing** (individuals exert less effort on a task when working as part of a group than when working alone, since individual contribution feels less noticeable/accountable -- this can reduce prosocial coordination in a crowd), **deindividuation** (being part of a large, often anonymous crowd can reduce a person's sense of individual identity and personal responsibility, and can weaken normal restraints on behaviour, making both extreme prosocial acts, such as large-scale spontaneous cooperation, and extreme antisocial acts, such as violence a lone individual would not commit, more likely) and **culture** (cultural norms about acceptable crowd behaviour, and the presence of a shared social identity/purpose, shape whether a crowd's behaviour tends towards cooperation or conflict). **Dispositional factors** also play a role: a crowd member's own **personality** (e.g. how impulsive or aggressive they are as an individual) and their **morality** (their personal sense of right and wrong, and how strongly they hold to it under group pressure) can make them more or less likely to go along with antisocial crowd behaviour even when deindividuation and social pressure are present.
