# S16_Nervous_System_and_Neurons - Lesson: The nervous system and neurons

## Goal
The learner describes the structure and function of the nervous system (central and peripheral, somatic and autonomic), the fight-or-flight response, the James-Lange theory of emotion, and the structure/function of sensory, relay and motor neurons and synaptic transmission, citing Hebb's theory of learning.

## Syllabus items taught here
- 7.1 - The nervous system: central and peripheral (somatic and autonomic) divisions; the fight-or-flight response; James-Lange theory of emotion
- 7.2 - Neuron structure and function: sensory, relay and motor neurons; synaptic transmission; Hebb's theory of learning

## How to teach this
Ask the learner what physically happens in their body in the seconds after a sudden fright (racing heart, faster breathing) -- that is the autonomic nervous system's fight-or-flight response in action. Every named study or theory in this course (e.g. Murdock, Bartlett, Piaget, McGarrigle and Donaldson, Hughes, Dweck, Gibson, Gregory, Gilchrist and Nesberg, Bruner and Minturn, Asch, Milgram, Adorno, Piliavin, Von Frisch, Darwin, Hebb, Penfield, Tulving, Wiles, Kaij) is a genuine, correctly attributed piece of psychology, checked against real psychological knowledge when this course was built -- present it as real research, never as illustrative fiction, and if the learner misremembers who did a study or what it found, correct this explicitly. AQA GCSE Psychology has a substantial Research Methods strand (3.1.4, including 3.1.4.1 Data Handling): sampling methods, experimental design, types of data, and descriptive statistics (mean, median, mode, range) are examined with real calculations, not just definitions -- every numerical worked example in this course was computed and checked with Python when the course was built, and the learner should always be made to show their working for a calculation, since AQA awards credit for correct method as well as a correct final answer. Diagrams this course cannot draw (e.g. the multi-store model boxes, visual illusions, brain lobes, a neuron, a scatter diagram) are described precisely in words -- have the learner sketch or describe each one back, since being able to label a diagram accurately is itself examined.

#### 7.1 The nervous system: central and peripheral (somatic and autonomic) divisions; the fight-or-flight response; James-Lange theory of emotion
The **nervous system** is divided into the **central nervous system (CNS)** (the brain and spinal cord, responsible for processing information and coordinating a response) and the **peripheral nervous system (PNS)** (all the nerves outside the CNS, connecting it to the rest of the body). The PNS is further divided into the **somatic nervous system** (controls voluntary, conscious movement of skeletal muscles, and carries sensory information to the CNS) and the **autonomic nervous system** (controls involuntary, automatic functions of internal organs, e.g. heart rate, digestion, breathing rate). The autonomic nervous system produces the **fight-or-flight response** when a threat is perceived: it rapidly prepares the body for action by increasing heart rate and breathing rate, diverting blood flow to the muscles, and releasing adrenaline, so the body is ready to either confront the threat ('fight') or escape it ('flight'). The **James-Lange theory of emotion** proposes an order of events that many people find counter-intuitive: rather than an emotion causing a physiological (bodily) response (e.g. feeling afraid, which then causes a racing heart), James and Lange argued the **physiological response happens first**, and the brain's interpretation of that bodily arousal **is** the emotion itself (e.g. noticing a racing heart and rapid breathing, and interpreting that specific pattern of arousal as 'fear') -- so, on this theory, we feel afraid *because* our body is reacting, not the other way around.

#### 7.2 Neuron structure and function: sensory, relay and motor neurons; synaptic transmission; Hebb's theory of learning
**Neurons** are the specialised cells that transmit information, as electrical impulses, around the nervous system. There are three main types: **sensory neurons** (carry information from sensory receptors, e.g. in the skin or eyes, towards the CNS), **relay neurons** (found mainly within the CNS, connecting sensory neurons to motor neurons, and involved in processing/decision-making), and **motor neurons** (carry information from the CNS out to effectors, e.g. muscles, to produce a response/movement). Where two neurons meet, there is a tiny gap called a **synapse**; **synaptic transmission** is the process by which a signal crosses this gap: an electrical impulse reaching the end of one neuron triggers the release of a chemical messenger, a **neurotransmitter**, into the synapse, which crosses the gap and binds to receptors on the next neuron, triggering a new electrical impulse there if the signal is strong enough -- this converts the signal from electrical to chemical and back to electrical again as it passes from neuron to neuron. **Hebb's theory of learning** (1949) proposed that learning and memory happen through changes in the strength of connections between neurons that are frequently activated together -- often summarised as 'neurons that fire together, wire together': when two connected neurons are repeatedly activated at the same time, the synaptic connection between them is strengthened, making it more likely that activating one will trigger the other in future, which Hebb proposed as the basic biological mechanism underlying learning and the formation of memories.
