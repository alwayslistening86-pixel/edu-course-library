# S14_Language_and_Thought - Lesson: Language and thought, and human/animal communication

## Goal
The learner compares Piaget's and the Sapir-Whorf views on the relationship between language and thought, citing cross-cultural evidence, and compares human and animal communication, including Von Frisch's honeybee study.

## Syllabus items taught here
- 6.1 - The relationship between language and thought: Piaget's and the Sapir-Whorf (linguistic relativity) views, with cross-cultural evidence
- 6.2 - Human and animal communication compared, including Von Frisch's honeybee dance study

## How to teach this
Ask the learner whether they think in words, or whether thoughts come first and words are just how they are expressed afterwards -- psychologists disagree on exactly this question. Every named study or theory in this course (e.g. Murdock, Bartlett, Piaget, McGarrigle and Donaldson, Hughes, Dweck, Gibson, Gregory, Gilchrist and Nesberg, Bruner and Minturn, Asch, Milgram, Adorno, Piliavin, Von Frisch, Darwin, Hebb, Penfield, Tulving, Wiles, Kaij) is a genuine, correctly attributed piece of psychology, checked against real psychological knowledge when this course was built -- present it as real research, never as illustrative fiction, and if the learner misremembers who did a study or what it found, correct this explicitly. AQA GCSE Psychology has a substantial Research Methods strand (3.1.4, including 3.1.4.1 Data Handling): sampling methods, experimental design, types of data, and descriptive statistics (mean, median, mode, range) are examined with real calculations, not just definitions -- every numerical worked example in this course was computed and checked with Python when the course was built, and the learner should always be made to show their working for a calculation, since AQA awards credit for correct method as well as a correct final answer. Diagrams this course cannot draw (e.g. the multi-store model boxes, visual illusions, brain lobes, a neuron, a scatter diagram) are described precisely in words -- have the learner sketch or describe each one back, since being able to label a diagram accurately is itself examined.

#### 6.1 The relationship between language and thought: Piaget's and the Sapir-Whorf (linguistic relativity) views, with cross-cultural evidence
Two contrasting views explain the relationship between language and thought. **Piaget** argued that **language depends on thought**: cognitive development comes first, and language is simply one way of expressing thoughts and concepts a child has already developed through their stage of cognitive development -- a child cannot express a concept in language until they have first understood it. In direct contrast, the **Sapir-Whorf hypothesis** (linguistic relativity) argues that **thinking depends on/is shaped by language**: the specific language a person speaks influences, or in its strongest form even determines, how they are able to think about and categorise the world -- if a language has no word for a concept, or fewer words to distinguish something, speakers of that language may find that concept harder to perceive or think about precisely. Evidence used to examine this debate includes **cross-cultural studies of colour recognition**: languages differ in how many basic colour terms they have and where they draw the boundaries between colours, and speakers of a language with fewer distinct colour terms have sometimes been found to be slower or less accurate at distinguishing between colours that their language does not separately name, compared with speakers of a language that does distinguish them -- broadly consistent with the Sapir-Whorf view that language shapes perception/thought, although other studies find that people can still perceive real colour differences perceptually even without a specific word for them, which is more consistent with Piaget's view that the underlying thought/perception can exist before, or independently of, language. Similarly, **cross-cultural differences in event recall** (e.g. whether a language grammatically requires specifying who caused an accidental event) have been linked to differences in how confidently speakers of different languages later recall or assign blame for that event, again used as evidence for the influence of language on thought.

#### 6.2 Human and animal communication compared, including Von Frisch's honeybee dance study
**Animal communication** serves specific, largely instinctive functions: **survival** (e.g. alarm calls warning of predators), **reproduction** (e.g. mating calls/displays), **territory** (e.g. marking or defending an area) and **finding food** (e.g. signalling the location of a food source to others). A well-studied example is **Von Frisch's** research decoding the **honeybee waggle dance**: returning worker bees perform a figure-of-eight dance on the honeycomb whose angle (relative to the sun) and duration/vigour communicate the direction and distance of a food source to other bees in the hive, a remarkably precise and information-rich signalling system, for which Von Frisch was awarded the Nobel Prize. **Human communication**, while sharing some similar instinctive elements (e.g. an alarm cry), differs in important ways: human language is generative and abstract, allowing people to **plan ahead and discuss future events** (e.g. discussing a holiday next year, or a hypothetical situation that has never happened), to communicate about things not physically present, and to combine a finite set of words into an effectively unlimited number of new, meaningful sentences -- a flexibility not clearly demonstrated in known animal communication systems, which tend to be limited to a fixed, largely instinctive set of signals tied closely to the immediate present or a specific biological function.
