# S08_Experimental_and_Other_Methods - Lesson: Research methods: experimental design and other research methods

## Goal
The learner explains experimental designs (independent groups, repeated measures, matched pairs) and types of experiment (laboratory, field, natural), and describes non-experimental methods (questionnaires, interviews, case studies, observation, correlation), each with a strength and weakness.

## Syllabus items taught here
- 4.3 - Experimental design and types of experiment: independent groups, repeated measures, matched pairs; laboratory, field and natural experiments
- 4.4 - Non-experimental methods: questionnaires, interviews, case studies and observation (behaviour categories, interobserver reliability), and correlation

## How to teach this
Ask the learner: if you wanted to test whether a memory technique works, would you rather use the same participants twice (before and after learning the technique) or two different groups of participants -- and what problem might each choice cause? Every named study or theory in this course (e.g. Murdock, Bartlett, Piaget, McGarrigle and Donaldson, Hughes, Dweck, Gibson, Gregory, Gilchrist and Nesberg, Bruner and Minturn, Asch, Milgram, Adorno, Piliavin, Von Frisch, Darwin, Hebb, Penfield, Tulving, Wiles, Kaij) is a genuine, correctly attributed piece of psychology, checked against real psychological knowledge when this course was built -- present it as real research, never as illustrative fiction, and if the learner misremembers who did a study or what it found, correct this explicitly. AQA GCSE Psychology has a substantial Research Methods strand (3.1.4, including 3.1.4.1 Data Handling): sampling methods, experimental design, types of data, and descriptive statistics (mean, median, mode, range) are examined with real calculations, not just definitions -- every numerical worked example in this course was computed and checked with Python when the course was built, and the learner should always be made to show their working for a calculation, since AQA awards credit for correct method as well as a correct final answer. Diagrams this course cannot draw (e.g. the multi-store model boxes, visual illusions, brain lobes, a neuron, a scatter diagram) are described precisely in words -- have the learner sketch or describe each one back, since being able to label a diagram accurately is itself examined.

#### 4.3 Experimental design and types of experiment: independent groups, repeated measures, matched pairs; laboratory, field and natural experiments
An **experiment** deliberately manipulates an independent variable to measure its effect on a dependent variable, and can use one of three **experimental designs**: **independent groups** (different participants are used in each condition of the IV) -- *strength:* no order effects (e.g. practice or fatigue from doing the task twice); *weakness:* individual differences between the separate groups of participants could affect the DV instead of the IV, unless groups are carefully matched or randomly allocated. **Repeated measures** (the same participants take part in every condition of the IV) -- *strength:* removes participant/individual differences between conditions, since the same people are compared with themselves; *weakness:* vulnerable to order effects (e.g. practice, boredom or fatigue from doing the task more than once), usually reduced by counterbalancing the order conditions are given in. **Matched pairs** (different participants are used in each condition, but are matched as closely as possible on relevant characteristics, e.g. age or ability, with one of each matched pair going into each condition) -- *strength:* reduces the impact of individual differences compared with independent groups, without the order effects of repeated measures; *weakness:* very time-consuming to find and match suitable pairs, and matching can never be perfect. Experiments also differ by **setting**: a **laboratory experiment** takes place in a controlled, artificial environment, allowing high control over extraneous variables but potentially reducing how naturally participants behave; a **field experiment** takes place in participants' natural, everyday environment while the IV is still deliberately manipulated by the researcher, improving how natural behaviour is but reducing control; a **natural experiment** studies the effect of an IV that would have varied anyway, without the researcher manipulating it (e.g. comparing outcomes before and after a real policy change), allowing research into situations that could not ethically or practically be manipulated, but with less control and no random allocation to conditions.

#### 4.4 Non-experimental methods: questionnaires, interviews, case studies and observation (behaviour categories, interobserver reliability), and correlation
Not all research methods are experiments. **Questionnaires** (a fixed set of written questions, which can be open or closed) and **interviews** (questions asked and answered face-to-face, by phone or online, which can be structured, unstructured or semi-structured) both gather self-report data directly from participants -- *strength:* can gather a large amount of information, including about thoughts and feelings that cannot be directly observed; *weakness:* rely on participants answering honestly and accurately, and are vulnerable to social desirability bias (giving answers that make the participant look good rather than answers that are true). A **case study** is a detailed, in-depth investigation of a single individual, small group, or event, often using multiple methods (interview, observation, testing) together -- *strength:* provides very rich, detailed data, useful for rare or unusual cases that could not otherwise be studied; *weakness:* findings from one unique case cannot easily be generalised to other people. **Observation studies** involve watching and recording behaviour as it naturally occurs, using pre-defined **behaviour categories** (a clear, operationalised list of the specific behaviours being counted/recorded, so observers know exactly what to look for) -- *strength:* records what people actually do, rather than what they say they do; *weakness:* observer bias/subjectivity in interpreting behaviour, reduced by checking **interobserver reliability** (the extent to which two or more independent observers, using the same behaviour categories, agree in what they record; high agreement between observers increases confidence that the categories are being applied consistently and objectively). **Correlation** studies the strength and direction of the **association between two co-variables** that occur naturally, without either being deliberately manipulated (unlike an experiment's IV), and is often displayed using a **scatter diagram** with one co-variable on each axis -- *strength:* allows the study of relationships that could not ethically or practically be manipulated in an experiment; *weakness:* correlation cannot establish cause and effect, since a third, unmeasured variable might explain the relationship between the two co-variables.
