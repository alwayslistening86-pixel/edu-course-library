# S03_Perception_Cues_and_Theories - Lesson: Perception: sensation, depth cues and theories of perception

## Goal
The learner distinguishes sensation from perception, identifies monocular and binocular depth cues and visual constancies, and compares Gibson's direct theory with Gregory's constructivist theory of perception.

## Syllabus items taught here
- 2.1 - Sensation and perception distinguished; monocular and binocular depth cues; visual constancies
- 2.2 - Theories of perception: Gibson's direct theory and Gregory's constructivist (indirect) theory

## How to teach this
Hold up (or describe) two fingers, one closer to your face and one further away, and ask the learner how they know which is closer with both eyes open versus one eye closed -- that difference is what binocular depth cues add. Every named study or theory in this course (e.g. Murdock, Bartlett, Piaget, McGarrigle and Donaldson, Hughes, Dweck, Gibson, Gregory, Gilchrist and Nesberg, Bruner and Minturn, Asch, Milgram, Adorno, Piliavin, Von Frisch, Darwin, Hebb, Penfield, Tulving, Wiles, Kaij) is a genuine, correctly attributed piece of psychology, checked against real psychological knowledge when this course was built -- present it as real research, never as illustrative fiction, and if the learner misremembers who did a study or what it found, correct this explicitly. AQA GCSE Psychology has a substantial Research Methods strand (3.1.4, including 3.1.4.1 Data Handling): sampling methods, experimental design, types of data, and descriptive statistics (mean, median, mode, range) are examined with real calculations, not just definitions -- every numerical worked example in this course was computed and checked with Python when the course was built, and the learner should always be made to show their working for a calculation, since AQA awards credit for correct method as well as a correct final answer. Diagrams this course cannot draw (e.g. the multi-store model boxes, visual illusions, brain lobes, a neuron, a scatter diagram) are described precisely in words -- have the learner sketch or describe each one back, since being able to label a diagram accurately is itself examined.

#### 2.1 Sensation and perception distinguished; monocular and binocular depth cues; visual constancies
**Sensation** is the raw physical process of detecting a stimulus through the sense organs (e.g. light hitting the retina); **perception** is the psychological process of organising and interpreting that sensory information to make sense of the world (e.g. recognising the pattern of light as 'a friend's face'). Depth (distance) is judged using **depth cues**. **Monocular depth cues** work with just one eye: **height in plane** (objects higher in the visual field, closer to the horizon, appear further away), **relative size** (of two same-sized objects, the one that casts a smaller image on the retina appears further away), **occlusion/interposition** (an object that partly blocks another is perceived as nearer), and **linear perspective** (parallel lines, e.g. railway tracks, appear to converge as they recede into the distance). **Binocular depth cues** need both eyes working together: **retinal disparity** (each eye sees a scene from a very slightly different angle; the brain uses the difference between the two images to judge depth) and **convergence** (the eyes rotate inward to focus on a very close object; the brain uses the degree of muscular strain from this inward turning as a depth cue). **Perceptual/visual constancies** allow objects to be perceived as unchanging in real-world size, shape or colour even when the image on the retina changes, e.g. a door is still perceived as rectangular even when viewed from an angle that casts a trapezoid-shaped image on the retina (shape constancy); a person is perceived as the same size as they walk away, even though their retinal image shrinks (size constancy).

#### 2.2 Theories of perception: Gibson's direct theory and Gregory's constructivist (indirect) theory
Two contrasting theories explain how perception happens. **Gibson's direct theory of perception** argues that the information available in the visual environment (the 'optic array') is rich and detailed enough on its own for perception to happen **directly**, with no need for the brain to use past experience, memory or inference -- perception is fast, automatic and driven by the environment itself. A key part of Gibson's theory is **motion parallax**: as an observer moves, objects closer to them appear to move faster across the visual field than objects further away, giving direct information about relative distance without any need to interpret or guess. Gibson also argued the environment offers **affordances** -- direct information about what actions an object allows (e.g. a flat surface directly affords 'can be walked on'). In contrast, **Gregory's constructivist (indirect) theory of perception** argues perception is an **active, indirect process**: sensory information alone is often incomplete or ambiguous, so the brain must use **inference**, drawing on past experience, expectation and context to construct a 'best guess' interpretation -- essentially treating perception like forming and testing a hypothesis. Gregory's theory is generally seen as better able to explain why the same ambiguous image can be perceived in different ways by different people, or why **visual illusions** can trick the visual system into a mistaken perception, since these depend on prior experience and inference rather than on information directly available in the stimulus.
