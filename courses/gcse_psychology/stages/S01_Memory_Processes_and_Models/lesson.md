# S01_Memory_Processes_and_Models - Lesson: Memory: processes, types and the multi-store model

## Goal
The learner explains the three processes of memory (encoding, storage, retrieval), distinguishes episodic/semantic/procedural memory, and describes and evaluates the multi-store model, including coding/capacity/duration and the primacy and recency effects shown by Murdock's serial position research.

## Syllabus items taught here
- 1.1 - Processes of memory: encoding, storage and retrieval; episodic, semantic and procedural memory
- 1.2 - Structures of memory: the multi-store model (sensory register, STM, LTM), coding/capacity/duration, primacy and recency (Murdock)

## How to teach this
Ask the learner to recall, in order, the last ten items on a shopping list you read out slowly -- most people do best on the first few and the last few, and worst in the middle. That pattern is what this stage explains. Every named study or theory in this course (e.g. Murdock, Bartlett, Piaget, McGarrigle and Donaldson, Hughes, Dweck, Gibson, Gregory, Gilchrist and Nesberg, Bruner and Minturn, Asch, Milgram, Adorno, Piliavin, Von Frisch, Darwin, Hebb, Penfield, Tulving, Wiles, Kaij) is a genuine, correctly attributed piece of psychology, checked against real psychological knowledge when this course was built -- present it as real research, never as illustrative fiction, and if the learner misremembers who did a study or what it found, correct this explicitly. AQA GCSE Psychology has a substantial Research Methods strand (3.1.4, including 3.1.4.1 Data Handling): sampling methods, experimental design, types of data, and descriptive statistics (mean, median, mode, range) are examined with real calculations, not just definitions -- every numerical worked example in this course was computed and checked with Python when the course was built, and the learner should always be made to show their working for a calculation, since AQA awards credit for correct method as well as a correct final answer. Diagrams this course cannot draw (e.g. the multi-store model boxes, visual illusions, brain lobes, a neuron, a scatter diagram) are described precisely in words -- have the learner sketch or describe each one back, since being able to label a diagram accurately is itself examined.

#### 1.1 Processes of memory: encoding, storage and retrieval; episodic, semantic and procedural memory
Memory involves three processes: **encoding** (converting information into a form the brain can store, e.g. turning spoken words into a meaningful/visual/acoustic code), **storage** (keeping that encoded information over time) and **retrieval** (accessing and bringing stored information back to mind, e.g. into consciousness or into an answer). Long-term memory is often divided into types: **episodic memory** (memory for personally experienced events, tied to a time and place, e.g. what you did on your last birthday), **semantic memory** (memory for facts and general knowledge, not tied to when you learned them, e.g. knowing Paris is the capital of France) and **procedural memory** (memory for learned skills and how to do things, often hard to put into words, e.g. riding a bike).

#### 1.2 Structures of memory: the multi-store model (sensory register, STM, LTM), coding/capacity/duration, primacy and recency (Murdock)
The **multi-store model** (Atkinson and Shiffrin) describes memory as three separate stores information flows through: the **sensory register** (holds a very brief, detailed trace of sensory information, e.g. what you just saw, for under a second, unless attended to); **short-term memory (STM)** (holds a limited amount of information for a short time, roughly seconds to under a minute); and **long-term memory (LTM)** (can hold an effectively unlimited amount of information for a very long time, potentially a lifetime, if rehearsed enough to transfer from STM). Each store is described by its **coding** (the format information is stored in -- STM is mainly acoustic/sound-based, LTM is mainly semantic/meaning-based), its **capacity** (how much it can hold -- STM is limited, LTM is very large) and its **duration** (how long information lasts there without rehearsal -- STM is brief, LTM can be permanent). Rehearsal is what transfers information from STM into LTM. *The serial position effect:* when people try to recall a list of items immediately, they typically recall the **first few items well (the primacy effect** -- these had time to be rehearsed into LTM) and the **last few items well (the recency effect** -- these are still in STM at the point of recall), but recall the **middle items worst** (too late to still be in STM, not rehearsed enough to reach LTM). Plotting recall against position in the list gives a U-shaped **serial position curve**, demonstrated in research by **Murdock (1962)**, whose free-recall studies are among the classic evidence that STM and LTM are genuinely separate stores, since a delay before recall (filled with a distracting task) removes the recency effect but leaves the primacy effect intact.
