# S19_Addiction - Lesson: Addiction: characteristics, explanations and treatments

## Goal
The learner distinguishes addiction from substance misuse, applies the ICD dependence syndrome criteria, and explains biological (citing Kaij's twin study) and psychological explanations of addiction, alongside its treatments.

## Syllabus items taught here
- 8.3 - Addiction: characteristics and ICD dependence syndrome criteria; biological (Kaij's twin study) and psychological explanations; treatments

## How to teach this
Ask the learner what they think separates someone who occasionally drinks alcohol from someone who is dependent/addicted -- psychologists use specific diagnostic criteria to draw this line, rather than just judging by how much someone consumes. Every named study or theory in this course (e.g. Murdock, Bartlett, Piaget, McGarrigle and Donaldson, Hughes, Dweck, Gibson, Gregory, Gilchrist and Nesberg, Bruner and Minturn, Asch, Milgram, Adorno, Piliavin, Von Frisch, Darwin, Hebb, Penfield, Tulving, Wiles, Kaij) is a genuine, correctly attributed piece of psychology, checked against real psychological knowledge when this course was built -- present it as real research, never as illustrative fiction, and if the learner misremembers who did a study or what it found, correct this explicitly. AQA GCSE Psychology has a substantial Research Methods strand (3.1.4, including 3.1.4.1 Data Handling): sampling methods, experimental design, types of data, and descriptive statistics (mean, median, mode, range) are examined with real calculations, not just definitions -- every numerical worked example in this course was computed and checked with Python when the course was built, and the learner should always be made to show their working for a calculation, since AQA awards credit for correct method as well as a correct final answer. Diagrams this course cannot draw (e.g. the multi-store model boxes, visual illusions, brain lobes, a neuron, a scatter diagram) are described precisely in words -- have the learner sketch or describe each one back, since being able to label a diagram accurately is itself examined.

#### 8.3 Addiction: characteristics and ICD dependence syndrome criteria; biological (Kaij's twin study) and psychological explanations; treatments
**Addiction** is a compulsive, often harmful, dependence on a substance or behaviour, distinguished from **substance misuse** (using a substance in a way that is harmful or against guidance, e.g. drinking heavily on one occasion) mainly by the presence of **dependence** -- misuse does not necessarily involve the compulsion, loss of control and withdrawal characteristic of true addiction. The **ICD** sets out criteria for a **dependence syndrome**, including: **tolerance** (needing increasing amounts of the substance to achieve the same effect), **withdrawal** (unpleasant physical/psychological symptoms when the substance is stopped or reduced), a strong **compulsion** to use the substance, difficulty controlling use (**loss of control** over the amount used or when use stops), neglecting other activities/interests in favour of using the substance, and continuing to use the substance despite awareness of clear harm being caused. Two types of explanation are given for addiction: a **biological explanation** proposes a **genetic vulnerability** to addiction, inherited from parents, making some individuals more biologically susceptible than others when exposed to a substance -- **Kaij's** classic twin study of alcoholism compared concordance rates (how often both twins in a pair were affected) for alcoholism between identical (monozygotic) and non-identical (dizygotic) twins, finding a significantly higher concordance rate in identical twins, who share all their genes, than in non-identical twins, who share on average half, supporting a genetic contribution to vulnerability (though not proving it is the whole explanation, since identical twins may also share more similar environments). A **psychological explanation** emphasises **peer influence**: observing and imitating the substance use of friends/peer groups, and social pressure to fit in with a peer group's norms, can increase the likelihood of starting and continuing to use a substance. Treatments include **aversion therapy** (pairing the substance with an unpleasant response, e.g. a drug that induces nausea if alcohol is then consumed, to create a learned negative association), **self-help groups** (peer-support groups where people with the same addiction share experience and support each other, e.g. following a **12-step programme**, a structured self-help model of progressive steps towards recovery, mutual support and accountability, originating with Alcoholics Anonymous).
