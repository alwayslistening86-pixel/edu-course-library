# S05_Brain_Development_and_Piaget - Lesson: Early brain development and Piaget's stage theory

## Goal
The learner outlines early brain development and the nature-nurture debate, and describes and evaluates Piaget's stage theory of cognitive development, including assimilation, accommodation, the four stages, and the McGarrigle and Donaldson and Hughes challenges to Piaget's claims about conservation and egocentrism.

- 3.2 - Piaget's stage theory of cognitive development: assimilation/accommodation, the four stages, conservation and egocentrism (McGarrigle and Donaldson; Hughes)

## How to teach this
Ask the learner whether a toddler and a teenager would reason about the same logic puzzle in the same way -- Piaget's theory says no, because thinking itself develops through distinct stages, not just by adding more facts. Every named study or theory in this course (e.g. Murdock, Bartlett, Piaget, McGarrigle and Donaldson, Hughes, Dweck, Gibson, Gregory, Gilchrist and Nesberg, Bruner and Minturn, Asch, Milgram, Adorno, Piliavin, Von Frisch, Darwin, Hebb, Penfield, Tulving, Wiles, Kaij) is a genuine, correctly attributed piece of psychology, checked against real psychological knowledge when this course was built -- present it as real research, never as illustrative fiction, and if the learner misremembers who did a study or what it found, correct this explicitly. AQA GCSE Psychology has a substantial Research Methods strand (3.1.4, including 3.1.4.1 Data Handling): sampling methods, experimental design, types of data, and descriptive statistics (mean, median, mode, range) are examined with real calculations, not just definitions -- every numerical worked example in this course was computed and checked with Python when the course was built, and the learner should always be made to show their working for a calculation, since AQA awards credit for correct method as well as a correct final answer. Diagrams this course cannot draw (e.g. the multi-store model boxes, visual illusions, brain lobes, a neuron, a scatter diagram) are described precisely in words -- have the learner sketch or describe each one back, since being able to label a diagram accurately is itself examined.

#### 3.1 Early brain development: neural structures (brain stem, thalamus, cerebellum, cortex) and the nature-nurture debate
The brain develops from before birth through childhood, with key structures maturing in a broadly predictable sequence: the **brain stem** (controls basic survival/autonomic functions -- breathing, heart rate -- and is functional from very early on), the **thalamus** (relays sensory information -- e.g. sight, sound, touch -- to higher brain areas), the **cerebellum** (coordinates movement and balance), and finally the **cortex** (the outer layer responsible for higher-order **cognition** -- thinking, reasoning, language, decision-making -- which continues developing well into adolescence and early adulthood). Early brain development supports increasingly complex **autonomic** (automatic body regulation), **sensory** (processing what is seen/heard/felt), **movement** and **cognitive** functions as the relevant structures mature. This raises the **nature-nurture debate**: how much of development is due to **nature** (innate, genetically programmed biological maturation of these brain structures) versus **nurture** (the effect of experience, environment, learning and upbringing on how the brain develops) -- most psychologists now see brain development as an interaction of both, where genetically programmed structures still need environmental stimulation and experience to develop typically.

#### 3.2 Piaget's stage theory of cognitive development: assimilation/accommodation, the four stages, conservation and egocentrism (McGarrigle and Donaldson; Hughes)
**Piaget's stage theory** argues that children's thinking develops through four universal, age-related stages, driven by two complementary processes: **assimilation** (fitting new information into an existing mental structure/schema without changing the schema) and **accommodation** (changing an existing schema, or creating a new one, because new information does not fit). The four stages are the **sensorimotor stage** (roughly birth to 2 years -- the infant understands the world mainly through senses and physical actions, and develops **object permanence**, understanding that objects still exist even when out of sight), the **pre-operational stage** (roughly 2 to 7 years -- language and symbolic thought develop, but the child's thinking is **egocentric** (struggling to see things from another person's point of view) and typically fails tests of **conservation**, the understanding that a quantity stays the same even when its appearance changes, e.g. wrongly believing a tall, thin glass holds more liquid than a short, wide glass that in fact holds the same amount), the **concrete operational stage** (roughly 7 to 11 years -- logical thinking about concrete, real objects and situations develops, egocentrism reduces, and conservation is typically mastered), and the **formal operational stage** (roughly 11 years and over -- abstract, hypothetical and systematic logical reasoning becomes possible). Piaget's theory has clear **educational applications**: teaching should be matched to a child's current stage, and 'discovery learning' (letting children actively explore, so assimilation and accommodation can occur) is favoured over simply being told facts. However, later research challenged how early Piaget's claimed limits actually appear. **McGarrigle and Donaldson's 'naughty teddy' study** repeated a standard conservation task, but this time a 'naughty teddy' puppet, rather than the researcher, appeared to accidentally knock over and rearrange the counters/liquid -- with this more meaningful, less confusing context, significantly more young children correctly conserved than in Piaget's original procedure, suggesting Piaget's method (the adult deliberately and visibly changing the arrangement) may itself have confused children rather than testing conservation cleanly. **Hughes' 'policeman doll' study** used a task requiring the child to hide a doll from two toy policemen's lines of sight (rather than Piaget's own 'three mountains' task); children as young as three and a half years old could often complete this correctly, suggesting egocentrism is not as fixed a limitation of the pre-operational stage as Piaget claimed, and that children can take another's viewpoint when the task is made meaningful to them.
