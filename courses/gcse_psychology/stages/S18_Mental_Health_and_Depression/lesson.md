# S18_Mental_Health_and_Depression - Lesson: Introduction to mental health and clinical depression

## Goal
The learner describes characteristics of mental health, cultural variation, and its effects on the individual and society, and explains clinical depression, including ICD diagnostic criteria, biological and psychological explanations, and antidepressant/CBT treatments, citing the Wiles CBT effectiveness study.

## Syllabus items taught here

- 8.2 - Clinical depression: unipolar/bipolar distinction and ICD criteria; biological and psychological explanations; treatments including antidepressants and CBT (Wiles' study)

## How to teach this
Ask the learner what 'good mental health' means to them, then note that psychologists' definitions also consider how ideas about mental health can vary between cultures and over time. Every named study or theory in this course (e.g. Murdock, Bartlett, Piaget, McGarrigle and Donaldson, Hughes, Dweck, Gibson, Gregory, Gilchrist and Nesberg, Bruner and Minturn, Asch, Milgram, Adorno, Piliavin, Von Frisch, Darwin, Hebb, Penfield, Tulving, Wiles, Kaij) is a genuine, correctly attributed piece of psychology, checked against real psychological knowledge when this course was built -- present it as real research, never as illustrative fiction, and if the learner misremembers who did a study or what it found, correct this explicitly. AQA GCSE Psychology has a substantial Research Methods strand (3.1.4, including 3.1.4.1 Data Handling): sampling methods, experimental design, types of data, and descriptive statistics (mean, median, mode, range) are examined with real calculations, not just definitions -- every numerical worked example in this course was computed and checked with Python when the course was built, and the learner should always be made to show their working for a calculation, since AQA awards credit for correct method as well as a correct final answer. Diagrams this course cannot draw (e.g. the multi-store model boxes, visual illusions, brain lobes, a neuron, a scatter diagram) are described precisely in words -- have the learner sketch or describe each one back, since being able to label a diagram accurately is itself examined.

#### 8.1 Introduction to mental health: characteristics of mental health, cultural variation, and the effects of poor mental health on the individual and society
**Mental health** refers to a person's emotional, psychological and social wellbeing, affecting how they think, feel and cope with everyday life; it exists on a continuum rather than being simply 'present' or 'absent'. Ideas about what counts as good mental health, and which experiences count as a mental health problem, show **cultural variation** -- norms and expectations for behaviour and emotional expression differ between cultures, which can affect both how a difficulty is experienced and whether/how it is diagnosed. Various aspects of **modern living** (e.g. social media use, workplace pressures, social isolation) have been suggested as factors affecting the incidence of some mental health difficulties, alongside a gradually **reduced social stigma** and increased awareness/willingness to discuss mental health openly in many societies, which affects how often difficulties are reported and diagnosed rather than necessarily how often they occur. Poor mental health can affect the **individual** (e.g. straining personal relationships, and affecting physical wellbeing through effects such as disrupted sleep or appetite) and **society** (e.g. increased demand for social care and health services, links with some crime, and economic costs such as reduced ability to work).

#### 8.2 Clinical depression: unipolar/bipolar distinction and ICD criteria; biological and psychological explanations; treatments including antidepressants and CBT (Wiles' study)
**Clinical depression** is distinguished from ordinary, temporary sadness by its severity, duration and impact on daily functioning; it is classed as **unipolar** (persistently low mood only) or **bipolar** (episodes of low mood alternating with episodes of unusually elevated mood/mania). The **ICD** (International Classification of Diseases) sets out diagnostic criteria for depression, including persistently low **mood**, low **energy**, disturbed **sleep** (too much or too little), changed **appetite**, and reduced **self-confidence**/self-worth, present most of the time for a sustained period. Two types of explanation are given for depression: a **biological explanation** proposes an imbalance of certain **neurotransmitters** (chemical messengers in the brain), particularly low levels of **serotonin**, contributing to low mood; a **psychological explanation** proposes that **negative schemas** (deeply held, overly negative patterns of belief about the self, the world and the future) and negative **attributions** (habitually explaining negative events as one's own fault, permanent and affecting everything, rather than as situational and temporary) make a person more vulnerable to becoming and remaining depressed. Treatments follow from these explanations: **antidepressant medication** (targeting the proposed neurotransmitter imbalance, e.g. increasing available serotonin) and **CBT (cognitive behavioural therapy)** (targeting the proposed negative schemas/attributions, helping a person identify and challenge unhelpful thought patterns and change related behaviour). **Wiles et al.'s** clinical trial investigated adding CBT to usual antidepressant treatment for patients whose depression had not responded well to medication alone, and found that those who received CBT alongside their usual treatment showed significantly better improvement in depression symptoms than those who continued with usual treatment alone, supporting CBT's effectiveness as a treatment for depression.
