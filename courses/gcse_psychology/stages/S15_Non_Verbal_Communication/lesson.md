# S15_Non_Verbal_Communication - Lesson: Non-verbal communication and its explanations

## Goal
The learner explains the functions of eye contact, types of body language and personal space, and compares Darwin's evolutionary explanation of non-verbal behaviour with evidence for innate and learned non-verbal behaviour, citing Yuki's emoticon study.

## Syllabus items taught here
- 6.3 - Non-verbal communication: eye contact, body language (posture, postural echo, touch) and personal space
- 6.4 - Explanations of non-verbal behaviour: Darwin's evolutionary account, innate and learned evidence (Yuki's emoticon study)

## How to teach this
Ask the learner to notice, next time they talk to someone, how much meaning is carried by tone, posture and eye contact rather than the actual words used -- psychologists study exactly this 'non-verbal' channel of communication. Every named study or theory in this course (e.g. Murdock, Bartlett, Piaget, McGarrigle and Donaldson, Hughes, Dweck, Gibson, Gregory, Gilchrist and Nesberg, Bruner and Minturn, Asch, Milgram, Adorno, Piliavin, Von Frisch, Darwin, Hebb, Penfield, Tulving, Wiles, Kaij) is a genuine, correctly attributed piece of psychology, checked against real psychological knowledge when this course was built -- present it as real research, never as illustrative fiction, and if the learner misremembers who did a study or what it found, correct this explicitly. AQA GCSE Psychology has a substantial Research Methods strand (3.1.4, including 3.1.4.1 Data Handling): sampling methods, experimental design, types of data, and descriptive statistics (mean, median, mode, range) are examined with real calculations, not just definitions -- every numerical worked example in this course was computed and checked with Python when the course was built, and the learner should always be made to show their working for a calculation, since AQA awards credit for correct method as well as a correct final answer. Diagrams this course cannot draw (e.g. the multi-store model boxes, visual illusions, brain lobes, a neuron, a scatter diagram) are described precisely in words -- have the learner sketch or describe each one back, since being able to label a diagram accurately is itself examined.

#### 6.3 Non-verbal communication: eye contact, body language (posture, postural echo, touch) and personal space
**Non-verbal communication (NVC)** is communication without words, through body-based signals. **Eye contact** serves several functions: **regulating the flow of conversation** (e.g. looking away can signal it is the other person's turn to speak, sustained eye contact can signal a wish to continue), **signalling attraction** (increased eye contact/pupil dilation towards someone a person is attracted to) and **expressing emotion** (e.g. a hard, prolonged stare can signal aggression or intimidation). **Body language** includes **posture** (open postures, e.g. uncrossed arms and facing the other person, tend to signal friendliness/interest, while closed postures, e.g. crossed arms, tend to signal defensiveness/disinterest), **postural echo** (unconsciously mirroring another person's posture, generally signalling rapport, agreement or that a conversation is going well) and **touch** (varies hugely by relationship and culture, from a formal handshake to intimate contact, and can signal warmth, dominance, comfort or aggression depending on the context). **Personal space** (the physical distance a person prefers to keep between themselves and others) varies with both **culture** (some cultures typically maintain closer conversational distances than others) and **gender** (some research finds different typical preferred distances, and different comfort with touch, between and within genders), and being too close for the expected personal space in that culture/relationship tends to cause discomfort.

#### 6.4 Explanations of non-verbal behaviour: Darwin's evolutionary account, innate and learned evidence (Yuki's emoticon study)
Two broad explanations account for non-verbal behaviour. **Darwin's evolutionary theory** (from *The Expression of the Emotions in Man and Animals*, 1872) argued that many basic non-verbal expressions of emotion (e.g. a smile for happiness, a snarl-like expression for anger/disgust) are **innate** -- evolved, biologically inherited signals, useful for survival/social communication, and therefore universal across human cultures rather than something each culture has to separately learn. Evidence supporting an innate basis includes similar facial expressions being observed in **neonates** (newborn babies, who have had essentially no opportunity to learn expressions by watching others) and in individuals who are **congenitally blind** (born without sight, and so cannot have learned facial expressions by visually copying others), both of whom still show recognisable versions of basic emotional expressions. However, other non-verbal behaviour is clearly **learned** through culture and experience rather than innate, and can therefore differ between cultures: **Yuki et al.'s** study of **emoticons** found that people from different cultures emphasise different facial features when interpreting emotion -- participants from Japan tended to focus more on the eyes when judging an emoticon's emotion (consistent with Japanese emoticons that vary mainly in the eyes, e.g. ^_^ ), while American participants tended to focus more on the mouth (consistent with Western emoticons that vary mainly in the mouth, e.g. :) vs :( ), reflecting learned, culturally specific display and interpretation rules layered on top of any innate emotional expression.
