# S02_Reconstructive_Memory - Lesson: Memory as an active, reconstructive process

## Goal
The learner explains the theory of reconstructive memory (including 'effort after meaning'), describes Bartlett's research, and explains factors (schemas, interference, context, leading information) that can make memory inaccurate.

## Syllabus items taught here
- 1.3 - Memory as an active process: the theory of reconstructive memory, effort after meaning (Bartlett), factors affecting accuracy

## How to teach this
Ask the learner to describe a memorable family event from several years ago in detail, then ask whether they can be certain every detail is exactly accurate rather than filled in later -- memory researchers argue it usually is not a perfect recording. Every named study or theory in this course (e.g. Murdock, Bartlett, Piaget, McGarrigle and Donaldson, Hughes, Dweck, Gibson, Gregory, Gilchrist and Nesberg, Bruner and Minturn, Asch, Milgram, Adorno, Piliavin, Von Frisch, Darwin, Hebb, Penfield, Tulving, Wiles, Kaij) is a genuine, correctly attributed piece of psychology, checked against real psychological knowledge when this course was built -- present it as real research, never as illustrative fiction, and if the learner misremembers who did a study or what it found, correct this explicitly. AQA GCSE Psychology has a substantial Research Methods strand (3.1.4, including 3.1.4.1 Data Handling): sampling methods, experimental design, types of data, and descriptive statistics (mean, median, mode, range) are examined with real calculations, not just definitions -- every numerical worked example in this course was computed and checked with Python when the course was built, and the learner should always be made to show their working for a calculation, since AQA awards credit for correct method as well as a correct final answer. Diagrams this course cannot draw (e.g. the multi-store model boxes, visual illusions, brain lobes, a neuron, a scatter diagram) are described precisely in words -- have the learner sketch or describe each one back, since being able to label a diagram accurately is itself examined.

#### 1.3 Memory as an active process: the theory of reconstructive memory, effort after meaning (Bartlett), factors affecting accuracy
The **theory of reconstructive memory**, developed by **Bartlett**, argues that memory is not a passive, accurate recording of events (like a video), but is **actively reconstructed** each time it is recalled, using existing knowledge structures called **schemas** (organised packets of knowledge and expectation about the world, built from past experience). When recalling something that does not quite fit an existing schema, people show **'effort after meaning'** -- they unconsciously distort, add to, or leave out details of the memory to make it fit better with what they already expect, producing a memory that feels accurate but may not be. In his classic study, **Bartlett (1932)** asked British participants to read an unfamiliar Native American folk tale, *'The War of the Ghosts'*, and recall it repeatedly over time. Participants' recollections became shorter, more conventional, and were distorted to fit more closely with their own cultural schemas -- unfamiliar details were changed into more familiar ones (e.g. 'canoes' sometimes became 'boats'), and the story was rationalised to make more sense to a British reader, supporting the idea that recall is a reconstruction shaped by existing schemas rather than a faithful replay. Several factors can reduce the accuracy of memory: **interference** (other memories, especially similar ones learned before or after, disrupting recall), **context** (recall tends to be more accurate when the context at retrieval matches the context at encoding), and **false memories** (a person confidently recalling an event, or a detail, that did not actually happen -- often caused by leading questions or misleading post-event information being incorporated into the original memory during reconstruction).
