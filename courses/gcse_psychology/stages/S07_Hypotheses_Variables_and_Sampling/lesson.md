# S07_Hypotheses_Variables_and_Sampling - Lesson: Research methods: hypotheses, variables and sampling

## Goal
The learner writes null and alternative hypotheses correctly, identifies independent, dependent and extraneous variables in a study, and explains the four sampling methods (random, opportunity, systematic, stratified) with a strength and weakness of each.

## Syllabus items taught here
- 4.1 - Formulating hypotheses (null and alternative) and types of variable (independent, dependent, extraneous)
- 4.2 - Sampling methods: random, opportunity, systematic and stratified, with strengths and weaknesses

## How to teach this
Ask the learner to design, in one sentence, a study testing whether revising with flashcards improves test scores compared with re-reading notes -- then ask them to name what they would change (the IV) and what they would measure (the DV). Every named study or theory in this course (e.g. Murdock, Bartlett, Piaget, McGarrigle and Donaldson, Hughes, Dweck, Gibson, Gregory, Gilchrist and Nesberg, Bruner and Minturn, Asch, Milgram, Adorno, Piliavin, Von Frisch, Darwin, Hebb, Penfield, Tulving, Wiles, Kaij) is a genuine, correctly attributed piece of psychology, checked against real psychological knowledge when this course was built -- present it as real research, never as illustrative fiction, and if the learner misremembers who did a study or what it found, correct this explicitly. AQA GCSE Psychology has a substantial Research Methods strand (3.1.4, including 3.1.4.1 Data Handling): sampling methods, experimental design, types of data, and descriptive statistics (mean, median, mode, range) are examined with real calculations, not just definitions -- every numerical worked example in this course was computed and checked with Python when the course was built, and the learner should always be made to show their working for a calculation, since AQA awards credit for correct method as well as a correct final answer. Diagrams this course cannot draw (e.g. the multi-store model boxes, visual illusions, brain lobes, a neuron, a scatter diagram) are described precisely in words -- have the learner sketch or describe each one back, since being able to label a diagram accurately is itself examined.

#### 4.1 Formulating hypotheses (null and alternative) and types of variable (independent, dependent, extraneous)
A **hypothesis** is a testable, precise statement predicting the outcome of a study. The **null hypothesis** states that there will be **no difference/relationship** between the variables being studied (beyond what would be expected by chance) -- it is the 'default' position a study sets out to test against. The **alternative hypothesis** states that there **will be** a difference or relationship, and can be **directional** (predicting the specific direction of the difference, e.g. 'participants will recall significantly more words in silence than with music playing') or **non-directional** (predicting only that a difference will exist, without saying which way, e.g. 'there will be a significant difference in words recalled between the silence and music conditions'). Every study also involves types of **variable**: the **independent variable (IV)** is the factor the researcher deliberately changes or selects between conditions (e.g. whether music is playing or not); the **dependent variable (DV)** is the factor that is measured, to see if it was affected by the IV (e.g. the number of words correctly recalled); and an **extraneous variable** is any other factor, not the IV, that could affect the DV if not controlled (e.g. time of day, participants' prior familiarity with the word list, or room temperature) -- if an extraneous variable is not controlled and does affect the DV, it becomes a **confounding variable**, making the results impossible to interpret with confidence.

#### 4.2 Sampling methods: random, opportunity, systematic and stratified, with strengths and weaknesses
**Sampling** is the process of selecting a **sample** (the group of participants actually studied) from a **target population** (the whole group the researcher wants to draw conclusions about). Four sampling methods are commonly used: **random sampling** (every member of the target population has an equal chance of being selected, e.g. by drawing names from a hat or using a random number generator) -- *strength:* free from researcher bias in who is chosen; *weakness:* can still by chance produce an unrepresentative sample, and is often impractical for a large population. **Opportunity sampling** (selecting whoever is conveniently available and willing to take part at the time, e.g. asking people passing by) -- *strength:* quick, easy and cheap; *weakness:* likely to be unrepresentative/biased, since it only draws from people available in that particular place and time. **Systematic sampling** (selecting every nth person from a full list of the target population, e.g. every 10th name) -- *strength:* generally free from researcher bias, since the selection rule is fixed in advance; *weakness:* can still produce an unrepresentative sample if there is a hidden pattern in the list that lines up with the chosen interval. **Stratified sampling** (identifying the important sub-groups, or 'strata', within the target population, e.g. by age or gender, and then randomly selecting participants from each sub-group in proportion to its size in the population) -- *strength:* produces a sample that closely reflects the composition of the target population, improving generalisability; *weakness:* takes more time and effort to identify the strata and sample proportionally, and still cannot guarantee a perfectly representative sample.
