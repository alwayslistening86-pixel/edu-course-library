# S17_B2_Grammar_Clauses_Register - Lesson: Complex clauses, the vocative case and formal register

## Goal
The learner builds concessive and purpose clauses, uses the vocative case appropriately, reports speech with full tense-shift rules, and uses formal nominal case forms.

## Syllabus items taught here
- RO-B2-G5 - Concessive and purpose clauses: learn and apply: concessive and purpose clauses
- RO-B2-G6 - The vocative case and address register: learn and apply: the vocative case and address register
- RO-B2-G7 - Reported speech: full tense-shift rules: learn and apply: reported speech, full tense-shift rules
- RO-B2-G8 - Formal genitive-dative and nominal case in register: learn and apply: formal genitive-dative and nominal case in register

## How to teach this
Ask the learner to write a short formal email addressing someone directly by name/title, explaining a purpose despite an obstacle, and reporting what someone else said in the past. This single task touches all four grammar points.

#### RO-B2-G5 Concessive and purpose clauses
Concessive (although/even though): deși, cu toate că + indicative: "Deși era obosit, a terminat proiectul." (Although he was tired, he finished the project.) Purpose (so that/in order to): ca să, pentru ca... să + subjunctive: "A plecat devreme ca să prindă trenul." (He left early to catch the train.) "I-am scris pentru ca să înțeleagă situația." (I wrote to him so that he would understand the situation.) These give B2 writing real argumentative texture beyond the simple "pentru că" (because) of B1.

#### RO-B2-G6 The vocative case and address register
Romanian retains a distinct vocative case, unlike most other Romance languages, used to address someone directly. Common patterns: masculine names often add -e or -ule (Ion -> Ioane; Radu -> Radule, informal/affectionate); feminine names often add -o (Ana -> Ano, though this can sound archaic/dialectal and is often avoided with modern names, which stay unchanged: Maria -> Maria); common nouns: domnule (sir, from domn), doamnă/doamnă! (madam, often unchanged or with vocative doamnă intact), băiete (boy!, from băiat), fetițo (little girl!, affectionate). Formal address commonly uses "Domnule profesor," "Doamnă doctor," in letters and speech, treated as fixed vocative-register phrases.

#### RO-B2-G7 Reported speech: full tense-shift rules
When the reporting verb is in the past, tenses shift back: present -> imperfect ("Sunt obosit" -> "A spus că este/era obosit"), perfect compus -> pluperfect ("Am plecat" -> "A spus că plecase"), viitor -> conditional ("Voi veni" -> "A spus că va veni/ar veni"). Romanian is more flexible than English here and often keeps the original tense if the statement is still true ("A spus că vine mâine" is acceptable even reported later), but formal/written style favours full backshifting, which B2 learners should be able to produce on request.

#### RO-B2-G8 Formal genitive-dative and nominal case in register
In formal and written Romanian, genitive-dative case endings on nouns (rather than the al/a/ai/ale + noun workaround from B1) mark ownership and indirect objects precisely: "opinia profesorului" (the teacher's opinion, genitive -ui ending), "i-am dat cărții o nouă copertă" is awkward; more natural: "am dat o carte studentului" (I gave a book to the student, dative -ui ending on masculine nouns), feminine: "am dat o carte studentei" (-ei ending). Formal register favours these direct case endings over paraphrase, and B2 learners should recognise and produce them in writing.

## Explicitly not here
Nuanced discourse connectors, idiom and full stylistic control are C1 material.
