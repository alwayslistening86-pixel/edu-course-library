# FHS_Practical_Ethics - Lesson: Practical Ethics (Paper 128)

## How to teach this
Open with the classic pair: "A trolley is heading toward five people; you can divert it onto a track with one person instead. Most people say pull the lever. Now imagine the only way to stop the trolley is to push a large stranger off a bridge into its path - same numbers, same outcome, but most people say don't push. If the outcome is identical, what's actually doing the moral work in making these feel so different?" Use the learner's own answer to introduce doing/allowing and intention (P128.10) before any applied topic, since the paper wants these concepts used as tools throughout, not treated as a standalone topic.

#### P128.1 War and self-defence (just war theory)
Just war theory splits the ethics of war into jus ad bellum (when going to war is justified - just cause, legitimate authority, last resort, proportionality) and jus in bello (what conduct within war is justified - discrimination between combatants and non-combatants, proportionality of specific attacks), which can come apart: a state might have just cause to fight but still act wrongly in how it fights, or vice versa. Individual self-defence provides the paradigm case underlying much of the theory: harming an attacker is justified because they forfeit some of their normal protection against harm by posing an unjust threat. Teach the learner to keep jus ad bellum and jus in bello analytically separate, since conflating them is a common and heavily penalised error.

#### P128.2 Treatment of animals
The paper asks whether moral status depends on species membership itself (a "speciesist" position, treated by many contemporary philosophers as an unjustified prejudice analogous to racism or sexism) or on morally relevant capacities - sentience, the capacity to suffer, rationality, self-awareness - that many non-human animals plainly possess to some degree. If moral status tracks capacities rather than species, it follows that similar capacities across species warrant similar moral consideration ("equal consideration of interests"), with major implications for factory farming and animal experimentation. Teach the learner to press on the "marginal cases" argument: any capacity-based criterion that excludes animals also risks excluding some humans (infants, the severely cognitively impaired) who lack that capacity too.

#### P128.3 Obligations to future generations
Intergenerational ethics asks what obligations, if any, the present generation owes to people who do not yet exist - relevant to climate change, resource depletion and long-term policy. This raises the genuinely hard non-identity problem: many policy choices affecting the future also determine which particular future people come to exist at all, so it is not obvious that a worse policy actually harms any specific future person (since a different policy would mean different people existing), even though the resulting state of the world is clearly worse. Teach the learner why this makes intergenerational ethics harder than ordinary harm-based reasoning, and why some philosophers move to impersonal, outcome-based principles instead of strict harm-to-a-person principles.

#### P128.4 Punishment
Retributivist justifications hold punishment is deserved because of the wrongdoing itself - proportionate suffering is intrinsically appropriate as a response to wrongdoing, independent of any further good it produces. Consequentialist justifications hold punishment is justified only by its effects - deterrence of future crime, incapacitation, rehabilitation, or protection of the public - with no independent weight given to desert as such. These generate genuinely different implications for how much and what kind of punishment is justified: a strict consequentialist could in principle justify punishing an innocent person if doing so produced sufficient deterrent benefit, which most people take to be a serious problem for pure consequentialism about punishment. Teach the learner to test each theory against this "punishing the innocent" objection directly.

#### P128.5 Abortion
Abortion debates typically turn on two separable questions: the moral status of the foetus (does it have the same right to life as a born person, and if so from what point in development), and, even granting full moral status, whether a right to life entails a right to the use of another person's body to sustain it (Judith Jarvis Thomson's violinist analogy argues it does not automatically). Teach the learner to keep these two questions distinct - a strong answer does not collapse "does the foetus have moral status" into "is abortion therefore impermissible," since the second question about bodily autonomy remains live even if the first is answered in the foetus's favour.

#### P128.6 Euthanasia
Euthanasia debates distinguish voluntary (at the competent patient's request), non-voluntary (the patient cannot express a preference) and involuntary (against the patient's wishes) cases, and active (directly causing death) from passive (withholding or withdrawing treatment, allowing death) means - connecting directly to the doing/allowing distinction (P128.10). Arguments for permitting voluntary euthanasia typically appeal to autonomy and the relief of suffering; arguments against often appeal to the sanctity of life, worries about a "slippery slope" to non-voluntary cases, or worries about pressure on vulnerable patients. Teach the learner to assess whether the active/passive distinction is doing real moral work here or is, as some philosophers argue, a difference without a genuine moral difference.

#### P128.7 Charitable giving
This topic asks how much, if anything, the relatively affluent are obligated (not merely encouraged) to give to prevent severe suffering elsewhere - Peter Singer's drowning-child argument contends that if we can prevent something very bad (a child drowning, or dying of a preventable disease) at comparatively little cost to ourselves, we are morally required to do so, and that physical distance from the person in need is not morally relevant to that obligation. Objections include the demandingness objection (this would require an implausibly large sacrifice of one's own projects and relationships) and questions about whether proximity, special relationships or causal responsibility actually do carry moral weight the argument denies them. Teach the learner to assess the drowning-child analogy's disanalogies as carefully as its analogy.

#### P128.8 Commodification of bodies and bodily organs
This topic asks whether markets in bodies or body parts (organ sales, commercial surrogacy, prostitution) are morally objectionable in principle, or only contingently problematic because of exploitation, coercion or unequal bargaining power that could in principle be regulated away. Some arguments hold certain goods are simply not the kind of thing that should be for sale (a "corruption" or "commodification" objection, independent of consent or exploitation); others hold that if a transaction is genuinely informed and voluntary, prohibiting it is itself paternalistic and may cause more harm than it prevents. Teach the learner to distinguish objections from exploitation (fixable by regulation) from objections to the market form itself (not fixable by regulation).

#### P128.9 Disability, racial and gender equality
This topic examines whether equal moral status and equal treatment are the same thing, and what follows for policy when they are not - for example, whether genuine equality sometimes requires different treatment (accommodations for disability, affirmative action addressing historical racial or gender disadvantage) rather than strictly identical treatment. It connects to P128.2's question of what grounds moral status: if capacities rather than group membership ground status, this has implications for how disability, race and gender are treated as categories in ethical and political argument, including scrutiny of when a category itself is doing illegitimate moral work. Teach the learner to distinguish equal moral status (a baseline claim) from equal treatment (a policy question that may or may not follow from it).

#### P128.10 Connecting concepts: doing/allowing harm, double effect, badness of death
A recurring thread across this paper: does it matter, morally, whether harm is caused versus merely allowed, and whether it is intended versus a foreseen but unintended side-effect? The doctrine of double effect (with roots in Aquinas, see P215.3) formalises this: an act with both good and bad effects may be permissible if the bad effect is foreseen but not intended as a means, even where it would not be permissible to intend that same harm directly. Separately, the paper asks why death is bad at all - a "deprivation account" says death is bad because it deprives someone of a future they would otherwise have had - and whether that account applies equally, more, or less to a foetus (P128.5) versus a terminally ill adult choosing euthanasia (P128.6). Teach the learner to treat these as genuine tools to deploy across topics, not as one isolated essay question.
