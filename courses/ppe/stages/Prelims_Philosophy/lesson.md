## How to teach this
Open with: "'If it's raining, the ground is wet. The ground is wet. Therefore, it's raining.' Something is wrong with this argument even though both premises could easily be true - what, exactly, and why does it matter for how you should evaluate any argument, not just this one?" Use the learner's own diagnosis to introduce validity before naming the fallacy.

#### PHIP.L1 Propositional logic: validity, soundness, truth tables
An argument is valid when the conclusion follows necessarily from the premises, regardless of whether the premises are actually true; it is sound when it is valid and the premises are in fact true. The opening example commits affirming the consequent: from "if P then Q" and "Q," one cannot validly infer "P," because the ground could be wet for reasons other than rain. Propositional logic (following Halbach's The Logic Manual, the paper's set text) formalises connectives (and, or, not, if-then) and uses truth tables to mechanically check whether an argument form is valid for every possible assignment of truth values to its components.

#### PHIP.L2 Predicate logic and inference patterns in ordinary language
Predicate logic extends propositional logic by symbolising the internal structure of statements - quantifiers ("all," "some"), predicates and individual terms - which lets it capture valid inferences that propositional logic cannot, such as "all philosophers are curious; Socrates is a philosopher; therefore Socrates is curious." The paper's core skill is translating an ordinary-language argument into symbolic form so its logical structure, and hence its validity, becomes visible - training that underwrites how the learner should read and evaluate every other philosophical argument in the paper, not just formal logic exercises.

#### PHIP.G1 Knowledge and scepticism
The classical analysis of knowledge as justified true belief looks intuitive until Gettier's counterexamples show a belief can be justified and true by luck rather than by genuinely tracking the fact - so justified true belief is not sufficient for knowledge. External-world scepticism pushes further, asking whether you can ever rule out radical deception (a vivid dream, an evil demon, a simulated reality) about the external world at all, and if not, whether you can be said to know anything about it. Teach the learner to distinguish "I cannot be certain" from "I do not know," since most theories of knowledge do not require certainty.

#### PHIP.G2 Induction
Inductive reasoning draws general conclusions from particular observed instances (the sun has risen every day so far, so it will rise tomorrow), but Hume's problem of induction asks what justifies the further assumption that unobserved cases resemble observed ones. Any attempt to justify induction by appeal to its past success is itself an inductive argument, so it cannot non-circularly justify induction as a method. Teach the learner why this is a genuine philosophical problem rather than a merely practical one: virtually all of empirical science relies on inductive inference, so a good answer must engage with proposed responses (regularity of nature, probabilistic justification), not just restate the puzzle.

#### PHIP.G3 Mind and body
Substance dualism (Descartes) holds mind and body are two distinct kinds of substance, raising the interaction problem: how could a non-physical mind causally affect a physical body, and vice versa? Physicalist alternatives hold that mental states are identical to, or realised by, physical brain states, avoiding the interaction problem but facing their own challenge: explaining subjective conscious experience (why there is "something it is like" to be in pain) in purely physical terms. Teach the learner to state each position's central motivation and its strongest objection, rather than simply reporting that "some philosophers think X."

#### PHIP.G4 Personal identity
What makes a person at one time the same person as someone at an earlier or later time? Bodily/physical continuity theories tie identity to continuity of the same physical organism; psychological continuity theories (Locke, and later Parfit) tie it to overlapping chains of memory, character and intention, generating puzzle cases (teleportation, brain splitting, gradual replacement) where the two criteria can come apart. Teach the learner to use such thought experiments properly: not as party tricks, but as a way of testing which criterion best matches our considered judgements about when someone has, or has not, survived.

#### PHIP.G5 Free will
Determinism holds that every event, including human choices, is the inevitable result of prior causes plus the laws of nature. Hard determinists conclude genuine free will and moral responsibility are illusions; libertarians (about free will) deny determinism is true of human choice; compatibilists argue free will is compatible with determinism once "free" is properly understood - typically as acting according to one's own desires and reasons, without external coercion, regardless of whether those desires were themselves causally determined. Teach the learner that the live debate is mostly about which conception of "free" is the one that actually matters for responsibility, not simply "is determinism true."

#### PHIP.G6 God and evil
The problem of evil argues that the existence of a God who is omnipotent, omniscient and wholly good is in tension with the amount and kind of suffering in the world: if God could prevent evil, would know about it, and would want to prevent it, why does it occur? Theodicies attempt to resolve the tension (the free-will defence: evil is the price of genuine moral freedom; soul-making theodicies: suffering enables morally valuable growth), while classical arguments for God's existence (cosmological, teleological, ontological) are assessed for whether they succeed independently of the problem of evil. Teach the learner to state the argument as a genuine inconsistency claim, not merely as an emotional objection.

#### PHIP.M1 Happiness, pleasure and the criterion of right action (Mill's Utilitarianism)
Mill's Utilitarianism, the paper's set text, argues the right action is the one that produces the greatest happiness (pleasure and the absence of pain) for the greatest number - the "greatest happiness principle." Mill departs from Bentham's purely quantitative hedonism by distinguishing higher (intellectual, moral) and lower (bodily) pleasures qualitatively, arguing that a being capable of higher pleasures would not trade them for any quantity of lower ones. Teach the learner Mill's own "proof": that the only evidence something is desirable is that people actually desire it, and that happiness is what all people ultimately desire - then have them assess whether this argument actually establishes what Mill needs it to.

#### PHIP.M2 Foundation of moral principles and justice, and contemporary utilitarianism
Utilitarianism faces classic objections: it can seemingly require sacrificing an innocent person if that maximises aggregate happiness, and it seems to demand impartial calculation in cases (promises, special obligations to family) where partiality feels morally required. Mill's own chapter on justice tries to accommodate rule-like moral principles (promise-keeping, rights) as themselves justified by their long-run utility rather than competing with utilitarianism. Contemporary versions (rule utilitarianism, preference utilitarianism) attempt to answer these objections while keeping consequentialism's core structural commitment - that rightness is ultimately about outcomes. Teach the learner to weigh whether these moves rescue utilitarianism or merely relocate the objection.
