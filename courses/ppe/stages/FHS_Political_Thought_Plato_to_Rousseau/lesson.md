# FHS_Political_Thought_Plato_to_Rousseau - Lesson: Political Thought - Plato to Rousseau (Paper 215)

## How to teach this
Open with: "Hobbes argues that without a powerful sovereign, life is 'solitary, poor, nasty, brutish, and short' - so rational people should submit to near-absolute authority to escape it. Locke, writing decades later and drawing on some of the same premises about a pre-political 'state of nature,' concludes almost the opposite: that legitimate government is limited, conditional, and can be resisted if it violates natural rights. How can such similar starting points produce such different politics?" Keep this contrast live throughout: the paper wants the learner to explain divergence, not just summarise each thinker in isolation.

#### P215.1 Plato, Republic
The Republic argues justice in the soul (a harmonious relation between reason, spirit and appetite) mirrors justice in the state, and that a properly ordered city should be ruled by philosopher-kings - those who have grasped the Form of the Good through education, rather than by popular consent or hereditary right. Plato is deeply sceptical of democracy, seeing it as prone to flattery and instability, rule by whoever is most persuasive rather than most wise. Teach the learner Plato's analogy structure (soul-state) and have them assess whether the analogy actually supports the political conclusion Plato draws from it.

#### P215.2 Aristotle, Politics
Aristotle's Politics takes a more empirical, comparative approach than Plato: examining actual historical constitutions, arguing humans are naturally political animals (zoon politikon) who can only flourish within a polis, and that the best regime depends on a city's actual circumstances (size, culture, economic base) rather than one ideal form fitting all cities. Aristotle's mixed-constitution ideal (blending oligarchic and democratic elements) reflects this practical, comparative method. Teach the learner to contrast Aristotle's bottom-up, empirical method with Plato's top-down, ideal-theoretic method as two competing ways of doing political philosophy.

#### P215.3 Aquinas, Political Writings
Aquinas synthesises Aristotelian political naturalism with Christian natural law theory: political authority is natural and necessary for human flourishing (following Aristotle), but is also bound by a higher natural law - itself derived from eternal, divine law - that sets limits on what any human ruler may legitimately command. An unjust law, for Aquinas, is not really law at all in the fullest sense and may not bind in conscience, though Aquinas is cautious about when disobedience or resistance is actually justified. Teach the learner how this natural-law framework anticipates, and differs from, the later social-contract tradition's account of limits on legitimate authority (see P215.5-P215.9).

#### P215.4 Machiavelli, The Prince and Discourses
The Prince and Discourses are often read together as showing two faces of the same realist project: The Prince examines how a ruler actually keeps power, frequently requiring the appearance rather than the substance of conventional virtue ("it is better to be feared than loved, if one cannot be both"), while the Discourses examines how a republic sustains liberty through institutional checks and civic virtue among its citizens. Teach the learner this tension between princely and republican modes of political survival, and have them assess whether Machiavelli is best read as amoral, as a civic republican, or as offering genuinely separate advice for two different kinds of regime.

#### P215.5 Hobbes, Leviathan Parts I-II
Hobbes's state of nature (Leviathan Part I) is a war of all against all, driven by rough equality of power, scarcity and mutual fear, making life without government intolerable regardless of individual virtue. Part II derives the social contract: individuals mutually and simultaneously transfer their natural right of self-government to a sovereign, who alone can guarantee peace - and because the alternative (the state of nature) is so bad, Hobbes concludes the sovereign's authority should be close to absolute and largely unconditional. Teach the learner that Hobbes's conclusion follows fairly tightly from his bleak premise about human nature and scarcity, which is exactly the premise Locke will later contest (P215.6).

#### P215.6 Locke, Second Treatise of Civil Government
Locke's state of nature is governed by natural law and is tolerable, if inconvenient - people already have rights to life, liberty and property, and mostly respect them, but lack a common judge to resolve disputes impartially. Government is instituted by consent solely to remedy this inconvenience and better secure pre-existing rights, so the sovereign's authority is inherently conditional: a government that persistently violates the trust placed in it can legitimately be resisted. Teach the learner to trace precisely how Locke's more tolerable state of nature (compared with Hobbes's) is what licenses his far more limited, conditional conception of legitimate government - the same contractarian method, a different premise, a different politics.

#### P215.7 Montesquieu, The Spirit of the Laws
Montesquieu develops the separation of powers (legislative, executive, judicial) as a structural safeguard against tyranny, arguing from comparative study of real constitutions (including England's) that liberty is best protected not by trusting any single ruler's virtue but by arranging institutions so that power checks power. He also argues that the right form of government and law depends on a society's climate, customs, economy and history (an early comparative-institutional method), anticipating Aristotle's contextual approach (P215.2) but applied to modern states. Teach the learner to connect separation of powers directly to the counter-majoritarian institutions covered at Prelims (POLP.B3).

#### P215.8 Hume, Moral and Political Writings
Hume brings a sceptical, empiricist challenge to the entire social-contract tradition (P215.5-P215.6, P215.9): he argues real political obligation rarely if ever actually rests on genuine, voluntary consent - most people are simply born into an existing political order and never meaningfully "agree" to it - and that habit, custom and perceived utility, not an original contract, are what actually explain why people obey government. Teach the learner to use Hume as a critical test of the whole contractarian method: if Hume is right, does that undermine Locke's and Rousseau's conclusions, or can their normative claims survive even if their historical/hypothetical contract story is fictional?

#### P215.9 Rousseau, Discourse on Inequality and The Social Contract
The Discourse on Inequality argues that inequality is not natural but arises historically, especially through the institution of private property, corrupting an originally more equal and independent human condition. The Social Contract then asks how legitimate political authority is possible given this corruption, answering with the general will: citizens are genuinely free only when they obey a law they have collectively authored themselves in pursuit of the common good, not merely a law imposed on them or reflecting only private interests. Teach the learner to connect this back to POLP.A2 (Prelims) and to assess whether Rousseau's demanding conception of freedom-as-self-legislation is coherent or utopian.

#### P215.10 Cross-cutting themes: civic virtue, natural law, utopian thought, personal vs political
The paper explicitly wants answers to connect its texts through shared themes, not treat each thinker in isolation. Civic virtue runs from Aristotle through Machiavelli's Discourses to Montesquieu. Natural law runs from Aquinas through Locke's rights-based reworking. Utopian political thought is most visible in Plato's philosopher-kings and Rousseau's general will, both imagining a demanding, non-obvious standard for genuine political legitimacy. The personal/political relationship recurs in Plato's soul-state analogy and in Rousseau's claim that genuine political freedom transforms, rather than merely constrains, the individual. Teach the learner to draft a thesis that spans at least two thinkers via one of these themes, since that structure is what a strong Finals answer typically uses.

## Explicitly not here
Bentham to Weber (a separate, later period of political thought) is the next stage; do not import Mill, Marx or Weber into an answer for this paper.
