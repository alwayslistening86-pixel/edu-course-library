## How to teach this
Open with: "J.S. Mill defends near-absolute individual liberty in matters that only affect oneself, famously arguing society may never coerce someone 'for their own good.' Yet Mill was also a committed utilitarian, believing right action maximises aggregate happiness. If enough people would genuinely be made happier by restricting someone's private choices, why doesn't utilitarianism simply override Mill's liberty principle?" This tension - does Mill's liberalism actually follow from his utilitarianism, or sit uneasily beside it - is a template for how the paper wants texts interrogated, not just reported.

#### P216.1 Bentham, Political Thought
Bentham's utilitarianism is famously quantitative: the "felicific calculus" treats all pleasures and pains as commensurable units that can in principle be summed, and the right act or law is whichever produces the greatest total happiness (pleasure minus pain) across everyone affected. Bentham applied this directly to legal and political reform, arguing existing law and institutions should be judged, and rebuilt, by their actual contribution to aggregate utility rather than by tradition or natural-rights rhetoric, which he dismissed as "nonsense upon stilts." Teach the learner Bentham's reformist, calculative version of utilitarianism as the baseline Mill will complicate (P216.2).

#### P216.2 J.S. Mill, On Liberty and selected essays
Mill inherits Bentham's utilitarian framework but complicates it in two ways: he distinguishes higher (intellectual, moral) and lower (bodily) pleasures qualitatively, not just by quantity, and in On Liberty he argues - on ultimately utilitarian grounds - that individual liberty is instrumentally essential to long-run human flourishing and social progress, not simply one value to trade off case by case against aggregate happiness. The harm principle (also relevant at Prelims, POLP.A5) follows: society may coerce someone only to prevent harm to others, never merely for their own good or because the majority disapproves. Teach the learner the genuinely contested question: is Mill's liberty principle actually derived from utilitarianism, or does it quietly import a separate, non-utilitarian commitment to individuality?

#### P216.3 Hegel, Philosophy of Right and Philosophy of World History
Hegel argues true freedom is not simply the absence of external constraint (as in the liberal, contractarian tradition) but is realised through participation in rational institutions - family, civil society, and ultimately the state - which he sees as the highest actualisation of ethical life (Sittlichkeit). History, for Hegel, is the progressive unfolding of freedom's self-realisation through successive social and political forms, a teleological view of historical change. Teach the learner how sharply this differs from the individual-first, state-as-instrument picture running through Locke and Bentham/Mill, since Hegel makes the state itself constitutive of, not merely protective of, genuine freedom.

#### P216.4 Saint-Simon, Selected Writings
Saint-Simon is a key early socialist and technocratic thinker, arguing that society should be organised and directed by scientific and industrial expertise (engineers, scientists, productive "industrialists") rather than by inherited aristocratic privilege or purely political elites - a direct influence on later socialist and technocratic thought, including aspects of Marx and twentieth-century planning ideas. Teach the learner to treat Saint-Simon as a bridge figure: reacting against both the old aristocratic order and against purely political (rather than economic/technical) conceptions of how society should be organised.

#### P216.5 Tocqueville, Democracy in America
Tocqueville offers an empirically grounded, ambivalent assessment of democratic society (see also POLP.A3 at Prelims): admiring the equality and associational liberty he observed, while warning of genuine dangers intrinsic to democracy's own logic - the "tyranny of the majority," and a soft, administrative despotism that could emerge even without any single tyrant, as citizens retreat into private life and cede initiative to an ever-more-centralised, paternalistic state. Teach the learner to use Tocqueville as a counterpoint to both Hegel's state-centred optimism and Marx's class-based critique: his worry is about democratic equality's own unintended effects, not about capitalism or the state as such.

#### P216.6 Marx, Selected Writings
Marx develops historical materialism: a society's economic base (the mode of production and the class relations built on it) shapes its political and ideological superstructure (law, religion, political ideas), rather than ideas driving history independently. Class struggle is the engine of historical change, and Marx's critique of liberal rights argues they are formally equal (everyone has the same legal rights) but substantively serve bourgeois class interests under capitalism, masking real material inequality. Teach the learner how this is a fundamental challenge to the individualist assumptions running through Locke, Bentham and Mill - Marx would say their "individual" is already a specific, historically contingent, bourgeois construction, not a neutral starting point.

#### P216.7 Weber, From Max Weber
Weber's sociology of authority distinguishes traditional (custom-based), charismatic (based on a leader's personal qualities) and legal-rational (rule-based, bureaucratic) legitimacy as three ideal types explaining why people obey authority. His account of bureaucratic "rationalisation" describes modern life as increasingly organised around calculable, rule-governed procedures, producing efficiency but also a loss of meaning and spontaneity (the "disenchantment" of the world). Teach the learner to see Weber as offering a genuinely different lens on modern politics than either Hegel's idealism or Marx's materialism - one centred on legitimacy and bureaucratic rationality rather than the unfolding of freedom or the dynamics of class.

#### P216.8 Durkheim, Division of Labour and Professional Ethics
Durkheim's Division of Labour in Society examines how social solidarity changes as societies modernise: from "mechanical" solidarity (based on shared beliefs and similarity between members in simple societies) to "organic" solidarity (based on interdependence between differentiated roles in complex, modern societies). Professional Ethics and Civic Morals examines the role professional and civic groups play in holding a complex, individualistic modern society together, preventing the atomisation and normlessness ("anomie") that rapid modernisation can otherwise produce. Teach the learner to connect Durkheim's worry about social cohesion under modernity to Tocqueville's concern about democratic atomisation, despite their very different theoretical starting points.

#### P216.9 Cross-cutting themes: state/society/family, individual/community, history/social change, science/religion
The paper explicitly tests these connecting themes. State, society and family: Hegel makes the family and civil society necessary stages on the way to the rational state, while liberal thinkers (Mill, Bentham) treat the state's reach into family and civil life as something to be justified case by case. Individual and community: Marx and Durkheim both challenge liberal individualism, but from different directions (class interest vs social solidarity). History and social change: Hegel's teleological unfolding of freedom, Marx's historical materialism and Weber's rationalisation thesis are three competing engines of historical change. Science and religion: Saint-Simon's technocracy and Weber's disenchantment both track the rising authority of scientific/rational over traditional/religious ways of organising society. Teach the learner to build an answer around one of these themes spanning at least two thinkers.

## Explicitly not here
Plato to Rousseau (the earlier period, a separate paper) is not required here; keep Hobbes, Locke and Rousseau out of an answer for this paper unless explicitly used as background contrast.
