## How to teach this
Open with the scenario the paper is built to test: "A government wins a general election with 35% of the vote under first-past-the-post, while a rival party wins 25% of the vote but far fewer seats. Critics call the result 'undemocratic.' Defenders say the system delivered a clear, stable government. Who's right, and what does 'democratic' even mean here?" Make the learner notice that answering well needs both the empirical mechanism (how first-past-the-post actually behaves) and a theoretical account of what democracy requires - which is exactly the paper's two-section structure.

#### POLP.A1 Nature and grounds of rights (Locke)
Locke's Second Treatise of Civil Government argues that in the state of nature people already possess natural rights to life, liberty and property, governed (if imperfectly enforced) by natural law. Government is instituted by consent to remedy the "inconveniences" of the state of nature - the absence of a common judge - and exists to better secure those pre-existing rights, not to create them. Because the trust is conditional, a government that persistently violates it can legitimately be resisted. Teach the learner to distinguish rights as natural (pre-political, limiting what government may do) from rights as merely legal (created and revocable by the state) and to see why this distinction matters for how far a state may legitimately intrude on person or property.

#### POLP.A2 Nature and grounds of democracy (Rousseau)
Rousseau's Social Contract grounds legitimate authority not in consent to a ruler but in the "general will" - the collectively authored law of a self-governing community. Genuine freedom, for Rousseau, is obedience to a law one has had a hand in making, not merely the absence of interference. This yields a demanding conception of democracy: majority votes are legitimate only insofar as they track the general will (the common good), not simply the sum of private, self-interested wills. Teach the learner to contrast this with a simple headcount conception of democracy, and to apply it to the opening scenario: does 35% of the vote under first-past-the-post plausibly track a general will, or merely an aggregation of private interests?

#### POLP.A3 Role of civil society (Tocqueville)
Tocqueville's Democracy in America offers an empirically grounded, ambivalent account of democratic society: he admires the equality and associational liberty he found in America, particularly the dense network of voluntary civil associations that counterbalance central power, but warns of genuine dangers intrinsic to democratic equality itself - the "tyranny of the majority," and a soft, administrative despotism that can emerge from democratic societies' own logic even without any single tyrant. Teach the learner that Tocqueville is not simply pro- or anti-democracy: his distinctive contribution is showing how democracy's own virtues (equality, associational life) can, left unchecked, produce its own distinctive pathologies.

#### POLP.A4 Power in the democratic state (Marx and Engels)
The Communist Manifesto argues that political power in a capitalist democratic state is not neutral between classes: the state functions, on this view, as an instrument that reflects and protects the interests of the dominant economic class, so formally equal political rights (the vote, free speech) can coexist with deep substantive inequality in who actually shapes outcomes. Teach the learner to use this as a critical lens on the opening scenario: a Marx-influenced answer would ask not just whether the electoral mechanism is fair in form, but whose economic interests the resulting government actually serves.

#### POLP.A5 Liberty, state paternalism and free speech (Mill)
Mill's On Liberty defends the harm principle: the only justification for exercising power over a person against their will is to prevent harm to others - not for their own good (anti-paternalism), and not because the majority disapproves. Mill extends this to speech, arguing that even false or offensive opinion should be tolerated because open contest is how a society tests and corrects its beliefs, and that silencing opinion robs both the silenced and the silencing majority. Teach the learner to apply the harm principle to hard cases (seatbelt laws, hate speech, paternalistic health regulation) and to notice where "harm to others" itself becomes contested.

#### POLP.B1 Constitutional variations: presidentialism, parliamentarism, types of democracy
Presidential systems (separately elected executive and legislature, fixed terms) and parliamentary systems (executive drawn from and accountable to the legislature, no fixed term) differ in how they handle deadlock, accountability and the "who leads" question when the executive's party lacks a majority. Compare majoritarian democracies (concentrating power, e.g. UK-style Westminster systems) with consensus democracies (dispersing power across coalitions, courts and federal units, e.g. many continental European systems), and connect each to different trade-offs between decisiveness and inclusiveness - directly relevant to evaluating the opening scenario's first-past-the-post outcome.

#### POLP.B2 Politics outside advanced industrial democracies: authoritarianism, state strength, social movements, revolutions
Not all states are stable democracies: some are authoritarian (concentrated power without genuine competitive elections), and states vary sharply in capacity - a "strong" state can enforce law and collect revenue across its territory, a "weak" state cannot. Social movements (organised, sustained collective action outside formal institutions) and revolutions (fundamental, often violent, overthrow of a political order) are two routes by which political change happens outside routine democratic competition. Teach the learner to compare why some authoritarian regimes are stable for decades while others collapse suddenly, and what role state strength plays in that difference.

#### POLP.B3 Institutions of the democratic state: legislatures, executives, counter-majoritarian powers, war and peace
Legislatures make law and (in parliamentary systems) sustain or remove the executive; executives implement policy and, increasingly, initiate it. Counter-majoritarian institutions - constitutional courts, independent central banks, entrenched bills of rights - deliberately limit what even a clear democratic majority may do, raising a genuine tension with majority-rule accounts of democracy (compare POLP.A2). War and peace powers test this directly: who actually decides to commit a state to war, and how far can that decision be insulated from ordinary legislative control, are live comparative questions.

#### POLP.B4 Parties, party systems, voters, populism and identity politics
Party systems range from two-party (favoured by first-past-the-post, per Duverger's law) to multi-party (favoured by proportional representation), with different implications for government stability and voter choice. Voting behaviour research asks whether voters choose on policy, party identity, economic performance or group identity. Populism mobilises a claimed opposition between "the people" and a corrupt elite, often cutting across conventional left-right lines; identity politics organises political claims around group membership (ethnicity, gender, religion) rather than, or alongside, class or ideology. Teach the learner to use these concepts to explain why a party can win 35% of the vote yet dominate seats.

## Explicitly not here
Pure political theory (liberty, justice, rights as freestanding philosophical topics) returns in much greater depth at Finals in Theory of Politics (P203); this stage only needs the Prelims-level grounding in each named text.
