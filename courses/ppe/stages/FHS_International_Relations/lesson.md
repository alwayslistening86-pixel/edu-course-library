# FHS_International_Relations - Lesson: International Relations (Paper 214)

## Goal
The learner correctly identifies and applies the relevant IR theory to a question, illustrates with contemporary or historical (post-1990) material as the paper requires, and states and defends a clear argument.

- P214.2 - Liberal institutionalism and democratic peace theory: learn and apply: liberal institutionalism
- P214.3 - Critical approaches to international relations: learn and apply: critical approaches
- P214.4 - Postcolonialism and empire: learn and apply: postcolonialism and empire
- P214.5 - International law: learn and apply: international law
- P214.6 - Power and security: learn and apply: power and security

- P214.10 - Post-1990 international affairs (illustrative knowledge requirement): learn and apply: post-1990 international affairs

## How to teach this
Open with: "Two states have deeply integrated economies, dense diplomatic ties, and shared membership in international institutions - yet tensions over a disputed territory escalate toward military confrontation. A realist says this was always likely: institutions and trade are thin veneers over an anarchic system where states ultimately rely on power. A liberal institutionalist says the fact tensions haven't already exploded, given the underlying dispute, shows interdependence and institutions genuinely restrain conflict. Who has the better explanation?" Insist the learner name what evidence would actually distinguish the two theories, not just assert one.

#### P214.1 Classical (realist) approaches to international relations
Realism holds the international system is fundamentally anarchic - no overarching authority above sovereign states - which makes security competition and self-help the basic logic of international politics, regardless of any state's internal character or ideology. States are assumed to act rationally to maximise power or security; institutions and international law are, on this view, ultimately constrained by and reflective of underlying power distributions rather than genuinely autonomous, independent forces capable of overriding state interest. Teach the learner realism's core mechanism (anarchy forces self-help) as the theory the paper's other approaches are most often defined against.

#### P214.2 Liberal institutionalism and democratic peace theory
Liberal institutionalism challenges the realist picture without abandoning rational-state assumptions: it argues repeated interaction, economic interdependence, and international institutions can genuinely change states' incentives - making cooperation more likely and costly defection less attractive - even within an anarchic system, because reputational and institutional costs of defection are real. Democratic peace theory, a related liberal claim, holds that mature democracies rarely if ever go to war with one another, for reasons tied to shared norms, domestic accountability to a war-averse public, or institutional constraints on rapid unilateral escalation. Teach the learner to treat the near-absence of democracy-democracy wars as a genuinely striking empirical pattern requiring explanation, not just an assertion.

#### P214.3 Critical approaches to international relations
Critical IR theory challenges realism's and liberalism's shared assumption that states are the natural, neutral unit of analysis, asking instead whose interests concepts like "security," "order" and "the national interest" actually serve, and how power operates through ideas, discourse and identity construction, not just material capability or institutional design. Teach the learner that a critical answer typically does not propose a rival causal mechanism to realism or liberalism, but instead questions the framing both share - for example, asking who gets to count as a legitimate security actor and who is rendered invisible by that framing.

#### P214.4 Postcolonialism and empire
Postcolonial approaches examine how the international system's basic concepts - sovereignty, development, the state itself, "civilisation" - were shaped by colonial history and continue to encode hierarchies between the Global North and South, even in formally equal institutions like the United Nations. This challenges mainstream IR theory (realism and liberalism alike) to reckon with whose historical experience it implicitly takes as the default, standard case when generalising about "the state" or "the international system." Teach the learner to apply this to a concrete case: how colonial-era border-drawing continues to shape conflict and state capacity in many postcolonial states today.

#### P214.5 International law
International law - treaties, customary international law, the jurisdiction of bodies like the International Court of Justice - raises a direct theoretical puzzle: realists doubt it meaningfully constrains powerful states absent enforcement power, while liberal institutionalists argue it shapes state behaviour through reputational costs, reciprocity and the way it structures expectations even without a world government to enforce it. Teach the learner to use a real case (a state violating or complying with a treaty obligation) to test which theory's prediction about international law's actual bite better fits the evidence.

#### P214.6 Power and security
Power in IR is contested beyond simple military capability: it includes economic leverage, institutional agenda-setting power, and "soft power" (the ability to shape others' preferences through attraction rather than coercion). Security itself has been broadened by some approaches beyond traditional military security to include economic, environmental and human security. Teach the learner that how a theory defines power and security substantially predetermines what counts as evidence for or against it - a purely military conception of power will systematically understate the significance liberal or critical theories assign to institutions and ideas.

#### P214.7 Globalisation, global governance and international cooperation
Economic globalisation, transnational institutions (the UN, WTO, regional bodies like the EU) and non-state actors (multinational corporations, NGOs, transnational advocacy networks) complicate a purely state-centric picture of world politics. Genuine live debates ask whether globalisation is eroding state sovereignty, or simply changing the form sovereignty takes (states choosing to pool authority in institutions to achieve outcomes they could not achieve alone). Teach the learner to distinguish global governance (coordination without a world government) from world government (which does not exist), and to assess specific institutions' actual authority rather than assuming globalisation uniformly weakens states.

#### P214.8 Theories of war and peace, and domestic determinants of foreign policy
Beyond system-level theories (realism, liberalism), domestic-level explanations look inside the state: regime type (democratic peace theory, P214.2), domestic political coalitions, bureaucratic politics and leader psychology can all shape foreign policy choices in ways a purely systemic (anarchy-driven) theory cannot explain. Teach the learner to use a real conflict or peace case where domestic politics (an election, a coalition government's internal divisions, a leader's individual choices) plausibly explains foreign policy behaviour that a purely systemic account would not predict.

#### P214.9 Ethnic, national and cultural sources of conflict
Not all conflict is explained by interstate security competition: ethnic, national and cultural cleavages within and across state borders can generate conflict through identity-based mobilisation, competing nationalist claims to the same territory, or the political instrumentalisation of ethnic difference by elites seeking power. Teach the learner to distinguish primordialist accounts (ethnic identity and enmity as ancient and fixed) from constructivist accounts (ethnic conflict as actively constructed and mobilised for contemporary political purposes), since the paper rewards analytical precision about mechanism, not just naming "ethnic conflict" as a cause.

#### P214.10 Post-1990 international affairs (illustrative knowledge requirement)
The paper explicitly requires candidates to know the major developments in international affairs from 1990 onwards and to cite these wherever appropriate - questions referencing events before 1990 will not be set, so illustrative material must be drawn from the post-Cold War period onward (the end of the Cold War itself, post-Cold War institutional expansion, major post-1990 conflicts, the rise of new powers, and contemporary international crises). Teach the learner to build a small personal bank of well-understood post-1990 cases across each theoretical approach (a realist case, a liberal-institutionalist case, a case illustrating postcolonial or critical themes), ready to deploy under exam conditions rather than improvised.

## Explicitly not here
Detailed political theory concepts (liberty, justice, democracy as freestanding philosophical topics) belong to Theory of Politics (P203); IR uses them only as applied to the international system.
