# S08_Highways_Trespass_and_Nuisance - Lesson: Highways, trespass to land, nuisance and Rylands v Fletcher

## Goal
The learner explains a highway authority's statutory duty to maintain, public rights of way, trespass to land, public and private nuisance, and the rule in Rylands v Fletcher.

## How to teach this
Ask: a pothole appears overnight and a cyclist is injured in it the next morning. Is the council automatically liable? Teach each topic as: the rule (the statute section or case authority, stated precisely), why it exists (the policy/purpose CILEX's own module text gives), and how it is applied to facts. CILEX's Foundation-stage exams (F1-F4) are 60 multiple-choice and short 'automated' (fill-in/matching) questions testing precise, exam-ready knowledge -- push the learner to state rules exactly (case names, section numbers, dates) rather than paraphrase loosely. F5 and F6 also include practical scenario tasks (a communication/drafting exercise for F5; producing research documents for F6) -- give the learner realistic client scenarios and paralegal tasks, not just questions about the skill in the abstract. Throughout, reinforce that a CILEX trainee at this level must recognise the limits of their own authority and know when to escalate to a supervisor -- this is an explicit learning outcome in F2, F3 and F5, not incidental. Law changes: this course states the law as understood to be current in September 2026 (England and Wales); flag to the learner that specific figures (SDLT/LTT thresholds, IHT nil-rate bands, fee levels) and any pending reform (e.g. the Bill of Rights Bill, which CILEX's own F1 material lists as syllabus content but which had not been enacted -- the Human Rights Act 1998 remains in force) should be checked against a live source (legislation.gov.uk, gov.uk, cilex.org.uk) before a real exam sitting.

#### TORT.5 Outline of the law relating to highways: Highways Act 1980, public rights of way
**The law relating to highways.** The **Highways Act 1980, s41** imposes a statutory duty on the highway authority to maintain highways maintainable at public expense. The **extent of the duty** is to keep the highway safe for ordinary traffic, taking into account factors such as the nature/importance of the highway and the traffic reasonably expected on it; it does not guarantee a perfectly hazard-free surface, and **transient hazards** (e.g. a spillage or pothole that appeared very recently) may not amount to a breach if the authority could not reasonably have known of and remedied it in time. The authority has a statutory **defence of reasonable care under s58**: showing it took such care as was reasonable in all the circumstances (e.g. a reasonable inspection and repair system) to secure the highway was not dangerous. **Public rights of way** (footpaths, bridleways etc.) are separately protected, including under the **Countryside and Rights of Way Act 2000**, which also created public access rights over certain open land. Not all roads are maintainable at public expense; **local authority adopted highways** are those the authority has taken responsibility for, typically following a formal adoption process for new estate roads.

#### TORT.6 Trespass and nuisance: trespass to land, public/private nuisance, the rule in Rylands v Fletcher
**Trespass to land.** Direct, intentional (or negligent) and unjustified interference with land in another's possession -- e.g. entering without permission, remaining after permission is withdrawn, or placing/throwing objects on the land. It is actionable without proof of damage. **Defences** include a **licence** (permission, express or implied, which if exceeded or exceeded in duration reverts the entrant to a trespasser) and **statutory authority** (e.g. police or other enforcement officials exercising a lawful power of entry).

**Nuisance.** **Public nuisance** is a crime and a tort: an act or omission materially affecting the reasonable comfort/convenience of a **class of Her/His Majesty's subjects** (not just one individual); an individual claimant must additionally show **special damage** beyond that suffered by the public generally to bring a civil claim; some aspects now also fall under statutory public order provisions such as the **Police, Crime, Sentencing and Courts Act 2022**. **Private nuisance** protects a claimant's use/enjoyment of land against unlawful, indirect interference (e.g. noise, smell, vibration): the claimant needs a **proprietary interest** in the affected land, and the court balances the interests of both parties, asking whether the interference is **reasonable** given factors including **sensitivity** of the claimant's use (an abnormally sensitive use is less protected), the **extent/duration of harm**, the **locality** (what is normal for that area), any **malice**, and the **usefulness of the defendant's conduct**. **Defences**: **prescription** (20 years' uninterrupted nuisance, available only for private nuisance), **statutory authority** (the activity is expressly or impliedly authorised by statute), and the principle that "**coming to the nuisance**" is not itself a defence, though the usefulness of the defendant's conduct may still be weighed.

**Rylands v Fletcher.** A strict-liability tort: where a defendant brings onto land, in the course of a **non-natural use** of that land, something **likely to do mischief if it escapes** (e.g. water, fire, explosives, chemicals) and it does **escape**, causing damage to the claimant's land/interest, the defendant is liable without proof of negligence. Liability falls on those with control of the land/thing (typically the occupier). "Non-natural use" is judged against ordinary use of land in context, and modern authority has aligned the rule closely with nuisance (requiring the escape to be reasonably foreseeable in kind).
