## Test items
1. Explain the requirements of the rule in Rylands v Fletcher and how it differs from ordinary negligence. [6 marks]
2. A factory stores industrial chemicals on its land as part of its ordinary manufacturing use. A tank ruptures during a storm and chemicals escape onto a neighbouring farm, killing livestock. The neighbouring farmer also complains that noise from the factory, running only during normal working hours, has disturbed them for years. Using IRAC, advise on both potential claims. [14 marks]

## Answer key (for the tutor only)
1. [6] B1 the defendant must bring onto land, in the course of a non-natural use, something likely to do mischief if it escapes; B1 the thing must actually escape from the defendant's land/control; B1 the escape must cause damage to the claimant's land or interest in land; B1 liability falls on the person in control of the land/thing (usually the occupier); B1 it is a form of strict liability -- the claimant need not prove the defendant was negligent, unlike ordinary negligence which requires proof of a breach of a duty of care; B1 modern authority requires the type of damage to have been reasonably foreseeable, aligning the rule closely with nuisance principles.
2. [14] Mark using issue-rule-application-conclusion (IRAC): identify the issue(s), state the rule precisely with its authority, apply it to the facts given, and reach a supported conclusion advising the client. Credit: issue = strict liability for the chemical escape, and private nuisance for the noise; rule = Rylands v Fletcher requires a non-natural use, something likely to do mischief, an escape, and resulting damage, foreseeable in kind; private nuisance requires unlawful, substantial interference with use/enjoyment of land, balancing reasonableness factors including locality and duration; application = storing hazardous chemicals is likely a non-natural use even in an industrial context if the scale/hazard is significant, and the escape causing livestock deaths would satisfy Rylands (subject to arguing the storm was a novus actus/act of God, a possible defence); noise confined to normal working hours in an industrial locality is less likely to be an actionable nuisance, since locality and reasonable industrial activity are weighed in the defendant's favour; conclusion = the chemical escape claim has good prospects under Rylands v Fletcher (and possibly negligence), while the noise nuisance claim is weaker given the locality and confined hours, though not hopeless if the noise is unusually severe.
