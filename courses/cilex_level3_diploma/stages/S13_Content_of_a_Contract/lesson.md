# S13_Content_of_a_Contract - Lesson: The content of a contract: terms, implied terms, exemption clauses

## Goal
The learner distinguishes express and implied terms and representations, explains terms implied by statute and by fact/custom, and explains the control of exemption and unfair terms.

## Syllabus items taught here
- CON.5 - The content of a contract: express and implied terms, exemption clauses and unfair terms

## How to teach this
Ask: a secondhand car is sold 'as seen' with a clause excluding all liability for defects. The car turns out to be unroadworthy. Does the exclusion clause protect the seller? Teach each topic as: the rule (the statute section or case authority, stated precisely), why it exists (the policy/purpose CILEX's own module text gives), and how it is applied to facts. CILEX's Foundation-stage exams (F1-F4) are 60 multiple-choice and short 'automated' (fill-in/matching) questions testing precise, exam-ready knowledge -- push the learner to state rules exactly (case names, section numbers, dates) rather than paraphrase loosely. F5 and F6 also include practical scenario tasks (a communication/drafting exercise for F5; producing research documents for F6) -- give the learner realistic client scenarios and paralegal tasks, not just questions about the skill in the abstract. Throughout, reinforce that a CILEX trainee at this level must recognise the limits of their own authority and know when to escalate to a supervisor -- this is an explicit learning outcome in F2, F3 and F5, not incidental. Law changes: this course states the law as understood to be current in September 2026 (England and Wales); flag to the learner that specific figures (SDLT/LTT thresholds, IHT nil-rate bands, fee levels) and any pending reform (e.g. the Bill of Rights Bill, which CILEX's own F1 material lists as syllabus content but which had not been enacted -- the Human Rights Act 1998 remains in force) should be checked against a live source (legislation.gov.uk, gov.uk, cilex.org.uk) before a real exam sitting.

#### CON.5 The content of a contract: express and implied terms, exemption clauses and unfair terms
**Express and implied terms; representations.** **Express terms** are those actually stated (orally or in writing) and agreed by the parties. A statement made during negotiations may instead be a **representation** (an inducement to contract, not itself a contractual promise), the distinction turning on factors such as the **importance** the parties placed on it, whether the party making it had **special knowledge/skill**, the **timing** relative to the contract, and whether it was later **reduced to writing** in the contract itself. **Implied terms** supplement the express agreement: terms implied **in fact** (to reflect the parties' presumed but unstated intentions, satisfying the **business efficacy** test -- the term is necessary to make the contract work -- or the **"officious bystander" test** -- so obvious it goes without saying); terms implied **by custom** (established trade or local custom, provided not inconsistent with the express terms); and terms implied **by law/statute**. Statute implies significant terms into contracts for the supply of goods, services and digital content: the **Consumer Rights Act 2015** applies to **business-to-consumer** contracts, implying terms that goods must be of **satisfactory quality**, **fit for any particular purpose** made known to the trader, and **as described** (s9-s11), that services must be performed with **reasonable care and skill** (s49), within a **reasonable time** if none is fixed (s52), and digital content must be of satisfactory quality; the **Sale of Goods Act 1979** and **Supply of Goods and Services Act 1982** contain corresponding implied terms for **business-to-business** contracts.

**Exemption clauses.** A clause seeking to exclude or limit liability must (1) be validly **incorporated** into the contract (by signature -- binding even if unread, ***L'Estrange v Graucob***, subject to misrepresentation of its effect; by reasonable notice given before/at the time of contracting, with unusually onerous clauses requiring specially clear notice, ***Interfoto v Stiletto***; or by a consistent course of dealing); (2) as a matter of **construction**, cover the loss/breach that actually occurred (ambiguity is construed *contra proferentem*, against the party relying on the clause); and (3) survive statutory control. The **Unfair Contract Terms Act 1977** applies primarily to business liability: it renders void any clause excluding or limiting liability for **death or personal injury caused by negligence**; other exclusions of negligence liability, and exclusions in contracts on one party's **written standard terms** or in **consumer** contracts (to the extent not superseded by the 2015 Act), must satisfy a **reasonableness test**. For **consumer** contracts, the **Consumer Rights Act 2015 Part 2** now governs unfair terms specifically: a term is **unfair**, and therefore not binding on the consumer, if, contrary to the requirement of good faith, it causes a **significant imbalance** in the parties' rights and obligations to the detriment of the consumer; a term must also be **transparent** (plain, intelligible language) and, for a term specifying the main subject matter or price, is only assessed for fairness if it is not transparent and prominent. Terms excluding/limiting liability for death or personal injury from negligence, or for the core statutory implied terms as to quality/fitness/description, are treated as automatically unfair/ineffective against a consumer.

## Explicitly not here
Vitiating factors affecting the validity of a contract (mistake, misrepresentation, duress, undue influence) are S14.
