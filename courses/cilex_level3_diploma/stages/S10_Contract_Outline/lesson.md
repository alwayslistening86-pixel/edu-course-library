# S10_Contract_Outline - Lesson: Contract law in outline: privity, professional/consumer contracts, illegality

## Goal
The learner explains what a contract is and the categories contracts fall into, the doctrine of privity and its statutory exception, and the effect of illegality on a contract.

## Syllabus items taught here
- CON.1 - Contract law in outline: privity and third-party rights, professional/business/consumer contracts, illegality

## How to teach this
Ask: a father pays a garage to service a car as a birthday present for his daughter. If the garage does the work badly, can the daughter sue, even though she never paid anything and wasn't a party to the agreement? Teach each topic as: the rule (the statute section or case authority, stated precisely), why it exists (the policy/purpose CILEX's own module text gives), and how it is applied to facts. CILEX's Foundation-stage exams (F1-F4) are 60 multiple-choice and short 'automated' (fill-in/matching) questions testing precise, exam-ready knowledge -- push the learner to state rules exactly (case names, section numbers, dates) rather than paraphrase loosely. F5 and F6 also include practical scenario tasks (a communication/drafting exercise for F5; producing research documents for F6) -- give the learner realistic client scenarios and paralegal tasks, not just questions about the skill in the abstract. Throughout, reinforce that a CILEX trainee at this level must recognise the limits of their own authority and know when to escalate to a supervisor -- this is an explicit learning outcome in F2, F3 and F5, not incidental. Law changes: this course states the law as understood to be current in September 2026 (England and Wales); flag to the learner that specific figures (SDLT/LTT thresholds, IHT nil-rate bands, fee levels) and any pending reform (e.g. the Bill of Rights Bill, which CILEX's own F1 material lists as syllabus content but which had not been enacted -- the Human Rights Act 1998 remains in force) should be checked against a live source (legislation.gov.uk, gov.uk, cilex.org.uk) before a real exam sitting.

#### CON.1 Contract law in outline: privity and third-party rights, professional/business/consumer contracts, illegality
**Contract law in outline.** A contract is a legally binding agreement between two or more parties, enforceable through the courts, requiring (as later stages cover) offer, acceptance, consideration, intention to create legal relations and capacity. Contracts are categorised by context, which affects the rules that apply: **professional contracts** (e.g. retainers for legal, accountancy or surveying services, often subject to sector regulatory rules alongside general contract law); **business-to-business contracts** (between commercial parties of broadly equal bargaining power, where courts are generally more willing to hold parties to their bargain, including tougher exemption/limitation clauses); and **consumer contracts** (between a trader acting for business purposes and a consumer acting wholly or mainly outside their trade/business/profession), which attract enhanced statutory protection, notably the **Consumer Rights Act 2015** (terms implied into goods, services and digital content contracts, and control of unfair terms) and the **Consumer Contracts (Information, Cancellation and Additional Charges) Regulations 2013** (information duties and cancellation/cooling-off rights for distance and off-premises contracts).

**Privity of contract.** The common law doctrine of **privity** provides that only a party to a contract can sue or be sued on it, and a contract cannot generally impose obligations on, or confer enforceable rights on, someone who is not a party to it -- even if the contract was made for their benefit. This caused practical unfairness where a contract was expressly intended to benefit a third party who then had no means of enforcing it. The **Contracts (Rights of Third Parties) Act 1999** creates a statutory exception: a person who is not a party to a contract (a **third party**) may in their own right enforce a term of the contract if (**s1(1)(a)**) the contract expressly provides that they may, or (**s1(1)(b)**) the term purports to confer a benefit on them (unless, on the proper construction of the contract, the parties did not intend the term to be enforceable by the third party). The third party must be expressly identified in the contract by name, as a member of a class, or by a particular description, though need not be in existence when the contract is made (e.g. an unborn child or a company not yet incorporated). Once the third party's right has "crystallised" (e.g. by relying on the term with the promisor's knowledge), the original contracting parties generally cannot vary or rescind the contract to extinguish that right without the third party's consent, subject to any term reserving that ability. The Act does not affect any right the third party may have independently of it (e.g. under the law of trusts or agency).

**Illegality.** A contract that is illegal, either in its formation (e.g. an agreement to commit a crime or tort) or in its performance (a lawful agreement carried out in an unlawful way), or that is contrary to public policy (e.g. a contract to oust the jurisdiction of the courts, or an unreasonable restraint of trade), is generally **void or unenforceable**. The courts will not usually assist a party to enforce or recover under an illegal contract (reflecting the maxim *ex turpi causa non oritur actio*), though the modern approach considers a range of factors -- including the underlying purpose of the prohibition, any other relevant public policy, and proportionality -- rather than applying a rigid rule, and a genuinely innocent party may in some circumstances still recover.
