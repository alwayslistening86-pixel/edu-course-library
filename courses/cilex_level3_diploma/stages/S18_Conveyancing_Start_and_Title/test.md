## Test items
1. A buyer's conveyancer notices the seller's title is registered with only 'possessory title'. What does this indicate? Choose every correct option.
   A. The title is the best available class, guaranteeing ownership subject only to register entries and overriding interests
   B. The title is based on possession rather than a documented paper title, or the original title deeds were lost, and is a weaker guarantee than absolute title
   C. The seller has not yet paid off their mortgage
   D. The property has not yet been allocated a title number
2. Explain what a requisition on title is and give two examples of the kind of matter it might be used to address. [6 marks]
3. A buyer's conveyancer, reviewing the official copies for a freehold house the buyer intends to convert into two flats, finds a restrictive covenant on the charges register prohibiting use of the property other than as a single private dwelling, and that the title is registered with good leasehold title rather than absolute title despite the seller describing it as freehold. Using IRAC, advise on the significance of each finding and the steps the conveyancer should take. [12 marks]

## Answer key (for the tutor only)
1. Correct: B (exactly these options, no others)
2. [6] B2 a requisition on title is a formal written query or requirement raised by the buyer's conveyancer with the seller's conveyancer after reviewing the official copies/title, seeking to resolve doubts or obtain confirmations/undertakings before completion; B2 requiring an undertaking to discharge/redeem an existing mortgage on completion; B2 querying the effect or continued relevance of a restrictive covenant or other charges register entry; B2 asking for confirmation an entry no longer affects the property; B2 clarifying discrepancies between the title plan and the physical boundaries described in the contract -- any 2 examples for full marks.
3. [12] Mark using issue-rule-application-conclusion (IRAC): identify the issue(s), state the rule precisely with its authority, apply it to the facts given, and reach a supported conclusion advising the client. Credit: issue = whether the restrictive covenant prevents the buyer's intended use, and why a purportedly freehold property shows good leasehold title; rule = a restrictive covenant on the charges register is a third-party interest binding the registered proprietor and successors, and can be enforced against use inconsistent with its terms unless released, varied, or unenforceable on some basis (e.g. no one with the benefit able to enforce it, though this cannot be assumed without investigation); good leasehold title indicates a leasehold interest whose grant was not verified against the landlord's own title, and would be inconsistent with a genuinely freehold sale, suggesting a discrepancy requiring urgent clarification via requisitions; application = the covenant is a serious issue for the buyer's conversion plan and should be raised as a requisition/enquiry, potentially requiring restrictive covenant indemnity insurance, a deed of release/variation from those with the benefit, or advice that the conversion cannot proceed as planned; the title class discrepancy suggests either an error in the register, a mismatch between the seller's description and the true legal estate, or that the property is in fact leasehold, and must be resolved by requisition before proceeding, since it goes to the fundamental nature of what the buyer is acquiring; conclusion = the conveyancer should raise formal requisitions on both points, advise the buyer not to exchange until resolved, and consider indemnity insurance or specialist advice on the covenant and clarification of the true title position before any further step.
