# S04_UK_Constitution_and_Legislative_Power - Lesson: The UK constitution and legislative power

## Goal
The learner explains the nature, sources and key doctrines of the uncodified UK constitution, the Westminster model and devolution, and how primary and secondary legislation are created and controlled.

## Syllabus items taught here
- LS.4 - The nature of the UK constitution: sovereignty, rule of law, separation of powers, sources, devolution
- LS.5 - Legislative power: the UK legislature, primary legislation, secondary legislation and its control

## How to teach this
Ask: the UK has no single written constitutional document like the USA's -- so where do the rules about how the country is governed actually come from? Teach each topic as: the rule (the statute section or case authority, stated precisely), why it exists (the policy/purpose CILEX's own module text gives), and how it is applied to facts. CILEX's Foundation-stage exams (F1-F4) are 60 multiple-choice and short 'automated' (fill-in/matching) questions testing precise, exam-ready knowledge -- push the learner to state rules exactly (case names, section numbers, dates) rather than paraphrase loosely. F5 and F6 also include practical scenario tasks (a communication/drafting exercise for F5; producing research documents for F6) -- give the learner realistic client scenarios and paralegal tasks, not just questions about the skill in the abstract. Throughout, reinforce that a CILEX trainee at this level must recognise the limits of their own authority and know when to escalate to a supervisor -- this is an explicit learning outcome in F2, F3 and F5, not incidental. Law changes: this course states the law as understood to be current in September 2026 (England and Wales); flag to the learner that specific figures (SDLT/LTT thresholds, IHT nil-rate bands, fee levels) and any pending reform (e.g. the Bill of Rights Bill, which CILEX's own F1 material lists as syllabus content but which had not been enacted -- the Human Rights Act 1998 remains in force) should be checked against a live source (legislation.gov.uk, gov.uk, cilex.org.uk) before a real exam sitting.

#### LS.4 The nature of the UK constitution: sovereignty, rule of law, separation of powers, sources, devolution
**The nature of the UK constitution.** The UK constitution is **uncodified** (not contained in one single document) but drawn from multiple sources, and traditionally **unentrenched** (ordinary statute can, in principle, change it, since no Act binds a future Parliament). Its purpose is to allocate and limit governmental power and protect individual rights. Key doctrines: **parliamentary sovereignty** -- Parliament can make or unmake any law, and no other body (including the courts) can override an Act of Parliament; **the rule of law** -- everyone, including the government, is subject to and accountable under the law, applied predictably and equally; **the separation of powers** -- power is divided between the legislature (makes law), the executive (implements/administers law and policy) and the judiciary (interprets and applies law and resolves disputes), each providing a check on the others, though the UK's separation is partial (e.g. ministers sit in Parliament).

**Sources of the constitution.** **Key constitutional documents** (e.g. Magna Carta 1215, the Bill of Rights 1689); **Acts of Parliament** (e.g. the Human Rights Act 1998); **common law** (constitutional principles developed by judges, e.g. aspects of judicial review); the **Royal Prerogative** (residual, historic powers exercised by the Crown, now mostly by ministers on the Crown's behalf, e.g. treaty-making, some aspects of defence); **constitutional conventions** (non-legal but binding-in-practice political rules, e.g. that the monarch assents to Bills passed by Parliament); and **international law**, including the impact of the **European Convention on Human Rights** (given further effect domestically by the Human Rights Act 1998) and the changed status of retained EU law following the UK's withdrawal from the EU.

**Political governance and power.** The **Westminster model** features a fused executive/legislature (the government is drawn from and accountable to Parliament), majoritarian elections, and (historically) strong single-party government. The UK is a **unitary state** in law (ultimate sovereignty rests with the UK Parliament), but power has been **devolved** to the Scottish Parliament/Government, the Welsh Senedd/Welsh Government, and the Northern Ireland Assembly/Executive, who legislate and govern in defined devolved areas (in Wales, this includes some aspects of housing and local taxation such as Land Transaction Tax, though core contract, tort and criminal law taught in this course remain shared England-and-Wales law).

#### LS.5 Legislative power: the UK legislature, primary legislation, secondary legislation and its control
**Legislative power.** The UK legislature is **bicameral**: the elected **House of Commons** and the largely appointed **House of Lords**, with the **monarch's** role now formal (Royal Assent by convention). **Primary legislation** (Acts of Parliament) may be preceded by **consultation** (Green Papers setting out options, White Papers setting out firm proposals, Law Commission reports, and formal consultations). Bills are: **Public Bills** (Government Bills, introduced by a minister, or Private Members' Bills, introduced by a backbencher); **Private Bills** (affecting specific individuals/organisations/localities); **Hybrid Bills** (mixed public/private effect). A Bill's stages in each House: **First Reading** (formal introduction), **Second Reading** (debate on principle), **Committee Stage** (detailed scrutiny/amendment), **Report Stage** (further amendment), **Third Reading** (final debate), then the same process in the other House, followed by **Royal Assent**. The **Parliament Acts 1911-1949** allow the Commons to bypass Lords' opposition to most Bills in defined circumstances. An Act of Parliament has a structured format: long title, enacting formula, sections, subsections, and often schedules.

**Secondary (delegated) legislation.** Made under powers granted by an enabling Act (e.g. statutory instruments, regulations, orders), allowing detailed/technical rules to be made or updated without a full Act. **Parliamentary control**: publication requirements, laying before Parliament, and scrutiny by Parliamentary committees (e.g. the affirmative or negative resolution procedure). **Judicial control**: delegated legislation can be challenged as **ultra vires** -- **substantive** (the content goes beyond the powers the enabling Act granted) or **procedural** (a required procedure, such as consultation, was not followed). **Advantages**: saves parliamentary time, allows technical/expert input, allows quick updating. **Disadvantages**: less parliamentary scrutiny/debate, risk of sub-delegation, less democratic accountability.
