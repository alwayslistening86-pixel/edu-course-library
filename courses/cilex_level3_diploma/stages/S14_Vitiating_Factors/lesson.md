# S14_Vitiating_Factors - Lesson: Vitiating factors: mistake, misrepresentation, duress and undue influence

## Goal
The learner explains the vitiating factors of mistake, misrepresentation, duress and undue influence, their categories, and their effect on the validity of a contract.

## Syllabus items taught here
- CON.6 - Vitiating factors: mistake, misrepresentation, duress and undue influence

## How to teach this
Ask: a buyer is pressured by a business partner, under an implied threat to end a long trading relationship, into signing a guarantee they didn't really want to sign. Is the guarantee enforceable? Teach each topic as: the rule (the statute section or case authority, stated precisely), why it exists (the policy/purpose CILEX's own module text gives), and how it is applied to facts. CILEX's Foundation-stage exams (F1-F4) are 60 multiple-choice and short 'automated' (fill-in/matching) questions testing precise, exam-ready knowledge -- push the learner to state rules exactly (case names, section numbers, dates) rather than paraphrase loosely. F5 and F6 also include practical scenario tasks (a communication/drafting exercise for F5; producing research documents for F6) -- give the learner realistic client scenarios and paralegal tasks, not just questions about the skill in the abstract. Throughout, reinforce that a CILEX trainee at this level must recognise the limits of their own authority and know when to escalate to a supervisor -- this is an explicit learning outcome in F2, F3 and F5, not incidental. Law changes: this course states the law as understood to be current in September 2026 (England and Wales); flag to the learner that specific figures (SDLT/LTT thresholds, IHT nil-rate bands, fee levels) and any pending reform (e.g. the Bill of Rights Bill, which CILEX's own F1 material lists as syllabus content but which had not been enacted -- the Human Rights Act 1998 remains in force) should be checked against a live source (legislation.gov.uk, gov.uk, cilex.org.uk) before a real exam sitting.

#### CON.6 Vitiating factors: mistake, misrepresentation, duress and undue influence
**Mistake.** A vitiating factor that, where established, can render a contract **void** (as if it never existed, so no property can pass and third parties acquire no title) -- a high bar, narrowly applied. **Common mistake**: both parties share the same fundamental mistaken belief, e.g. as to the **existence of the subject matter** (*res extincta* -- the subject matter no longer exists, ***Couturier v Hastie***) or, exceptionally, its quality, where the mistake is so fundamental it renders the subject matter essentially different from what was believed (a very narrow doctrine post-***Great Peace Shipping v Tsavliris***). **Mutual mistake**: the parties are at cross-purposes, each meaning something different, such that no true agreement (consensus ad idem) was reached; the contract is void if, objectively, no reasonable interpretation of what was agreed can be identified (***Raffles v Wichelhaus***). **Unilateral mistake**: only one party is mistaken, and the other knows or ought to have known of the mistake, commonly involving **mistaken identity** -- where identity was a genuinely fundamental term of the contract (usually where parties contracted at a distance and the innocent party can show they intended to deal with someone else entirely), the contract may be void; where parties contracted face-to-face, there is a strong presumption they intended to contract with the person physically present, so the contract is more likely merely **voidable for fraud** (leaving a genuinely innocent third-party purchaser protected) rather than void (***Lewis v Averay***; contrast ***Cundy v Lindsay***).

**Misrepresentation.** A **false statement of existing fact or law** (not opinion, not a statement of future intention, though a statement of opinion by someone with special knowledge, or intention known to be false, can qualify) made by one party which **induces** the other to enter the contract, rendering it **voidable** (not void) -- the innocent party may rescind and/or claim damages. Categories, by the maker's state of mind: **fraudulent** (made knowingly, without belief in its truth, or recklessly as to truth -- ***Derry v Peek***; damages in the tort of deceit, and remoteness is wide); **negligent** (made carelessly, without reasonable grounds for belief in its truth) -- under **s2(1) Misrepresentation Act 1967**, once the claimant shows a false statement induced the contract, the burden shifts to the representor to prove they had reasonable grounds to believe it true, or damages follow (assessed as if fraudulent, i.e. generously); and **innocent** (made with a genuine and reasonable belief in its truth) -- rescission is the primary remedy, with damages **in lieu of rescission** available at the court's discretion under **s2(2)** of the 1967 Act. **Rescission** (setting the contract aside, restoring parties as far as possible to their pre-contract position) is an equitable remedy and can be **barred**: by affirmation (the innocent party, knowing of the misrepresentation, treats the contract as continuing), lapse of time, the intervention of **third-party rights** (a bona fide purchaser for value without notice), or where restitution is no longer possible (restitutio in integrum impossible).

**Duress and undue influence.** **Duress** renders a contract voidable where consent was obtained through illegitimate pressure -- traditionally physical (**duress to the person**), but also **economic duress**, requiring illegitimate pressure (e.g. an unlawful threat, or an unjustified threat to break an existing contract in a way that leaves no realistic practical alternative) which was a significant cause of the claimant entering the contract, leaving them no reasonable alternative but to comply. **Undue influence** is an equitable doctrine protecting a party whose free will was overborne through the abuse of a relationship of trust and confidence: **actual undue influence** (direct evidence of improper pressure, no special relationship needed) or **presumed undue influence** (arising from a recognised relationship of trust -- e.g. solicitor/client, doctor/patient -- or a relationship the claimant proves in fact involved trust and confidence, combined with a transaction calling for explanation because it is not readily explicable by the relationship, shifting the burden to the defendant to rebut the presumption, e.g. by showing the claimant received independent advice). Where a bank or other lender takes a guarantee/security from a person (e.g. a spouse) whose consent may have been procured by another's undue influence or misrepresentation, the lender is put **on inquiry** in certain circumstances and, to avoid being fixed with constructive notice and losing the security, should take reasonable steps such as ensuring the guarantor receives independent legal advice (***Royal Bank of Scotland v Etridge (No 2)***).
