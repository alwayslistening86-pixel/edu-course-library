# S12_Consideration_Intention_and_Capacity - Lesson: Formation of a valid contract: consideration, intention to create legal relations and capacity

## Goal
The learner explains the doctrine of consideration and its exception via promissory estoppel, the presumptions governing intention to create legal relations, and the rules on contractual capacity.

## Syllabus items taught here
- CON.3 - Formation: consideration, including promissory estoppel
- CON.4 - Formation: intention to create legal relations and capacity

## How to teach this
Ask: a struggling builder agrees to accept a smaller payment than owed to settle a debt, and the creditor later tries to sue for the rest. Can they? Teach each topic as: the rule (the statute section or case authority, stated precisely), why it exists (the policy/purpose CILEX's own module text gives), and how it is applied to facts. CILEX's Foundation-stage exams (F1-F4) are 60 multiple-choice and short 'automated' (fill-in/matching) questions testing precise, exam-ready knowledge -- push the learner to state rules exactly (case names, section numbers, dates) rather than paraphrase loosely. F5 and F6 also include practical scenario tasks (a communication/drafting exercise for F5; producing research documents for F6) -- give the learner realistic client scenarios and paralegal tasks, not just questions about the skill in the abstract. Throughout, reinforce that a CILEX trainee at this level must recognise the limits of their own authority and know when to escalate to a supervisor -- this is an explicit learning outcome in F2, F3 and F5, not incidental. Law changes: this course states the law as understood to be current in September 2026 (England and Wales); flag to the learner that specific figures (SDLT/LTT thresholds, IHT nil-rate bands, fee levels) and any pending reform (e.g. the Bill of Rights Bill, which CILEX's own F1 material lists as syllabus content but which had not been enacted -- the Human Rights Act 1998 remains in force) should be checked against a live source (legislation.gov.uk, gov.uk, cilex.org.uk) before a real exam sitting.

#### CON.3 Formation: consideration, including promissory estoppel
**Consideration.** For a simple contract (one not made by deed) to be binding, each party must provide consideration -- something of value given in exchange for the other's promise; a promise supported by no consideration is a **gratuitous promise**, not generally enforceable (unless made by deed). Key rules: consideration must **move from the promisee** but need not move to the promisor; it must be **sufficient** (of some recognisable value in the eyes of the law, even if very small -- famously a peppercorn) but need **not be adequate** (courts do not assess whether the bargain was a fair/equal exchange -- ***Chappell v Nestlé***); it must **not be past** -- an act done before the promise it is supposedly given in exchange for cannot generally be consideration for that later promise, unless the act was done at the promisor's request with an understanding that payment would follow (***Pao On v Lau Yiu Long***); performance of an **existing public duty** is not generally good consideration (unless the party does something over and above that duty -- ***Glasbrook Bros v Glamorgan CC***); performance of an **existing contractual duty owed to the same party** is not generally good consideration for a fresh promise of extra payment, unless a practical benefit is conferred on the promisor beyond the original bargain and the promise was not procured by duress (***Williams v Roffey Bros***); performance of an existing duty owed to a **third party** can be good consideration for a new promise; **part payment of a debt** is not generally good consideration for a promise to forgo the balance (the rule in ***Pinnel's Case***, affirmed in ***Foakes v Beer***), subject to recognised exceptions (e.g. payment at the creditor's request in a different form, earlier than due, or with something additional, or a genuine composition agreement with multiple creditors).

**Promissory estoppel.** An equitable doctrine mitigating the harshness of *Foakes v Beer*: where (1) there is an existing legal relationship/contract between the parties, (2) one party makes a **clear and unequivocal promise or representation** that they will not enforce their full strict legal rights, (3) the other party **relies** on that promise (detrimental reliance is not strictly required by all authority, but is the safer/traditional requirement to satisfy), and (4) it would be **inequitable** for the promisor to go back on the promise, the promisor is estopped (prevented) from enforcing their full original rights (***Central London Property Trust v High Trees House***). Promissory estoppel is generally **suspensory** rather than extinctive (it can be reversed on reasonable notice for future obligations, though may operate to permanently extinguish rights to past-due amounts already forgone) and operates only as a **"shield, not a sword"** -- it cannot by itself found a fresh cause of action, only act as a defence.

#### CON.4 Formation: intention to create legal relations and capacity
**Intention to create legal relations.** A contract requires both parties to have intended their agreement to be legally binding, not merely a social or moral arrangement. The courts apply **presumptions**: in a **social or domestic context** (family, friends), there is a rebuttable presumption of **no** intention to create legal relations (***Balfour v Balfour***), which can be rebutted by clear evidence to the contrary, particularly where the parties are not on good terms or have structured the arrangement formally (***Merritt v Merritt***). In a **commercial or business context**, there is a strong rebuttable presumption that the parties **do** intend to be legally bound, which can be rebutted by clear words (e.g. an express "subject to contract" or "binding in honour only" clause), but the burden on the party seeking to rebut it is heavy.

**Capacity.** The general rule is that a **minor** (under 18) lacks full contractual capacity: contracts for **necessaries** (goods/services suitable to the minor's condition in life and actual requirements at the time of the contract or delivery) are binding on the minor, as are generally beneficial **contracts of service/apprenticeship/education**; most other contracts are **voidable** at the minor's option (they can ratify or repudiate within a reasonable time after reaching majority). Persons **lacking mental capacity** (assessed under the **Mental Capacity Act 2005** -- unable to understand, retain, weigh or communicate a decision about the specific transaction) may have a contract voidable if the other party knew or ought to have known of the incapacity, though contracts for necessaries remain enforceable at a reasonable price. A person contracting **while intoxicated** to the extent they did not understand what they were doing, known to the other party, may similarly be able to avoid the contract, subject to ratification once sober, and remains liable for necessaries. **Corporations** generally have full capacity to contract within the scope of their constitution (modern company law limits the practical impact of acting outside stated objects on third parties), while **unincorporated associations** (e.g. some clubs) have no separate legal personality and contract through their members/officers.
