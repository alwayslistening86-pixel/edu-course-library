## Test items
1. Which of the following is the best statement of the rule that consideration must be sufficient but need not be adequate? Choose every correct option.
   A. The courts require consideration to represent a fair, roughly equal-value exchange between the parties
   B. Consideration must have some recognisable value in the eyes of the law, however small, but the courts will not assess whether the bargain was a good one
   C. Consideration is only valid if it is expressed in money
   D. Consideration must always exceed the value of the promise given in return
2. Explain the four requirements for promissory estoppel to arise, and why it is described as a 'shield, not a sword'. [8 marks]
3. A struggling small supplier owes a customer £10,000. The customer, worried about getting nothing if the supplier becomes insolvent, agrees in writing to accept £6,000 in full and final settlement, and the supplier pays that sum a week later. Six months on, once the supplier has recovered, the customer sues for the remaining £4,000. Using IRAC, advise the supplier. [12 marks]

## Answer key (for the tutor only)
1. Correct: B (exactly these options, no others)
2. [8] B1 there must be an existing legal relationship/contract between the parties; B1 a clear and unequivocal promise or representation that the promisor will not enforce their full strict legal rights; B1 reliance by the promisee on that promise; B1 it must be inequitable for the promisor to go back on the promise; B4 it is a 'shield, not a sword' because it operates only defensively, to prevent enforcement of the original strict right -- it cannot itself be used to found a new, independent cause of action or create rights that did not otherwise exist.
3. [12] Mark using issue-rule-application-conclusion (IRAC): identify the issue(s), state the rule precisely with its authority, apply it to the facts given, and reach a supported conclusion advising the client. Credit: issue = whether the promise to accept part payment in full settlement is enforceable, i.e. whether the customer can go back on it; rule = under Pinnel's Case/Foakes v Beer, part payment alone is not good consideration for forgoing the balance, so absent an exception the customer could in principle still sue; promissory estoppel (High Trees) may assist where there is a clear promise, reliance, and it would be inequitable to go back on it, though it is generally suspensory and a shield not a sword; application = the customer made a clear, unequivocal promise to accept £6,000 in full settlement, and the supplier relied on it by paying that sum; it may be inequitable to allow the customer to now claim the balance, particularly if the supplier's position changed in reliance (though recovery of the business might reduce the equity in the supplier's favour, and estoppel may only suspend rather than extinguish the right, especially if the customer gave reasonable notice going forward); conclusion = the supplier has a real argument that the customer is estopped from claiming the outstanding £4,000, but this depends on the strength of reliance and the inequity of allowing the customer to resile, and is not guaranteed to be a complete, permanent bar.
