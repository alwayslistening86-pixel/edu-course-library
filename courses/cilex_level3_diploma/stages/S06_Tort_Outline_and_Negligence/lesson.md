# S06_Tort_Outline_and_Negligence - Lesson: Tort law in outline and the tort of negligence

## Goal
The learner explains the function and protected interests of tort law, and the elements of negligence: duty of care, breach, causation and remoteness, including res ipsa loquitur.

## Syllabus items taught here
- TORT.1 - Tort law in outline: its function, and interests it protects including under the Human Rights Act 1998
- TORT.2 - Negligence: duty of care, breach, causation, remoteness, res ipsa loquitur

## How to teach this
Ask: a cyclist swerves to avoid a pothole and hits a pedestrian. Who, if anyone, is liable, and what would you need to prove? Teach each topic as: the rule (the statute section or case authority, stated precisely), why it exists (the policy/purpose CILEX's own module text gives), and how it is applied to facts. CILEX's Foundation-stage exams (F1-F4) are 60 multiple-choice and short 'automated' (fill-in/matching) questions testing precise, exam-ready knowledge -- push the learner to state rules exactly (case names, section numbers, dates) rather than paraphrase loosely. F5 and F6 also include practical scenario tasks (a communication/drafting exercise for F5; producing research documents for F6) -- give the learner realistic client scenarios and paralegal tasks, not just questions about the skill in the abstract. Throughout, reinforce that a CILEX trainee at this level must recognise the limits of their own authority and know when to escalate to a supervisor -- this is an explicit learning outcome in F2, F3 and F5, not incidental. Law changes: this course states the law as understood to be current in September 2026 (England and Wales); flag to the learner that specific figures (SDLT/LTT thresholds, IHT nil-rate bands, fee levels) and any pending reform (e.g. the Bill of Rights Bill, which CILEX's own F1 material lists as syllabus content but which had not been enacted -- the Human Rights Act 1998 remains in force) should be checked against a live source (legislation.gov.uk, gov.uk, cilex.org.uk) before a real exam sitting.

#### TORT.1 Tort law in outline: its function, and interests it protects including under the Human Rights Act 1998
**Tort law in outline.** Tort is part of civil law; a tort is a civil wrong (independent of contract) giving rise to a cause of action for a remedy, usually damages. Its purpose is to compensate claimants for loss caused by another's wrongful conduct and, secondarily, to deter such conduct. It protects: the **rights of individuals and organisations**, including rights recognised under the **Human Rights Act 1998** (e.g. **Article 6**, fair trial, relevant to access to a remedy; **Article 8**, private/family life, increasingly relevant to privacy-related and some nuisance/data claims); **property interests** (personal property and land, via trespass/nuisance/negligence); and **economic interests** (financial loss, though pure economic loss is treated cautiously in negligence, see below).

#### TORT.2 Negligence: duty of care, breach, causation, remoteness, res ipsa loquitur
**Negligence.** Developed from case-by-case common law, negligence requires: **(1) a duty of care** -- classically tested by the "neighbour principle", now refined through **reasonable foreseeability** of injury, sufficient **proximity** between claimant and defendant, and whether it is **fair, just and reasonable** to impose a duty (the *Caparo* three-stage test), though many relationships fall into **established categories of duty** (e.g. road users to other road users, employers to employees, doctors to patients) where a duty is simply recognised. Duties can arise from **acts** more readily than **omissions** (the law is generally cautious about liability for pure inaction absent an assumed responsibility or special relationship). **(2) Breach** -- judged against the standard of the **reasonable person**; a defendant with a **particular professional or recognised skill** (medical staff, lawyers, financial advisers, surveyors) is judged against the standard of a reasonably competent member of that profession, not an ordinary person. Factors relevant to breach: the **magnitude of risk** (likelihood of injury and seriousness of likely harm), the **cost/practicability of precautions**, and the **social utility/purpose** of the defendant's conduct. **(3) Causation and damage**: **factual causation** uses the **"but for" test** -- would the harm have occurred but for the defendant's breach; a **novus actus interveniens** (an intervening act, by the claimant or a third party, sufficiently independent and unforeseeable) can break the chain of causation. **(4) Remoteness of damage** -- a defendant is only liable for damage of a type that was **reasonably foreseeable** (foresight, not hindsight); special rules include the **"egg-shell skull" rule** (the defendant takes the claimant as found, so is liable for the full extent of an unexpectedly severe injury of a foreseeable type, even if the claimant's own vulnerability increased its severity) and specific protection for **rescuers** who are injured intervening (their intervention is generally treated as foreseeable, not a novus actus). **Res ipsa loquitur** ("the thing speaks for itself") allows an inference of negligence where an accident is of a kind that would not normally happen without negligence, occurred in circumstances under the defendant's management/control, and there is no other explanation; the defendant can rebut the inference by giving a reasonable, non-negligent explanation. Negligence increasingly intersects with **cybersecurity** (e.g. a professional's negligent handling of client data/systems).
