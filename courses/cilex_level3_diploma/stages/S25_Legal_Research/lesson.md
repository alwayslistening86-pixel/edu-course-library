# S25_Legal_Research - Lesson: Conducting legal research: sources, citation, analysis and ethical use

## Goal
The learner explains the scope and sources of legal research, how to cite primary legal sources correctly, and how to apply and report research findings ethically and accurately.

## Syllabus items taught here
- PLS.8 - Conducting legal research: scope and sources, citing primary sources, applying and reporting research ethically

## How to teach this
Ask: a client asks whether a particular Court of Appeal case from a few years ago is still good law. How would a trainee actually go about finding out, and how would they show their working? Teach each topic as: the rule (the statute section or case authority, stated precisely), why it exists (the policy/purpose CILEX's own module text gives), and how it is applied to facts. CILEX's Foundation-stage exams (F1-F4) are 60 multiple-choice and short 'automated' (fill-in/matching) questions testing precise, exam-ready knowledge -- push the learner to state rules exactly (case names, section numbers, dates) rather than paraphrase loosely. F5 and F6 also include practical scenario tasks (a communication/drafting exercise for F5; producing research documents for F6) -- give the learner realistic client scenarios and paralegal tasks, not just questions about the skill in the abstract. Throughout, reinforce that a CILEX trainee at this level must recognise the limits of their own authority and know when to escalate to a supervisor -- this is an explicit learning outcome in F2, F3 and F5, not incidental. Law changes: this course states the law as understood to be current in September 2026 (England and Wales); flag to the learner that specific figures (SDLT/LTT thresholds, IHT nil-rate bands, fee levels) and any pending reform (e.g. the Bill of Rights Bill, which CILEX's own F1 material lists as syllabus content but which had not been enacted -- the Human Rights Act 1998 remains in force) should be checked against a live source (legislation.gov.uk, gov.uk, cilex.org.uk) before a real exam sitting.

#### PLS.8 Conducting legal research: scope and sources, citing primary sources, applying and reporting research ethically
**Conducting legal research.** Effective legal research begins with correctly **scoping the question** -- identifying the precise legal issue(s), the relevant jurisdiction (England and Wales, unless otherwise indicated) and area of law, and what the client actually needs to know, before searching. **Primary sources** are the law itself: legislation (Acts of Parliament and secondary legislation, authoritative text available free via **legislation.gov.uk**, which shows both the original enacted version and, where updated, the current version "as amended") and case law (judgments, available via free sources such as the **National Archives' Find Case Law service (caselaw.nationalarchives.gov.uk)**, and via subscription legal databases such as **Westlaw** and **LexisNexis/Lexis+**, which also provide case analysis, citator tools showing subsequent judicial treatment, and cross-referencing to related material). **Secondary sources** explain, summarise or comment on the law without being the law itself: practitioner textbooks, legal encyclopaedias (e.g. Halsbury's Laws of England), academic commentary, and professional body guidance (e.g. Law Society Practice Notes, CILEX guidance) -- valuable for orientation and context, but never a substitute for checking the actual primary source before relying on a proposition in client advice. A **citator** (a specific tool within Westlaw/Lexis+, and separately available via Find Case Law's own case-history features) shows whether a case has subsequently been **followed, applied, distinguished, doubted or overruled**, essential for confirming whether older authority remains good law -- particularly important given the doctrine of precedent (S07) and the possibility that even a binding lower-court decision has since been overtaken by a later, higher-court ruling.

**Citing primary sources correctly.** A **case citation** in modern practice uses the **neutral citation** (assigned by the court itself, court/database-neutral, e.g. *Smith v Jones* **[2019] EWCA Civ 123**, showing the year, court and sequential case number) alongside, where relevant, a **law report citation** (e.g. [2019] 2 WLR 456, referencing a named series of law reports, volume and page) -- party names are conventionally italicised. A **statute citation** references the Act's short title and year (e.g. the **Consumer Rights Act 2015**), with the specific provision identified by section (**s**), subsection, and paragraph as needed (e.g. s9(1)(a)); secondary legislation is cited by its title and Statutory Instrument number (e.g. SI 2017/692). Accurate, consistent citation allows any reader (a supervisor, a court, another practitioner) to locate and verify the exact source relied on, and is itself a mark of professional competence.

**Applying and reporting research ethically.** Research findings must be **applied accurately** to the client's actual facts, not overstated or selectively presented to support a predetermined conclusion -- an ethical obligation as much as a competence one, since advice built on a mischaracterised or incomplete legal picture can cause real client harm. A trainee must **report the limits of their research honestly**: flagging where the law is genuinely unclear, unsettled, or where authority conflicts, rather than presenting an uncertain position as settled; acknowledging where a question falls outside the trainee's competence or requires more senior input; and never fabricating or embellishing a source, including never citing a case or provision without having actually verified it exists and says what is claimed (particularly relevant given the risk of AI-generated tools producing plausible-sounding but fictitious citations, covered in S23). Research should be recorded/reported in a way a supervisor or colleague can follow and check -- showing the sources consulted, the reasoning applied, and, for a scenario/client matter, a clear, supported conclusion in the IRAC style used throughout this course.
