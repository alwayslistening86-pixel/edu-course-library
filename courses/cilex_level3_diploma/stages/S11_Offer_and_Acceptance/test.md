## Test items
1. A seller posts a letter revoking an offer on Monday. The buyer posts a letter of acceptance on Tuesday, before receiving the revocation, which arrives Wednesday. Is there a contract? Choose every correct option.
   A. Yes -- the postal rule makes acceptance effective on posting (Tuesday), and revocation is only effective on actual communication (Wednesday), so the binding acceptance came first
   B. No -- revocation was posted first, so it takes priority
   C. No -- the postal rule does not apply to revocation or acceptance of offers to sell goods
   D. Yes, but only from Wednesday, when both letters had been received
2. Explain why the postal rule does not apply to acceptance sent by email or through an interactive website, and what rule applies instead. [6 marks]
3. A customer orders a television from an online retailer's website; the site immediately sends an automated email stating 'we have received your order'. The retailer later emails to say the television is out of stock and the order cannot be fulfilled. Using IRAC, advise the customer on whether a contract was formed. [10 marks]

## Answer key (for the tutor only)
1. Correct: A (exactly these options, no others)
2. [6] B2 the postal rule is a historic exception justified by the inherent delay/unreliability of post as a non-instantaneous method; B2 email and website transactions are treated as broadly instantaneous methods of communication, so the postal rule's rationale does not apply; B2 instead, acceptance takes effect when and where it is actually received by the offeror (Entores v Miles Far East Corp), subject to business-hours principles for messages received outside normal working hours.
3. [10] Mark using issue-rule-application-conclusion (IRAC): identify the issue(s), state the rule precisely with its authority, apply it to the facts given, and reach a supported conclusion advising the client. Credit: issue = whether the automated order-received email constituted acceptance, forming a binding contract; rule = an online order is typically structured as the customer making an offer, with the trader's dispatch confirmation (not a bare acknowledgement of receipt) constituting acceptance, consistent with the Electronic Commerce Regulations 2002's separate information/acknowledgement duties; a mirror-image, communicated acceptance is required to form a contract; application = an automated 'order received' email is ordinarily only an acknowledgement of receipt, not a dispatch confirmation, and does not itself demonstrate the retailer's final, unqualified agreement to supply that specific item; conclusion = no binding contract was formed at the acknowledgement stage, so the retailer can lawfully decline to supply the (likely then non-existent, since out of stock) television, though the website's own terms should be checked for any different express wording.
