# S11_Offer_and_Acceptance - Lesson: Formation of a valid contract: offer and acceptance

## Goal
The learner explains the rules for a valid offer and its termination, the rules for valid acceptance including the postal rule, and how these apply to new and electronic methods of contract formation.

## Syllabus items taught here
- CON.2 - Formation: offer and acceptance, including new methods of contract formation

## How to teach this
Ask: a customer clicks 'buy now' on a website and receives an automatic email confirming the order was received. Is that a binding contract yet? Teach each topic as: the rule (the statute section or case authority, stated precisely), why it exists (the policy/purpose CILEX's own module text gives), and how it is applied to facts. CILEX's Foundation-stage exams (F1-F4) are 60 multiple-choice and short 'automated' (fill-in/matching) questions testing precise, exam-ready knowledge -- push the learner to state rules exactly (case names, section numbers, dates) rather than paraphrase loosely. F5 and F6 also include practical scenario tasks (a communication/drafting exercise for F5; producing research documents for F6) -- give the learner realistic client scenarios and paralegal tasks, not just questions about the skill in the abstract. Throughout, reinforce that a CILEX trainee at this level must recognise the limits of their own authority and know when to escalate to a supervisor -- this is an explicit learning outcome in F2, F3 and F5, not incidental. Law changes: this course states the law as understood to be current in September 2026 (England and Wales); flag to the learner that specific figures (SDLT/LTT thresholds, IHT nil-rate bands, fee levels) and any pending reform (e.g. the Bill of Rights Bill, which CILEX's own F1 material lists as syllabus content but which had not been enacted -- the Human Rights Act 1998 remains in force) should be checked against a live source (legislation.gov.uk, gov.uk, cilex.org.uk) before a real exam sitting.

#### CON.2 Formation: offer and acceptance, including new methods of contract formation
**Offer.** An offer is a clear statement of terms made with the intention that it becomes binding as soon as it is accepted (objectively assessed, per ***Smith v Hughes***). It must be distinguished from an **invitation to treat** -- an indication of willingness to negotiate or receive offers, not itself capable of acceptance -- including goods displayed in a shop window or on a shelf (***Pharmaceutical Society of GB v Boots***, the customer makes the offer at the till, which the trader accepts or rejects), advertisements for the sale of goods generally (***Partridge v Crittenden***, though a unilateral offer to the world, such as in ***Carlill v Carbolic Smoke Ball Co***, can be a genuine offer), and the display of goods at auction (the auctioneer's call for bids is an invitation to treat; each bid is an offer, accepted by the fall of the hammer). An offer may be terminated before acceptance by: **revocation** (withdrawal by the offeror, effective only once actually communicated to the offeree, even via a reliable third party -- ***Dickinson v Dodds***; a promise to keep an offer open is not binding unless supported by consideration, e.g. an option); **rejection** (including a **counter-offer**, which destroys the original offer -- ***Hyde v Wrench***; a mere request for information is not a counter-offer); **lapse of time** (after a stated period, or a reasonable time if none is stated); **failure of a condition** the offer was subject to; or the **death** of either party in some circumstances.

**Acceptance.** A final, unqualified agreement to all the terms of the offer, generally communicated to the offeror (a "mirror image" of the offer; acceptance introducing new terms is a counter-offer, not acceptance). **Silence cannot generally constitute acceptance** (***Felthouse v Bindley***). Acceptance must usually be communicated to take effect, but the **postal rule** is an exception: where post is a reasonable/contemplated method of communication, acceptance is complete and binding the moment the letter is properly posted, even if it is delayed or never arrives (***Adams v Lindsell***), unless the offer expressly excludes the postal rule or requires actual receipt. **New and electronic methods of contract formation**: for instantaneous methods (telephone, fax, and generally email and website transactions), acceptance takes effect when and where it is actually received by the offeror (the postal rule does not apply), subject to business-hours principles for when receipt is treated as effective outside normal working hours (***Entores v Miles Far East Corp***; ***The Brimnes***). Online retail transactions are typically structured so the customer's order is an offer and the trader's dispatch confirmation (rather than an automatic order-acknowledgement email) constitutes acceptance, consistent with the **Electronic Commerce (EC Directive) Regulations 2002**, which impose information and acknowledgement-of-receipt duties on online traders but leave the underlying contract law of offer and acceptance unchanged. Electronic signatures are recognised as capable of validly executing many contracts under the **Electronic Communications Act 2000** and (for deeds and certain formal documents) subject to specific execution formalities.
