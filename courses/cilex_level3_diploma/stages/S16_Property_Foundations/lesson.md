# S16_Property_Foundations - Lesson: Organisations, underpinning land law, documents, co-ownership and regulatory matters

## Goal
The learner identifies the organisations relevant to conveyancing/probate practice, the underpinning land law (estates, interests, title, third-party rights), key documents, co-ownership, and core regulatory matters including AML.

## Syllabus items taught here
- PPC.1 - Organisations relevant to conveyancing and probate practice
- PPC.2 - Underpinning land law: estates, interests, registered/unregistered title, third-party rights
- PPC.3 - Technical basics: documents -- contracts, deeds, wills and codicils, statutory declarations
- PPC.4 - Co-ownership: joint tenancy, tenancy in common, overreaching, conflicts of interest
- PPC.5 - Regulatory matters: client care, undertakings, money laundering, taxation awareness

## How to teach this
Ask: two unmarried partners buy a house together, contributing unequal deposits, and never discuss what happens if they split up. What actually happens to the ownership? Teach each topic as: the rule (the statute section or case authority, stated precisely), why it exists (the policy/purpose CILEX's own module text gives), and how it is applied to facts. CILEX's Foundation-stage exams (F1-F4) are 60 multiple-choice and short 'automated' (fill-in/matching) questions testing precise, exam-ready knowledge -- push the learner to state rules exactly (case names, section numbers, dates) rather than paraphrase loosely. F5 and F6 also include practical scenario tasks (a communication/drafting exercise for F5; producing research documents for F6) -- give the learner realistic client scenarios and paralegal tasks, not just questions about the skill in the abstract. Throughout, reinforce that a CILEX trainee at this level must recognise the limits of their own authority and know when to escalate to a supervisor -- this is an explicit learning outcome in F2, F3 and F5, not incidental. Law changes: this course states the law as understood to be current in September 2026 (England and Wales); flag to the learner that specific figures (SDLT/LTT thresholds, IHT nil-rate bands, fee levels) and any pending reform (e.g. the Bill of Rights Bill, which CILEX's own F1 material lists as syllabus content but which had not been enacted -- the Human Rights Act 1998 remains in force) should be checked against a live source (legislation.gov.uk, gov.uk, cilex.org.uk) before a real exam sitting.

#### PPC.1 Organisations relevant to conveyancing and probate practice
**Organisations relevant to conveyancing and probate practice.** **HM Land Registry (HMLR)** maintains the register of title for registered land in England and Wales, records dealings (transfers, mortgages, charges), and guarantees registered title (subject to limited exceptions). **HM Revenue & Customs (HMRC)** administers Stamp Duty Land Tax (on English land transactions), Inheritance Tax, and Capital Gains Tax. The **Welsh Revenue Authority** administers Land Transaction Tax (LTT) on Welsh land transactions, devolved since 2018. The **Law Society** represents and regulates guidance for solicitors (and its Conveyancing Quality Scheme accredits conveyancing practices), publishes the **Standard Conditions of Sale** and the **Code for Completion by Post**; **CILEX** regulates Chartered Legal Executives (through CILEX Regulation) working in this area. The **Probate Registry** (part of HM Courts & Tribunals Service, within the Family Division) issues grants of representation (probate/letters of administration). Local authorities respond to **local land charges searches** and hold planning/building control records. Other relevant bodies include mortgage lenders (and their conveyancers, sometimes via panel arrangements), the **Council for Licensed Conveyancers** (regulates licensed conveyancers), the **Financial Conduct Authority** (mortgage/insurance-related regulation), and search providers (for drainage, environmental, coal mining and other specialist searches).

#### PPC.2 Underpinning land law: estates, interests, registered/unregistered title, third-party rights
**Underpinning land law.** English land law recognises two **legal estates** capable of existing "at law" per the **Law of Property Act 1925**: the **freehold** (fee simple absolute in possession -- ownership without a fixed time limit) and the **leasehold** (term of years absolute -- ownership for a fixed, certain maximum duration, subject to a landlord's reversion). **Title** is either **registered** (the norm today; ownership and most interests are recorded on HMLR's register, triggered by events such as a sale, first mortgage or gift of unregistered land) or **unregistered** (title proved by an unbroken chain of historic deeds -- an **epitome/abstract of title** -- going back at least 15 years to a **good root of title**, increasingly rare as first registration has become near-universal). **Third-party interests** in land bind successive owners in different ways: **legal interests** (e.g. a legal easement, a legal mortgage/charge) generally bind the whole world once validly created/registered; **equitable interests** (e.g. a restrictive covenant, an equitable easement, a beneficial interest under a trust, an estate contract) bind a purchaser only if properly protected -- for registered land, generally by an entry (a **notice** or **restriction**) on the register, or, exceptionally, as an **overriding interest** (binding even without registration, e.g. the rights of a person in **actual occupation**, or certain legal easements) under the **Land Registration Act 2002**; for unregistered land, many equitable interests must be protected by registration as a **land charge** under the **Land Charges Act 1972**, or (for some older interests) depend on the doctrine of notice.

#### PPC.3 Technical basics: documents -- contracts, deeds, wills and codicils, statutory declarations
**Documents used in conveyancing and probate practice.** A **contract** for the sale of land must, under **s2 Law of Property (Miscellaneous Provisions) Act 1989**, be in writing, incorporate all agreed terms (expressly or by reference), and be signed by or on behalf of both parties, typically in identical "part" documents exchanged between the parties' conveyancers. A **deed** is required to create or transfer a legal estate in land (e.g. a **TR1** transfer, a legal mortgage/charge, a lease over seven years): under the **Law of Property (Miscellaneous Provisions) Act 1989 s1**, a deed must be in writing, make clear on its face it is intended to be a deed, and be validly executed -- signed in the presence of a witness who attests the signature (or, for a company, executed per the Companies Act 2006). A **will** must, under **s9 Wills Act 1837** (as amended), be in writing, signed by the testator (or by another at their direction, in their presence), with the testator intending by that signature to give effect to the will, and that signature made or acknowledged in the presence of two witnesses present at the same time, who then each sign or acknowledge their signature in the testator's presence. A **codicil** is a supplementary document amending an existing will (e.g. adding or changing a gift), executed with the same formalities as a will, and is often now superseded in practice by simply making a new will. A **statutory declaration** is a formal written statement of fact, made under the **Statutory Declarations Act 1835**, sworn or affirmed before an authorised person (e.g. a solicitor or commissioner for oaths), used in conveyancing/probate to evidence facts where no other documentary proof exists (e.g. a declaration as to identity, missing title deeds, or years of undisputed possession).

#### PPC.4 Co-ownership: joint tenancy, tenancy in common, overreaching, conflicts of interest
**Co-ownership.** Where land is owned by more than one person, they hold the **legal estate** as **joint tenants** on a **trust of land** (under the **Trusts of Land and Appointment of Trustees Act 1996**) -- legal joint tenancy cannot be severed and always carries the **right of survivorship** (on the death of one legal joint tenant, the legal estate automatically vests in the survivor(s), outside any will). The **beneficial (equitable) interest**, held behind that trust, may be held as either: **beneficial joint tenants** (each owns the whole notionally together, with the right of survivorship applying on death -- no individual "share" to leave by will) or **tenants in common** (each owns a distinct, quantifiable share, which does not pass by survivorship but forms part of their estate on death, distributable under their will or the intestacy rules). The "four unities" (possession, interest, title, time) are traditionally required for a joint tenancy; their absence, or an express declaration of shares, points to a tenancy in common. A beneficial joint tenancy can be **severed** into a tenancy in common during the co-owners' lifetimes (e.g. by written notice under **s36(2) Law of Property Act 1925**, by an act operating on the co-owner's own share such as a sale or mortgage of it, by mutual agreement, or by conduct evincing a common intention to sever). **Overreaching**: a purchaser paying the purchase price to **at least two trustees** (or a trust corporation) takes the legal estate free of the beneficiaries' equitable interests, which are instead detached from the land and swept into the proceeds of sale -- protecting a purchaser from undisclosed beneficial co-owners, provided the statutory requirement of two trustees is met (a sole surviving trustee generally cannot overreach). Co-ownership situations can raise **conflicts of interest** for the conveyancer -- e.g. acting for co-buyers with different funding contributions or differing views on how to hold the beneficial interest -- requiring separate advice on joint tenancy versus tenancy in common (and often a **declaration of trust**/deed of trust recording unequal contributions), and careful identification of whether the practitioner can act for both parties at all.

#### PPC.5 Regulatory matters: client care, undertakings, money laundering, taxation awareness
**Regulatory matters in conveyancing and probate practice.** **Client care**: at the outset of a retainer, the client must be given clear information about the scope of the matter, the fee earner responsible, costs (an estimate and how/when charged), the complaints procedure, and who regulates the firm -- consistent with the applicable regulator's Code of Conduct (SRA Code of Conduct; CILEX Regulation's equivalent outcomes). **Undertakings**: a formal, usually written promise by a solicitor/legal executive (or their firm) to do (or not do) something, or to cause something to happen, commonly used in conveyancing (e.g. an undertaking to redeem an existing mortgage on completion, or to hold a deposit as stakeholder) -- an undertaking is a serious, personally binding professional obligation, strictly enforced by the regulator, and must never be given carelessly or beyond what can actually be delivered. **Money laundering**: conveyancing and probate are recognised as areas of higher money-laundering risk (large sums, valuable assets). Firms must comply with the **Money Laundering, Terrorist Financing and Transfer of Funds (Information on the Payer) Regulations 2017** (as amended, including by the 2019 Amendment Regulations and subsequent updates) -- conducting **client due diligence** (verifying client identity and, for corporate/trust clients, beneficial ownership) before establishing a business relationship, applying **enhanced due diligence** for higher-risk situations (e.g. politically exposed persons, complex/unusually large transactions, cash-intensive sources of funds), maintaining records, and reporting **suspicious activity** to the firm's Money Laundering Reporting Officer, who assesses whether to submit a **Suspicious Activity Report** to the **National Crime Agency**; a trainee has a **personal** duty here and must never "tip off" a client that a report has been made. **Taxation awareness**: a conveyancer/probate practitioner should recognise (without necessarily giving detailed tax advice) when a transaction has tax implications requiring specialist input or client referral -- Stamp Duty Land Tax/Land Transaction Tax on a purchase, Capital Gains Tax on a disposal of property that is not the seller's only/main residence, and Inheritance Tax implications arising on death or lifetime gifts.
