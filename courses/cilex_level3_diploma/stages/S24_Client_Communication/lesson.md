# S24_Client_Communication - Lesson: Communicating with internal and external clients

## Goal
The learner explains how to communicate effectively with internal and external clients using verbal, non-verbal and written methods, and how to use precedent correspondence appropriately.

## Syllabus items taught here
- PLS.7 - Communication with internal and external clients: verbal, non-verbal, written, and precedent correspondence

## How to teach this
Ask: a trainee needs to explain to a worried client why their case is taking longer than expected. What should that conversation actually look and sound like? Teach each topic as: the rule (the statute section or case authority, stated precisely), why it exists (the policy/purpose CILEX's own module text gives), and how it is applied to facts. CILEX's Foundation-stage exams (F1-F4) are 60 multiple-choice and short 'automated' (fill-in/matching) questions testing precise, exam-ready knowledge -- push the learner to state rules exactly (case names, section numbers, dates) rather than paraphrase loosely. F5 and F6 also include practical scenario tasks (a communication/drafting exercise for F5; producing research documents for F6) -- give the learner realistic client scenarios and paralegal tasks, not just questions about the skill in the abstract. Throughout, reinforce that a CILEX trainee at this level must recognise the limits of their own authority and know when to escalate to a supervisor -- this is an explicit learning outcome in F2, F3 and F5, not incidental. Law changes: this course states the law as understood to be current in September 2026 (England and Wales); flag to the learner that specific figures (SDLT/LTT thresholds, IHT nil-rate bands, fee levels) and any pending reform (e.g. the Bill of Rights Bill, which CILEX's own F1 material lists as syllabus content but which had not been enacted -- the Human Rights Act 1998 remains in force) should be checked against a live source (legislation.gov.uk, gov.uk, cilex.org.uk) before a real exam sitting.

#### PLS.7 Communication with internal and external clients: verbal, non-verbal, written, and precedent correspondence
**Communicating with internal and external clients.** Effective legal communication adapts to the audience, the purpose and the method used. **Verbal communication** (in person, by telephone, or via video call) allows immediate clarification and rapport-building, but requires active listening, a measured pace, avoiding jargon, checking understanding (e.g. by summarising back), and being alert to the client's emotional state (many clients contact a lawyer at a stressful time -- bereavement, dispute, financial difficulty), responding with appropriate empathy alongside professionalism. **Non-verbal communication** includes tone of voice, body language, eye contact and appearance, all of which affect how a message and the trainee's professionalism are perceived, whether in person or (to a more limited degree) in the tone and formatting choices of written or video communication. **Written communication** (letters, emails, file notes, attendance notes) must be clear, accurate, appropriately formal, and proportionate to its purpose -- a formal client care letter differs in tone and structure from a brief internal email, but both must be precise and professionally presented; written communication also creates a **permanent record**, so accuracy and care in drafting matter for evidential as well as service reasons. **Precedent correspondence** (standard-form letters, clauses or templates maintained by a firm for recurring situations, e.g. a standard client care letter, a standard letter before action, or a standard reporting-on-title letter) improves consistency, efficiency and completeness (ensuring nothing standard is missed), but a trainee must always **tailor** a precedent to the specific client and facts rather than sending it unamended -- an unadapted precedent risks including wrong names, irrelevant/incorrect content, or omitting a matter-specific point the client needed to be told, and using one without genuine understanding of its content and purpose falls short of the standard of competence expected.
