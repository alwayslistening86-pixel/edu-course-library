# S07_Employers_and_Occupiers_Liability - Lesson: Employers' liability and occupiers' liability

## Goal
The learner explains the scope of an employer's statutory and common law duty to employees, vicarious liability for acts of employees/workers, and occupiers' liability to visitors and trespassers under the 1957 and 1984 Acts.

## Syllabus items taught here
- TORT.3 - Employers' liability: statutory and common law duties, vicarious liability
- TORT.4 - Occupiers' liability: Occupiers' Liability Acts 1957 and 1984

## How to teach this
Ask: a delivery driver, while on their round, assaults a member of the public in a dispute over parking. Could the employer be liable for something the driver did entirely off their own initiative? Teach each topic as: the rule (the statute section or case authority, stated precisely), why it exists (the policy/purpose CILEX's own module text gives), and how it is applied to facts. CILEX's Foundation-stage exams (F1-F4) are 60 multiple-choice and short 'automated' (fill-in/matching) questions testing precise, exam-ready knowledge -- push the learner to state rules exactly (case names, section numbers, dates) rather than paraphrase loosely. F5 and F6 also include practical scenario tasks (a communication/drafting exercise for F5; producing research documents for F6) -- give the learner realistic client scenarios and paralegal tasks, not just questions about the skill in the abstract. Throughout, reinforce that a CILEX trainee at this level must recognise the limits of their own authority and know when to escalate to a supervisor -- this is an explicit learning outcome in F2, F3 and F5, not incidental. Law changes: this course states the law as understood to be current in September 2026 (England and Wales); flag to the learner that specific figures (SDLT/LTT thresholds, IHT nil-rate bands, fee levels) and any pending reform (e.g. the Bill of Rights Bill, which CILEX's own F1 material lists as syllabus content but which had not been enacted -- the Human Rights Act 1998 remains in force) should be checked against a live source (legislation.gov.uk, gov.uk, cilex.org.uk) before a real exam sitting.

#### TORT.3 Employers' liability: statutory and common law duties, vicarious liability
**Employers' liability.** Employers owe employees duties arising from several sources: **statute** -- the **Health and Safety at Work etc. Act 1974** imposes a general duty on employers to ensure, so far as reasonably practicable, the health, safety and welfare of employees, supplemented by specific regulations such as the **Management of Health and Safety at Work Regulations 1999** (requiring risk assessment), assessed against a standard of reasonableness; **common law duty** -- a non-delegable duty to take reasonable care for employees' safety (safe system of work, competent staff, safe equipment, safe premises); **contractual duty** -- implied terms in the contract of employment. **Defences** available mirror general tort defences (e.g. contributory negligence). An employer can also be liable for the negligence of **independent contractors** it engages in limited circumstances (e.g. where it retains control, or the work is inherently hazardous), though generally liability for an independent contractor's own negligence rests with the contractor. **Vicarious liability** makes an employer strictly liable for torts committed by an employee **in the course of employment**, even though the employer itself was not at fault: it requires (1) an employment relationship, extended to **relationships "akin to employment"** and certain **worker** arrangements where the worker is sufficiently integrated into the employer's enterprise; and (2) that the wrongful act was committed in the **course of employment** -- a "close connection" test, so even unauthorised acts (dishonesty, disobedience, even some criminal activity) can fall within course of employment if closely connected to what the employee was authorised to do.

#### TORT.4 Occupiers' liability: Occupiers' Liability Acts 1957 and 1984
**Occupiers' liability.** Builds on negligence but is now largely governed by statute. **Occupiers' Liability Act 1957** governs the duty owed to **visitors** (those with express or implied permission to be on the premises): the **occupier** (a person with sufficient control over the premises, not necessarily the owner) owes a **common duty of care** to take reasonable care that the visitor will be reasonably safe using the premises for the purposes for which they are permitted to be there. Relevant factors: **time-limited permission** (a visitor exceeding permitted time may become a trespasser), **geographical/space restrictions** (going beyond the area of permission), **warnings** (a clear, adequate warning can discharge the duty) and **obvious risks** (an occupier need not warn of risks that are obvious). **Child visitors** are owed a higher standard -- premises must be reasonably safe for a child of that age, and account must be taken of **allurements** (features attractive but dangerous to children). An occupier may discharge liability for the work of a competent **independent contractor** if it was reasonable to entrust the work to them and reasonable steps (e.g. checking competence/insurance) were taken. **Limitations on liability**: exclusion of the duty by notice is subject to the **Unfair Contract Terms Act 1977** (cannot exclude liability for death/personal injury from negligence in a business context) and, for some claims, the **Consumer Rights Act 2015**; the **Law Reform (Personal Injuries) Act 1948** is also relevant to certain historic exclusion issues. **Occupiers' Liability Act 1984** governs the duty owed to **trespassers** (and others not owed a duty under the 1957 Act) -- a duty arises only where the occupier is aware of the danger (or has reasonable grounds to believe it exists), knows or has reasonable grounds to believe someone may be in the vicinity of the danger, and the risk is one the occupier could reasonably be expected to offer some protection against; there is generally no duty regarding risks that are **obvious**.
