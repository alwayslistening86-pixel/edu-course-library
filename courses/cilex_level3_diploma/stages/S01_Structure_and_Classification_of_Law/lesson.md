# S01_Structure_and_Classification_of_Law - Lesson: The structure and classification of law: civil/criminal, common law/equity

## Goal
The learner explains how law is classified (civil/criminal, common law/equity), why the distinction matters for burden of proof, objectives, remedies and citation, and the origin and modern role of equity.

## Syllabus items taught here
- LS.1 - The structure and classification of law: civil/criminal, common law/equity, the Judicature Acts 1873-75

## How to teach this
Ask: if a shopkeeper is both prosecuted for selling out-of-date food and sued by a customer who was made ill by it, why are these two completely separate cases with different rules? Teach each topic as: the rule (the statute section or case authority, stated precisely), why it exists (the policy/purpose CILEX's own module text gives), and how it is applied to facts. CILEX's Foundation-stage exams (F1-F4) are 60 multiple-choice and short 'automated' (fill-in/matching) questions testing precise, exam-ready knowledge -- push the learner to state rules exactly (case names, section numbers, dates) rather than paraphrase loosely. F5 and F6 also include practical scenario tasks (a communication/drafting exercise for F5; producing research documents for F6) -- give the learner realistic client scenarios and paralegal tasks, not just questions about the skill in the abstract. Throughout, reinforce that a CILEX trainee at this level must recognise the limits of their own authority and know when to escalate to a supervisor -- this is an explicit learning outcome in F2, F3 and F5, not incidental. Law changes: this course states the law as understood to be current in September 2026 (England and Wales); flag to the learner that specific figures (SDLT/LTT thresholds, IHT nil-rate bands, fee levels) and any pending reform (e.g. the Bill of Rights Bill, which CILEX's own F1 material lists as syllabus content but which had not been enacted -- the Human Rights Act 1998 remains in force) should be checked against a live source (legislation.gov.uk, gov.uk, cilex.org.uk) before a real exam sitting.

#### LS.1 The structure and classification of law: civil/criminal, common law/equity, the Judicature Acts 1873-75
**The structure and classification of law.** Law is all-pervasive: it regulates relationships between individuals, organisations and the state, and shapes daily life from contracts to road use. The primary classification is **civil law** (disputes between private parties -- individuals, companies -- over rights and obligations, e.g. contract, tort, land) versus **criminal law** (conduct the state prosecutes as an offence against society, e.g. theft, assault). Key distinctions: **burden and standard of proof** -- criminal cases must be proved by the prosecution beyond reasonable doubt (so the jury/magistrates are "sure"); civil cases are proved by the claimant on the balance of probabilities (more likely than not). **Objectives** -- criminal law punishes and deters; civil law compensates or restores the claimant's position. **Remedies** -- criminal courts impose sentences (custody, fines, community orders); civil courts award remedies such as damages, injunctions or specific performance. **Case citation** differs: a criminal case is styled *R v Defendant*; a civil case *Claimant v Defendant*.

**Common law and equity.** Common law developed from the 12th century through royal courts applying consistent, precedent-based rules nationwide. Its rigid procedures and limited remedy (damages only) left some claimants without adequate redress, so parties petitioned the King, whose Lord Chancellor developed **equity** -- a parallel, more flexible system of justice based on fairness/conscience, administered in the Court of Chancery, offering remedies common law could not (injunctions, specific performance, rescission, rectification) and recognising new property concepts (the **trust**). Equity and common law were administered in separate courts until the **Judicature Acts 1873-75** fused their administration into a single court system (all courts can now apply both), though the two remain conceptually distinct bodies of principle; where they conflict, equity prevails.
