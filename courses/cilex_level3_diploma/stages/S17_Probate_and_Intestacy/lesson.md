# S17_Probate_and_Intestacy - Lesson: Probate and intestacy, and entitlement to act

## Goal
The learner explains the rules of intestacy and entitlement to a grant of representation, the different types of grant, and the role of executors, administrators and lasting powers of attorney.

## Syllabus items taught here
- PPC.6 - Technical basics: probate and intestacy, including the intestacy rules
- PPC.7 - Entitlement to act and personnel: executors, administrators, grants, LPAs

## How to teach this
Ask: a person dies without a will, leaving a spouse and two children. Does the spouse automatically inherit everything? Teach each topic as: the rule (the statute section or case authority, stated precisely), why it exists (the policy/purpose CILEX's own module text gives), and how it is applied to facts. CILEX's Foundation-stage exams (F1-F4) are 60 multiple-choice and short 'automated' (fill-in/matching) questions testing precise, exam-ready knowledge -- push the learner to state rules exactly (case names, section numbers, dates) rather than paraphrase loosely. F5 and F6 also include practical scenario tasks (a communication/drafting exercise for F5; producing research documents for F6) -- give the learner realistic client scenarios and paralegal tasks, not just questions about the skill in the abstract. Throughout, reinforce that a CILEX trainee at this level must recognise the limits of their own authority and know when to escalate to a supervisor -- this is an explicit learning outcome in F2, F3 and F5, not incidental. Law changes: this course states the law as understood to be current in September 2026 (England and Wales); flag to the learner that specific figures (SDLT/LTT thresholds, IHT nil-rate bands, fee levels) and any pending reform (e.g. the Bill of Rights Bill, which CILEX's own F1 material lists as syllabus content but which had not been enacted -- the Human Rights Act 1998 remains in force) should be checked against a live source (legislation.gov.uk, gov.uk, cilex.org.uk) before a real exam sitting.

#### PPC.6 Technical basics: probate and intestacy, including the intestacy rules
**Probate and intestacy.** Where a person dies leaving a valid will, their estate is distributed according to its terms (**testate succession**). Where they die without a valid will, in whole or in part, the **intestacy rules** under the **Administration of Estates Act 1925** (as amended, notably by the **Inheritance and Trustees' Powers Act 2014**) apply to distribute the estate (**intestate succession**), following a strict statutory order. In outline, on **total intestacy**: if the deceased leaves a **spouse or civil partner** and **no children/descendants**, the spouse takes the whole estate; if they leave a spouse and **children/descendants**, the spouse takes the deceased's **personal chattels**, a **statutory legacy** (a fixed sum, index-linked and periodically updated by statutory instrument -- check the current figure against a live source), plus **half of the remaining estate** (absolutely, following 2014 Act reforms removing the former life interest trust), with the **other half divided equally** among the children (or their own descendants, if a child predeceased, under the "statutory trusts"); if there is **no surviving spouse**, the estate passes to the deceased's issue in equal shares (statutory trusts), and failing issue, in a fixed order of priority: parents; then siblings of the whole blood (or their issue); then siblings of the half blood (or their issue); then grandparents; then aunts/uncles of the whole blood (or their issue); then aunts/uncles of the half blood (or their issue); and, if no one in this order survives, the estate passes to the **Crown as bona vacantia**. A **cohabiting partner who is not married/in a civil partnership has no automatic entitlement** under intestacy, however long the relationship, though may be able to claim under the **Inheritance (Provision for Family and Dependants) Act 1975** as a dependant. Note: as under S16, a beneficial joint tenancy passes by survivorship **outside** the estate/intestacy rules altogether.

#### PPC.7 Entitlement to act and personnel: executors, administrators, grants, LPAs
**Entitlement to act and personnel.** A person who dies leaving a valid will usually appoints one or more **executors** in it, who derive their authority from the will itself (technically from the moment of death, though a **grant of probate** -- issued by the Probate Registry once the will's validity and the executor's entitlement are confirmed -- is normally needed to deal with most assets, e.g. selling registered land or accessing most bank accounts). Where there is no valid will, or a valid will fails to appoint an executor (or none is able/willing to act), the court appoints an **administrator**, who applies for a **grant of letters of administration**; entitlement to apply follows a statutory order of priority (broadly mirroring the intestacy beneficial entitlement -- spouse/civil partner first, then children, etc.) under the **Non-Contentious Probate Rules 1987**. Where there is a valid will but no executor able to act, the person entitled to the estate under the will applies for a **grant of letters of administration with will annexed**. Executors and administrators are together known as **personal representatives (PRs)**, who hold statutory and fiduciary duties to collect in the estate's assets, pay debts/taxes and liabilities, and distribute the estate correctly, and can be personally liable for a failure to do so (e.g. distributing to the wrong beneficiary, or before dealing with a known claim); PRs commonly protect themselves by advertising for claims/creditors and, where appropriate, obtaining professional valuations and tax clearance before final distribution. A **Lasting Power of Attorney (LPA)**, made under the **Mental Capacity Act 2005**, allows a person (the "donor"), while they still have capacity, to appoint one or more attorneys to make decisions on their behalf if they later lose capacity: a **property and financial affairs LPA** covers financial decisions (which can, with the donor's consent, be used even while they still have capacity) and a **health and welfare LPA** covers personal welfare/medical decisions (usable only once the donor lacks capacity); an LPA must be registered with the **Office of the Public Guardian** before use. An LPA ends on the donor's death -- it gives no authority over the estate, which then requires a grant of representation to the appropriate personal representative in the usual way.
