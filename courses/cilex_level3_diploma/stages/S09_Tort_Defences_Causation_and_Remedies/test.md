## Test items
1. Explain how contributory negligence under the Law Reform (Contributory Negligence) Act 1945 differs from a full defence such as volenti non fit injuria. [6 marks]
2. A pedestrian, while crossing against a red light while looking at their phone, is struck by a delivery driver who was travelling 15mph over the speed limit. The pedestrian suffers a broken leg (a foreseeable type of injury) but, due to an undiagnosed bone condition, the break is far more severe than in an average person. Using IRAC, advise on liability and damages. [14 marks]

## Answer key (for the tutor only)
1. [6] B1 volenti, if established, is a complete defence -- the claimant's claim fails entirely because they freely accepted the specific risk with full knowledge of it; B1 contributory negligence under the 1945 Act is a partial defence -- it does not defeat the claim but allows the court to apportion responsibility; B2 the court reduces damages by a percentage that is 'just and equitable' reflecting the claimant's own share of fault, e.g. for not wearing a seatbelt or ignoring an obvious risk; B2 the two can overlap on similar facts, but the outcome differs sharply -- volenti bars recovery entirely, contributory negligence only reduces the award.
2. [14] Mark using issue-rule-application-conclusion (IRAC): identify the issue(s), state the rule precisely with its authority, apply it to the facts given, and reach a supported conclusion advising the client. Credit: issue = whether the driver is liable in negligence, whether contributory negligence reduces damages, and whether the severity of injury affects recovery; rule = negligence requires duty/breach/causation/foreseeable-type damage; the egg-shell skull rule makes a defendant liable for the full extent of a foreseeable type of injury even if unusually severe due to the claimant's vulnerability; the 1945 Act allows apportionment for the claimant's own contributory fault; application = speeding is a clear breach by the driver causing a foreseeable type of injury (broken leg), so the egg-shell skull rule means the driver remains liable for the full, unusually severe extent of the break; the pedestrian's own conduct (crossing against the light while distracted) is a clear basis for a significant contributory negligence reduction; conclusion = the driver is liable in negligence for the full extent of the injury under the egg-shell skull rule, but total damages should be reduced by a substantial percentage for the pedestrian's contributory negligence.
