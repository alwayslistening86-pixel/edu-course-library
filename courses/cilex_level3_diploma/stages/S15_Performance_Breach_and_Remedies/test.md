## Test items
1. Under the innominate term approach from Hong Kong Fir Shipping v Kawasaki, when can the innocent party terminate the contract for breach of such a term? Choose every correct option.
   A. Whenever any breach of the term occurs, however minor
   B. Only if the term was labelled a 'condition' in the contract
   C. Only if the actual consequences of the breach deprive the innocent party of substantially the whole benefit they were intended to get from the contract
   D. Never -- innominate terms only ever give a right to damages
2. Explain the two limbs of the remoteness test from Hadley v Baxendale. [6 marks]
3. A wedding caterer is booked for a large outdoor wedding reception. Three days before the event, the venue (booked separately by the couple) is destroyed by an accidental fire, making the reception impossible to hold there or relocate in time. The couple had already paid the caterer a non-refundable £2,000 deposit toward a total £6,000 fee, and the caterer had already spent £1,500 on ingredients specifically for the event. Using IRAC, advise on the couple's and the caterer's positions regarding the £2,000 deposit and the balance. [14 marks]

## Answer key (for the tutor only)
1. Correct: C (exactly these options, no others)
2. [6] B3 first limb: loss recoverable if it arises naturally, i.e. in the ordinary course of things, from the breach -- the kind of loss any reasonable person would expect; B3 second limb: loss also recoverable if it was in the reasonable contemplation of both parties, at the time of contracting, as a probable result of the breach, because of special circumstances actually known to the defendant at that time -- unusual loss the defendant had no knowledge of remains too remote.
3. [14] Mark using issue-rule-application-conclusion (IRAC): identify the issue(s), state the rule precisely with its authority, apply it to the facts given, and reach a supported conclusion advising the client. Credit: issue = whether the catering contract is discharged by frustration following destruction of the venue, and the financial consequences; rule = a contract is frustrated where, without either party's fault, a supervening event makes performance impossible or radically different from what was contemplated (Taylor v Caldwell-type destruction, applied here to the loss of the specific venue undermining the shared common purpose); the Law Reform (Frustrated Contracts) Act 1943 governs adjustment -- sums paid before frustration are recoverable (any 'non-refundable' contractual label does not override the Act) subject to the court allowing the payee to retain/recover expenses incurred before discharge, and a party conferring a valuable benefit before frustration may recover a just sum for it; application = the accidental fire, occurring through no fault of either party and making the planned reception impossible at that venue with no time to relocate, is capable of frustrating the catering contract; the £2,000 deposit is prima facie recoverable by the couple despite being described as non-refundable, but the court may allow the caterer to retain/recover its £1,500 wasted expenditure on ingredients out of that sum; the £4,000 balance never becomes due, since future obligations are discharged; conclusion = the contract is likely frustrated; the couple can recover the deposit less a sum the court allows the caterer to retain for expenses reasonably incurred (potentially up to the full £1,500), and no further balance is payable.
