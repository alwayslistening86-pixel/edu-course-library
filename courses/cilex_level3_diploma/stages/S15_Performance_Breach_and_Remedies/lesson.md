# S15_Performance_Breach_and_Remedies - Lesson: Performance, termination, breach and frustration; causation and remedies for breach

## Goal
The learner explains how a contract is performed and discharged, including by frustration, the effect of breach and repudiation, and the remedies available for breach of contract.

## Syllabus items taught here
- CON.7 - Performance, termination and breach of contract, including discharge by frustration
- CON.8 - Causation and remedies for breach of contract: common law, equitable and statutory remedies

## How to teach this
Ask: a venue booked for a large outdoor event burns down the week before, through no one's fault. Does the organiser still have to pay the venue's fee? Teach each topic as: the rule (the statute section or case authority, stated precisely), why it exists (the policy/purpose CILEX's own module text gives), and how it is applied to facts. CILEX's Foundation-stage exams (F1-F4) are 60 multiple-choice and short 'automated' (fill-in/matching) questions testing precise, exam-ready knowledge -- push the learner to state rules exactly (case names, section numbers, dates) rather than paraphrase loosely. F5 and F6 also include practical scenario tasks (a communication/drafting exercise for F5; producing research documents for F6) -- give the learner realistic client scenarios and paralegal tasks, not just questions about the skill in the abstract. Throughout, reinforce that a CILEX trainee at this level must recognise the limits of their own authority and know when to escalate to a supervisor -- this is an explicit learning outcome in F2, F3 and F5, not incidental. Law changes: this course states the law as understood to be current in September 2026 (England and Wales); flag to the learner that specific figures (SDLT/LTT thresholds, IHT nil-rate bands, fee levels) and any pending reform (e.g. the Bill of Rights Bill, which CILEX's own F1 material lists as syllabus content but which had not been enacted -- the Human Rights Act 1998 remains in force) should be checked against a live source (legislation.gov.uk, gov.uk, cilex.org.uk) before a real exam sitting.

#### CON.7 Performance, termination and breach of contract, including discharge by frustration
**Performance and discharge.** A contract is normally discharged by **performance** -- each party fulfilling their obligations. The general rule requires **entire, precise performance** of an "entire obligation" before payment is due (***Cutter v Powell***), though this is tempered by exceptions: **substantial performance** (if performance is substantially, though not perfectly, complete, the price is payable subject to a deduction for the shortfall, ***Hoenig v Isaacs***); **acceptance of partial performance** (if the innocent party has a genuine choice and voluntarily accepts partial performance, a reasonable sum becomes payable); **divisible obligations** (a contract divided into stages/instalments, each separately payable as completed); and **prevention by the other party** (a party is excused, and may recover on a *quantum meruit* basis, if the other party's own conduct prevented complete performance). Discharge can also occur by **agreement** (both parties agree to release each other, itself requiring fresh consideration or a deed unless one side has already fully performed), or by **breach** (below).

**Breach and termination.** A breach occurs where a party fails to perform an obligation as agreed, without lawful excuse. Terms are classified by their importance: a **condition** is a term fundamental to the contract, breach of which entitles the innocent party to **terminate (treat the contract as discharged/repudiated) and/or claim damages**; a **warranty** is a lesser term, breach of which gives a right to **damages only**, not termination; an **innominate (intermediate) term** is judged by the **consequences of the breach** -- if the breach deprives the innocent party of substantially the whole benefit intended, termination is available; if not, damages only (***Hong Kong Fir Shipping v Kawasaki***). **Repudiatory breach** (a breach going to the root of the contract, or an **anticipatory breach** -- a clear, unequivocal indication before performance is due that a party will not perform) entitles the innocent party to **elect**: **accept the repudiation** and treat the contract as at an end (claiming damages), or **affirm** the contract and insist on performance (though affirmation carries risk, e.g. of frustration or further loss, and the innocent party generally has no duty to mitigate unless/until they accept the repudiation). Termination for breach discharges **future** obligations only; accrued rights (e.g. to payment already earned) survive.

**Discharge by frustration.** A contract is **frustrated**, and automatically discharged, where, after formation and without either party's fault, an unforeseen event occurs that makes performance **impossible**, **illegal**, or **radically different** from what was contemplated (not merely more difficult or expensive). Recognised categories: **destruction of the subject matter** (***Taylor v Caldwell***); **a party's death or incapacitating illness** in a personal-service contract; **supervening illegality**; **the non-occurrence of a fundamental common purpose/basis** the contract was made on (narrowly applied -- ***Krell v Henry***, contrast ***Herne Bay Steam Boat v Hutton***, where the specific purpose was not sufficiently fundamental/shared). Frustration is **not** available where the event was **self-induced** (caused by a party's own choice or default), where the contract makes **express provision** for the event (a *force majeure* clause), or where the event was **foreseeable** and the risk should have been provided for. Effect: the contract is discharged automatically from the moment of the frustrating event (not voidable, and not retrospective); the **Law Reform (Frustrated Contracts) Act 1943** governs the financial adjustment between the parties -- sums paid before frustration are recoverable and sums due cease to be payable (subject to the court allowing the payee to retain/recover expenses incurred), and a party who has obtained a valuable benefit under the contract before frustration may be ordered to pay a just sum for it.

#### CON.8 Causation and remedies for breach of contract: common law, equitable and statutory remedies
**Causation and remedies for breach of contract.** **Common law damages** aim to put the innocent party, so far as money can, in the position they would have been in **had the contract been properly performed** (the expectation interest), though a claimant may instead claim **reliance loss** (wasted expenditure) where expectation loss is hard to quantify. Requirements: **factual causation** (the breach caused the loss); **remoteness** -- loss recoverable only if it arises **naturally** from the breach in the ordinary course of things, or was within the **reasonable contemplation of both parties** at the time of contracting as a probable result of the breach (***Hadley v Baxendale***; unusual/special loss is only recoverable if the defendant had actual knowledge of the special circumstances making it likely); and **mitigation** (the claimant must take reasonable steps to minimise their loss; damages are reduced for any failure to do so, and no damages are recoverable for loss that reasonable mitigation would have avoided). **Contributory negligence** does not generally reduce contract damages unless the breach also amounts to a concurrent tortious duty. A contractual **liquidated damages clause**, genuinely pre-estimating loss, is enforceable; a clause that is instead a **penalty** (extravagant, unconscionable, and not a genuine attempt to estimate loss, judged in the modern law by whether it protects a legitimate interest out of proportion to that interest) is unenforceable, and the claimant must prove actual loss instead.

**Equitable remedies** are discretionary, available where damages are an inadequate remedy: **specific performance** (an order compelling actual performance, typically for contracts involving unique subject matter, e.g. land, rarely for contracts of personal service or requiring ongoing supervision); **injunctions** (prohibitory, to restrain a threatened/continuing breach, e.g. of a valid restraint-of-trade clause) or, exceptionally, mandatory. Equitable remedies are subject to equitable bars/discretion (e.g. delay/laches, the claimant's own conduct -- "he who comes to equity must come with clean hands", hardship to the defendant). **Statutory remedies**: for consumer contracts under the **Consumer Rights Act 2015**, a hierarchy of remedies applies to defective goods -- the **short-term right to reject** (within 30 days, a full refund), the right to a **repair or replacement**, and, if that fails or is not possible, a **price reduction or the final right to reject**. A *quantum meruit* claim (a reasonable sum for work done) may be available outside a complete, enforceable contract, e.g. where performance was accepted, or work done in anticipation of a contract that never materialised.
