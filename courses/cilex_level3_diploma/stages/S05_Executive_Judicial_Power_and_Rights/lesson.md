# S05_Executive_Judicial_Power_and_Rights - Lesson: Executive power, judicial power, judicial review, and civil liberties/human rights

## Goal
The learner explains the structure and accountability of the executive, the independence and method of the judiciary including precedent, the grounds and remedies for judicial review, and how human rights and equality are protected in domestic law.

## Syllabus items taught here
- LS.6 - Executive power: the Crown, the executive branch, ministerial accountability
- LS.7 - Judicial power: judicial independence, common law method, precedent, the status of decisions by court
- LS.8 - Judicial review: purpose, grounds, remedies, the Upper Tribunal
- LS.9 - The protection of civil liberties, human rights and equality: ECHR, HRA 1998, the Equality Act 2010

## How to teach this
Ask: a minister makes a decision everyone thinks is unfair, but not actually unlawful. Can a court do anything about it? Teach each topic as: the rule (the statute section or case authority, stated precisely), why it exists (the policy/purpose CILEX's own module text gives), and how it is applied to facts. CILEX's Foundation-stage exams (F1-F4) are 60 multiple-choice and short 'automated' (fill-in/matching) questions testing precise, exam-ready knowledge -- push the learner to state rules exactly (case names, section numbers, dates) rather than paraphrase loosely. F5 and F6 also include practical scenario tasks (a communication/drafting exercise for F5; producing research documents for F6) -- give the learner realistic client scenarios and paralegal tasks, not just questions about the skill in the abstract. Throughout, reinforce that a CILEX trainee at this level must recognise the limits of their own authority and know when to escalate to a supervisor -- this is an explicit learning outcome in F2, F3 and F5, not incidental. Law changes: this course states the law as understood to be current in September 2026 (England and Wales); flag to the learner that specific figures (SDLT/LTT thresholds, IHT nil-rate bands, fee levels) and any pending reform (e.g. the Bill of Rights Bill, which CILEX's own F1 material lists as syllabus content but which had not been enacted -- the Human Rights Act 1998 remains in force) should be checked against a live source (legislation.gov.uk, gov.uk, cilex.org.uk) before a real exam sitting.

#### LS.6 Executive power: the Crown, the executive branch, ministerial accountability
**Executive power.** Key roles: the **Prime Minister** (head of government, appoints/dismisses ministers, chairs Cabinet); **Secretaries of State** (head government departments); **Cabinet ministers** (senior ministers forming the Cabinet, collectively deciding major policy); the **Civil Service** (permanent, politically neutral officials implementing policy). The **Crown** retains **prerogative powers**, now almost always exercised on ministerial advice (e.g. some treaty-making, deployment of armed forces, granting honours), interacting with legislative power in that Parliament can restrict or abolish prerogative powers by statute, and the courts have confirmed the exercise of prerogative power can, in principle, be subject to judicial review. **Accountability**: **individual ministerial responsibility** -- a minister is personally accountable to Parliament for their department's conduct and their own; **collective cabinet responsibility** -- Cabinet decisions bind all ministers publicly, who must support them or resign.

#### LS.7 Judicial power: judicial independence, common law method, precedent, the status of decisions by court
**Judicial power.** **Judicial independence and impartiality** are constitutionally protected (security of tenure, the process for removing senior judges, and separation from the executive/legislature), including the Supreme Court's institutional independence since its creation. **Common law method**: cases are cited by party names and a reference (e.g. neutral citation); judgments state the **ratio decidendi** (the binding legal reasoning necessary to the decision) and may include **obiter dicta** (persuasive but non-binding remarks). The **hierarchy of the courts** determines the doctrine of **precedent (stare decisis)**: a court is generally bound by the decisions of courts above it in the same hierarchy (binding precedent), while decisions of courts elsewhere, or obiter dicta, are only persuasive. The **Supreme Court** normally follows its own past decisions but, per the **Practice Statement on Judicial Precedent 1966**, may depart from them when it appears right to do so. The **Court of Appeal** is generally bound by its own past decisions, subject to limited exceptions recognised in ***Young v Bristol Aeroplane Co Ltd* (1944)** (e.g. conflicting prior decisions, a prior decision inconsistent with a later Supreme Court decision, or one given per incuriam). The **High Court** is bound by the Court of Appeal and Supreme Court. Other influential courts: the (former) European Court of Justice (retained EU case law has a modified status post-Brexit), the European Court of Human Rights (whose case law UK courts must take into account, not are strictly bound by), and the Privy Council (persuasive, not binding, on English courts).

#### LS.8 Judicial review: purpose, grounds, remedies, the Upper Tribunal
**Judicial review.** Judicial review lets the High Court supervise the lawfulness of decisions by public bodies exercising public functions -- it reviews the legality of the decision-making process, not the merits of the decision itself. **Grounds**: **illegality** (the decision-maker acted outside their legal powers, misdirected themselves in law, or failed to exercise a discretion properly); **procedural unfairness** (breach of natural justice, e.g. failing to give a fair hearing or adequate reasons, or breach of a legitimate expectation); **irrationality** (the decision is so unreasonable no reasonable decision-maker could have made it -- **Wednesbury unreasonableness**). **Remedies** are discretionary: **mandatory orders** (compel the body to act), **prohibiting orders** (prevent unlawful action), **quashing orders** (nullify the unlawful decision), **injunctions**, **declarations** (state the legal position), and **damages** (only where an independent cause of action, e.g. under the Human Rights Act, also exists). The **Upper Tribunal** also has a judicial review jurisdiction for certain matters, under the **Tribunals, Courts and Enforcement Act 2007**.

#### LS.9 The protection of civil liberties, human rights and equality: ECHR, HRA 1998, the Equality Act 2010
**Civil liberties, human rights and equality.** The **European Convention on Human Rights (ECHR)** protects rights including life (Art 2), freedom from torture/inhuman treatment (Art 3), liberty (Art 5), fair trial (Art 6), private and family life (Art 8), and freedom of expression (Art 10). The **Human Rights Act 1998** gives the Convention further effect in domestic law: **s2** requires UK courts to *take into account* (not be strictly bound by) European Court of Human Rights case law; **s3** requires legislation to be read and given effect, so far as possible, compatibly with Convention rights; **s4** allows the higher courts to make a **declaration of incompatibility** where legislation cannot be read compatibly (this does not strike the legislation down -- it remains in force until Parliament amends it); **s6** makes it unlawful for a public authority to act incompatibly with Convention rights, giving individuals a route to challenge such acts. Note: a **Bill of Rights Bill**, intended by the then government to replace the Human Rights Act, was introduced to Parliament in 2022 but was withdrawn in 2023 and never became law -- the Human Rights Act 1998 remains in force; check a live source for any change before relying on this. In criminal investigations, the powers of the state (e.g. use of force, stop and search, detention) are constrained by Convention rights, particularly Arts 3, 5 and 8, and by PACE-derived detainee safeguards. The **Equality Act 2010** consolidates discrimination law: it defines **protected characteristics** (age, disability, gender reassignment, marriage/civil partnership, pregnancy/maternity, race, religion or belief, sex, sexual orientation), **direct discrimination** (treating someone less favourably because of a protected characteristic) and **indirect discrimination** (a provision, criterion or practice that disadvantages people sharing a protected characteristic and cannot be objectively justified).
