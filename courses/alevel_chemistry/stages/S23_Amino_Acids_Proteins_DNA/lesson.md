# S23_Amino_Acids_Proteins_DNA - Lesson: Amino acids, proteins, enzymes, DNA and anticancer drugs

## Goal
The learner explains the acid-base properties of amino acids, the structure of proteins (primary, secondary, tertiary), hydrolysis and chromatography of amino acids, enzyme action and inhibitor design, the structure of DNA, and the action of cisplatin as an anticancer drug.

## How to teach this
Ask how a drug can be designed to fit an enzyme's active site, like a key for a lock. Teach from the particles outwards: what the electrons and bonds are doing, then the equation or mechanism, then a worked example with units and sensible significant figures. Use relative atomic masses from AQA's Periodic Table (e.g. H 1.0, C 12.0, O 16.0, Na 23.0, Cl 35.5) and its data sheet values (N_A = 6.022 x 10^23 mol^-1, R = 8.31 J K^-1 mol^-1). Mechanisms are described in words: say where each curly arrow starts (a lone pair or a bond) and where it ends. Every numerical answer here was computed when the course was built.

#### 3.3.13.1 Amino acids (A-level only)
Amino acids have an amino group and a carboxylic acid group; α-amino acids are chiral (except glycine). They exist as zwitterions (H3N+-CHR-COO-) at the isoelectric point, which gives them high melting points and water solubility. In acid the COO- is protonated (H3N+-CHR-COOH); in alkali the NH3+ loses H+ (H2N-CHR-COO-).

#### 3.3.13.2 Proteins (A-level only)
Proteins are sequences of amino acids joined by peptide (amide) links. Primary structure: the sequence. Secondary: α-helix and β-pleated sheet, held by hydrogen bonds between N-H and C=O. Tertiary: the overall 3D fold, held by hydrogen bonds, ionic interactions, and disulfide bridges (S-S between cysteines). Hydrolysis of peptide links (heat with 6 mol dm^-3 HCl) gives amino acids; they can be separated and identified by thin-layer chromatography, with a locating agent (ninhydrin) or UV light, and identified from their Rf values.

#### 3.3.13.3 Enzymes (A-level only)
Enzymes are proteins with an active site that is stereospecific: only one enantiomer of a substrate fits (lock and key). An inhibitor blocks the active site by binding there. Computer modelling of active sites allows drugs to be designed as inhibitors that fit precisely.

#### 3.3.13.4 DNA (A-level only)
DNA: a polymer of nucleotides, each with a phosphate, 2-deoxyribose (a sugar) and one of four bases (adenine, cytosine, guanine, thymine). The sugar-phosphate backbone forms by condensation between the phosphate and the sugar's OH groups. Two strands twist into a double helix held by hydrogen bonds between complementary base pairs: A-T (two hydrogen bonds) and C-G (three).

#### 3.3.13.5 Action of anticancer drugs (A-level only)
Cisplatin, Pt(NH3)2Cl2 (the cis isomer), is an anticancer drug. A chloride ligand is replaced by a nitrogen of a guanine base in DNA (ligand substitution); binding to two guanines distorts the DNA and prevents replication, so cells cannot divide. It also affects healthy cells (e.g. hair loss, kidney damage), so dose and use involve balancing benefits and risks.
