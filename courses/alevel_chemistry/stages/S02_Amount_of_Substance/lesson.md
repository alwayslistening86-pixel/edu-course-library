## How to teach this
Ask how many atoms are in a teaspoon of water (about 5 g) and make the learner see why chemists count in moles. Teach from the particles outwards: what the electrons and bonds are doing, then the equation or mechanism, then a worked example with units and sensible significant figures. Use relative atomic masses from AQA's Periodic Table (e.g. H 1.0, C 12.0, O 16.0, Na 23.0, Cl 35.5) and its data sheet values (N_A = 6.022 x 10^23 mol^-1, R = 8.31 J K^-1 mol^-1). Mechanisms are described in words: say where each curly arrow starts (a lone pair or a bond) and where it ends. Every numerical answer here was computed when the course was built.

#### 3.1.2.1 Relative atomic mass and relative molecular mass
Relative atomic mass A_r: the weighted mean mass of an atom of an element relative to 1/12 of the mass of a carbon-12 atom. Relative molecular mass M_r (or relative formula mass for ionic compounds): the sum of the A_r values. *Example:* M_r of CaCO3 = 40.1 + 12.0 + 3(16.0) = 100.1.

#### 3.1.2.2 The mole and the Avogadro constant
One mole contains 6.022 x 10^23 particles (the Avogadro constant, N_A). Amount n = mass/M_r; particles = n x N_A. Concentration c = n/V (mol dm^-3, with V in dm^3). *Example:* 5.0 g of water is 0.278 mol, or 1.67 x 10^23 molecules. *Example:* 25.0 cm^3 of 0.100 mol dm^-3 NaOH contains 0.0250 x 0.100 = 2.50 x 10^-3 mol.

#### 3.1.2.3 The ideal gas equation
pV = nRT with p in Pa, V in m^3, T in K and R = 8.31 J K^-1 mol^-1. Convert: 1 dm^3 = 10^-3 m^3, 1 cm^3 = 10^-6 m^3, 1 kPa = 10^3 Pa, T/K = t/°C + 273. *Example:* 0.0500 mol of gas at 100 kPa and 25 °C occupies V = nRT/p = 0.001238 m^3 = 1240 cm^3. The equation also finds M_r of a volatile liquid from the mass and volume of its vapour.

#### 3.1.2.4 Empirical and molecular formula
Empirical formula: the simplest whole-number ratio of atoms. Molecular formula: the actual number of atoms (a whole-number multiple of the empirical formula, found from M_r). Method: mass (or %) ÷ A_r, divide by the smallest, then scale to whole numbers. *Example:* C 85.7%, H 14.3%: C 7.14, H 14.3, ratio 1:2, empirical CH2; if M_r = 56, the molecular formula is C4H8.

#### 3.1.2.5 Balanced equations and associated calculations
Balanced equations (including ionic equations, e.g. H+(aq) + OH-(aq) → H2O(l)) give mole ratios for reacting masses, gas volumes, solution concentrations and titrations. Percentage yield = actual/theoretical x 100%. Atom economy = M_r of desired product ÷ total M_r of all reactants x 100% (higher atom economy means less waste; addition reactions have 100%). *Example:* 10.0 g of CaCO3 decomposes: CaCO3 → CaO + CO2; theoretical CaO = 5.60 g; atom economy for CaO = 56.0%. If 5.20 g is obtained, yield = 92.8%.

#### RP1 Required practical 1: make up a volumetric solution and carry out a simple acid-base titration
**Standard solution:** weigh the solid by difference, dissolve in a beaker of distilled water, transfer with washings to a volumetric flask, make up to the mark (bottom of the meniscus at eye level), stopper and invert. **Titration:** rinse the pipette with the solution it will deliver and the burette with its solution; add a few drops of indicator to the conical flask; run in titrant, swirling, adding dropwise near the end point (on a white tile); record burette readings to 0.05 cm^3; repeat until concordant titres (within 0.10 cm^3) and average them. *Example:* 25.0 cm^3 of Na2CO3 solution (1.325 g made up to 250 cm^3; M_r = 106.0) needs 25.00 cm^3 of 0.100 mol dm^-3 HCl (Na2CO3 + 2HCl → 2NaCl + H2O + CO2).

#### MS 0.0 Recognise and make use of appropriate units in calculation
Units: mass in g (or kg), volume in cm^3 or dm^3 (1 dm^3 = 1000 cm^3), concentration in mol dm^-3, pressure in Pa, temperature in K. Always convert before substituting and give units with answers.

#### MS 0.1 Recognise and use expressions in decimal and ordinary form
Decimal and standard form: 0.000250 = 2.50 x 10^-4. Use the calculator's EXP or x10^x key.

#### MS 0.2 Use ratios, fractions and percentages
Ratios and percentages: mole ratios from equations; percentage composition by mass; percentage yield and atom economy.

#### MS 1.1 Use an appropriate number of significant figures
Give the answer to the same number of significant figures as the least precise data (typically 3); don't round intermediate values.

#### MS 2.2 Change the subject of an equation
Change the subject: n = m/M_r ↔ m = nM_r ↔ M_r = m/n; V = nRT/p; c = n/V ↔ V = n/c.

#### MS 2.3 Substitute numerical values into algebraic equations using appropriate units for physical quantities
Substitute values with consistent units (e.g. pV = nRT in Pa, m^3 and K) and check the unit of the result.
