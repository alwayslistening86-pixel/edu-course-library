# S15_Kp - Lesson: Kp for homogeneous gaseous equilibria

## Goal
The learner calculates mole fractions and partial pressures, writes and calculates Kp with units, and explains the effect of changing conditions on the equilibrium position and Kp.

## Syllabus items taught here
- 3.1.10 - Equilibrium constant Kp for homogeneous systems (A-level only)

## How to teach this
Ask why chemists use pressures rather than concentrations for gas equilibria. Teach from the particles outwards: what the electrons and bonds are doing, then the equation or mechanism, then a worked example with units and sensible significant figures. Use relative atomic masses from AQA's Periodic Table (e.g. H 1.0, C 12.0, O 16.0, Na 23.0, Cl 35.5) and its data sheet values (N_A = 6.022 x 10^23 mol^-1, R = 8.31 J K^-1 mol^-1). Mechanisms are described in words: say where each curly arrow starts (a lone pair or a bond) and where it ends. Every numerical answer here was computed when the course was built.

#### 3.1.10 Equilibrium constant Kp for homogeneous systems (A-level only)
Mole fraction of A = moles of A/total moles of gas. Partial pressure pA = mole fraction x total pressure; the partial pressures add up to the total. For aA(g) + bB(g) ⇌ cC(g) + dD(g): Kp = pC^c pD^d/(pA^a pB^b), with units from the expression. Only temperature changes Kp (same rules as Kc); changing total pressure changes the position of equilibrium but not Kp; a catalyst changes neither. *Example:* 1.00 mol N2O4 is 20% dissociated (N2O4 ⇌ 2NO2) at a total pressure of 100 kPa: moles 0.80 and 0.40 (total 1.20); partial pressures 66.7 and 33.3 kPa; Kp = 16.7 kPa.
