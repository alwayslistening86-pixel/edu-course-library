# S17_Acids_and_Bases - Lesson: Brønsted-Lowry acids, pH, Ka, titration curves and buffers

## How to teach this
Ask why the pH of pure water at 50 °C is less than 7 and yet the water is still neutral. Teach from the particles outwards: what the electrons and bonds are doing, then the equation or mechanism, then a worked example with units and sensible significant figures. Use relative atomic masses from AQA's Periodic Table (e.g. H 1.0, C 12.0, O 16.0, Na 23.0, Cl 35.5) and its data sheet values (N_A = 6.022 x 10^23 mol^-1, R = 8.31 J K^-1 mol^-1). Mechanisms are described in words: say where each curly arrow starts (a lone pair or a bond) and where it ends. Every numerical answer here was computed when the course was built.

#### 3.1.12.1 Brønsted–Lowry acid–base equilibria in aqueous solution (A-level only)
A Brønsted-Lowry acid is a proton donor; a base is a proton acceptor. Acid-base equilibria involve proton transfer, forming conjugate pairs: HA + H2O ⇌ H3O+ + A-. Strong acids fully dissociate (HCl, HNO3, H2SO4 for its first proton); weak acids partially dissociate (ethanoic acid).

#### 3.1.12.2 Definition and determination of pH (A-level only)
pH = -log10[H+]; [H+] = 10^-pH. For strong monoprotic acids, [H+] = [acid]. *Example:* 0.0250 mol dm^-3 HCl has pH 1.60. Diluting a strong acid 10-fold raises its pH by 1.

#### 3.1.12.3 The ionic product of water, Kw (A-level only)
Water dissociates slightly: H2O ⇌ H+ + OH-; Kw = [H+][OH-] = 1.00 x 10^-14 mol^2 dm^-6 at 298 K. In pure water [H+] = [OH-] = root Kw. For strong bases, [OH-] = [base] x number of OH-; [H+] = Kw/[OH-]. *Example:* 0.0500 mol dm^-3 NaOH: [H+] = 2.00 x 10^-13, pH 12.70. Kw increases with temperature (dissociation is endothermic): at 50 °C, Kw = 5.48 x 10^-14, so neutral pH = 6.63.

#### 3.1.12.4 Weak acids and bases Ka for weak acids (A-level only)
For a weak acid HA: Ka = [H+][A-]/[HA]; pKa = -log10 Ka. Assuming [H+] = [A-] and negligible dissociation: [H+] = root(Ka[HA]). *Example:* 0.100 mol dm^-3 ethanoic acid (Ka = 1.74 x 10^-5): [H+] = 0.00132 mol dm^-3, pH = 2.88. A larger Ka (smaller pKa) means a stronger acid.

#### 3.1.12.5 pH curves, titrations and indicators (A-level only)
pH curves (pH against volume of titrant): strong acid-strong base (vertical section about pH 3-11), weak acid-strong base (starts higher, vertical section about 7-11, equivalence point above 7), strong acid-weak base (vertical section about 3-7, equivalence point below 7), weak acid-weak base (no sharp vertical section: no indicator works). Choose an indicator whose colour-change range lies within the vertical section: methyl orange (3.1-4.4) for strong acid-weak base; phenolphthalein (8.3-10) for weak acid-strong base; either for strong-strong. At half-neutralisation of a weak acid, [HA] = [A-], so pH = pKa: read pKa from the curve.

#### 3.1.12.6 Buffer action (A-level only)
A buffer resists changes in pH when small amounts of acid or base are added. Acidic buffer: a weak acid and its salt (ethanoic acid + sodium ethanoate): large reservoirs of HA and A-; added H+ reacts with A- to form HA; added OH- reacts with HA (H+) to form water and A-; the ratio [HA]/[A-] barely changes. Basic buffer: ammonia + ammonium chloride. For an acidic buffer, [H+] = Ka[HA]/[A-]. *Example:* 0.100 mol ethanoic acid + 0.0500 mol sodium ethanoate in 1.00 dm^3: [H+] = 1.74 x 10^-5 x 0.100/0.0500 = 3.48 x 10^-5, pH = 4.46. Uses: shampoos, biological systems (blood is buffered by carbonic acid/hydrogencarbonate at about pH 7.4).

#### RP9 Required practical 9: pH changes when a weak acid reacts with a strong base and a strong acid with a weak base
**Method:** calibrate a pH meter with buffer solutions; add the base from a burette to the acid in a beaker in small portions (0.5-1 cm^3, smaller near the end point), stirring and recording the pH after each addition; plot pH against volume. Find the equivalence point (the centre of the vertical section) and, for a weak acid, the pKa (pH at half the equivalence volume).

#### MS 2.5 Use logarithms in relation to quantities that range over several orders of magnitude
Logarithms handle concentrations spanning orders of magnitude: pH = -log10[H+] and pKa = -log10 Ka; each unit of pH is a factor of 10 in [H+].
