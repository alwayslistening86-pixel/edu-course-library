# S22_Amines_and_Polymers - Lesson: Amines and condensation polymers

## Goal
The learner describes the preparation of aliphatic and aromatic amines, explains the relative base strength of amines, their nucleophilic reactions including quaternary ammonium salts, the formation and structure of polyesters and polyamides, and the biodegradability and disposal of polymers.

## Syllabus items taught here

- 3.3.11.2 - Base properties (A-level only)
- 3.3.11.3 - Nucleophilic properties (A-level only)

## How to teach this
Ask why nylon ropes don't rot but cotton ones do. Teach from the particles outwards: what the electrons and bonds are doing, then the equation or mechanism, then a worked example with units and sensible significant figures. Use relative atomic masses from AQA's Periodic Table (e.g. H 1.0, C 12.0, O 16.0, Na 23.0, Cl 35.5) and its data sheet values (N_A = 6.022 x 10^23 mol^-1, R = 8.31 J K^-1 mol^-1). Mechanisms are described in words: say where each curly arrow starts (a lone pair or a bond) and where it ends. Every numerical answer here was computed when the course was built.

#### 3.3.11.1 Preparation (A-level only)
Primary aliphatic amines: from halogenoalkanes with excess ammonia (heated in ethanol, sealed tube): nucleophilic substitution; or by reducing nitriles with LiAlH4 or with H2 and a nickel catalyst (from S10's nitriles). Aromatic amines: reduce nitro compounds with tin and concentrated HCl, then NaOH (e.g. nitrobenzene → phenylamine). Aromatic amines are used to make dyes.

#### 3.3.11.2 Base properties (A-level only)
Amines are weak bases: the nitrogen lone pair accepts a proton. Base strength: primary aliphatic amines > ammonia > aromatic amines. Alkyl groups release electrons (positive inductive effect), increasing electron density on N and making the lone pair more available; in phenylamine the lone pair is delocalised into the benzene ring, making it less available.

#### 3.3.11.3 Nucleophilic properties (A-level only)
Amines are nucleophiles (N lone pair). Primary amines react with halogenoalkanes to form secondary and tertiary amines and quaternary ammonium salts (R4N+ X-), by nucleophilic substitution; the mixture of products depends on the ratio. Quaternary ammonium salts with long chains are cationic surfactants (fabric softeners, hair conditioners). Amines react with acyl chlorides and anhydrides to form N-substituted amides (S21).

#### 3.3.12.1 Condensation polymers (A-level only)
Condensation polymers form with the loss of a small molecule (water or HCl) at each link. Polyesters: a diol + a dicarboxylic acid (or diacyl dichloride), e.g. Terylene (PET) from benzene-1,4-dicarboxylic acid and ethane-1,2-diol. Polyamides: a diamine + a dicarboxylic acid (or dichloride), e.g. nylon-6,6 from hexane-1,6-diamine and hexanedioic acid; Kevlar from benzene-1,4-diamine and benzene-1,4-dicarboxylic acid. Draw the repeating unit from the monomers and vice versa. Hydrogen bonds between polyamide chains (N-H to C=O) give strength; Kevlar's flat chains pack closely and hydrogen-bond strongly (bullet-proof vests).

#### 3.3.12.2 Biodegradability and disposal of polymers (A-level only)
Polyalkenes are chemically inert (non-polar C-C and C-H bonds) and not biodegradable. Polyesters and polyamides can be hydrolysed at their ester and amide links (by acids, bases or enzymes), so they are biodegradable, though slowly. Disposal: landfill (land use, slow decay), incineration (energy recovery, but CO2 and toxic gases), recycling (saves resources and energy, but collection and sorting cost). Weighing the advantages and disadvantages is part of sustainable chemistry.
