# S04_Energetics - Lesson: Enthalpy changes, calorimetry, Hess's law and bond enthalpies

## Goal
The learner defines standard enthalpy changes, calculates enthalpy changes from calorimetry, applies Hess's law with formation and combustion data, uses mean bond enthalpies, carries out required practical 2, and handles means, uncertainties and estimates.

## How to teach this
Ask why a hand warmer gets hot and an instant cold pack gets cold, and what 'the system' is in each. Teach from the particles outwards: what the electrons and bonds are doing, then the equation or mechanism, then a worked example with units and sensible significant figures. Use relative atomic masses from AQA's Periodic Table (e.g. H 1.0, C 12.0, O 16.0, Na 23.0, Cl 35.5) and its data sheet values (N_A = 6.022 x 10^23 mol^-1, R = 8.31 J K^-1 mol^-1). Mechanisms are described in words: say where each curly arrow starts (a lone pair or a bond) and where it ends. Every numerical answer here was computed when the course was built.

#### 3.1.4.1 Enthalpy change
Enthalpy change ΔH: the heat energy change at constant pressure. Exothermic ΔH negative; endothermic positive. Standard conditions: 100 kPa and a stated temperature, usually 298 K, with substances in their standard states. Standard enthalpy of combustion ΔcH°: one mole of a substance burned completely in oxygen. Standard enthalpy of formation ΔfH°: one mole of a compound formed from its elements in their standard states (zero for elements).

#### 3.1.4.2 Calorimetry
Calorimetry: q = mcΔT (m = mass of the solution or water, c = 4.18 J g^-1 K^-1). ΔH = -q/n (per mole of the limiting reactant), in kJ mol^-1. *Example:* 50.0 cm^3 of 1.00 mol dm^-3 HCl and 50.0 cm^3 of 1.00 mol dm^-3 NaOH rise by 6.8 K: q = 100 x 4.18 x 6.8 = 2840 J; n(water) = 0.0500 mol; ΔH = -56.8 kJ mol^-1. Results are less exothermic than data-book values because of heat loss, incomplete combustion (for fuels) and non-standard conditions.

#### 3.1.4.3 Applications of Hess’s law
Hess's law: the enthalpy change of a reaction is independent of the route. From formation data: ΔH = ΣΔfH(products) - ΣΔfH(reactants). From combustion data: ΔH = ΣΔcH(reactants) - ΣΔcH(products). *Example:* ΔfH of propane from combustion data (C -394, H2 -286, C3H8 -2220 kJ mol^-1): 3C + 4H2 → C3H8: ΔH = 3(-394) + 4(-286) - (-2220) = -106 kJ mol^-1.

#### 3.1.4.4 Bond enthalpies
Mean bond enthalpy: the enthalpy to break one mole of a bond in the gas phase, averaged over many compounds. ΔH = Σ(bonds broken) - Σ(bonds made). Results differ from Hess's-law values because mean values are averages, and substances may not be gaseous. *Example:* CH4 + 2O2 → CO2 + 2H2O (C-H 413, O=O 498, C=O 805, O-H 464): broken 4(413) + 2(498) = 2648; made 2(805) + 4(464) = 3466; ΔH = -818 kJ mol^-1.

#### RP2 Required practical 2: measurement of an enthalpy change
**Method (reaction in solution):** use a polystyrene cup with a lid, measure the starting temperature for a few minutes, add the second reagent at a known time, stir and record the temperature every 30 s. **Analysis:** plot temperature against time and extrapolate the cooling line back to the time of mixing to find the maximum temperature change corrected for heat loss. For fuels, use a spirit burner under a copper calorimeter; weigh the burner before and after.

#### MS 0.3 Estimate results
Estimate results before calculating: e.g. a neutralisation should give roughly -57 kJ mol^-1; a combustion of a hydrocarbon hundreds or thousands of kJ mol^-1.

#### MS 1.2 Find arithmetic means
Arithmetic mean of concordant results only: titres 23.45, 23.50, 23.40 (ignore a rough 24.1) give 23.45 cm^3.

#### MS 1.3 Identify uncertainties in measurements and use simple techniques to determine uncertainty when data are combined
Uncertainty: a thermometer reading to 0.5 °C has uncertainty ±0.25 °C per reading, so a temperature change (two readings) has ±0.5 °C; for ΔT = 6.8 °C this is 7.4%. A burette reading has ±0.05 cm^3, and a titre (two readings) ±0.10 cm^3. Percentage uncertainty = uncertainty/value x 100%; add percentage uncertainties when multiplying or dividing.
