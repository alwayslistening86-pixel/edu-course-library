# S19_Transition_Metal_Chemistry - Lesson: Colour, variable oxidation states, catalysts and aqueous ions

## How to teach this
Ask why copper sulfate solution is blue but turns deep blue when ammonia is added. Teach from the particles outwards: what the electrons and bonds are doing, then the equation or mechanism, then a worked example with units and sensible significant figures. Use relative atomic masses from AQA's Periodic Table (e.g. H 1.0, C 12.0, O 16.0, Na 23.0, Cl 35.5) and its data sheet values (N_A = 6.022 x 10^23 mol^-1, R = 8.31 J K^-1 mol^-1). Mechanisms are described in words: say where each curly arrow starts (a lone pair or a bond) and where it ends. Every numerical answer here was computed when the course was built.

#### 3.2.5.4 Formation of coloured ions (A-level only)
In a complex, the d orbitals split into two energy levels; absorbing visible light excites an electron between them: ΔE = hν = hc/λ. The light not absorbed is transmitted or reflected, giving the colour. Changing the oxidation state, the coordination number or the ligand changes ΔE and so the colour. No colour arises when there are no d electrons, a full d sub-level (Zn^2+, Cu+), or ΔE outside the visible range. Colorimetry (visible spectroscopy) measures the absorbance of light of a chosen wavelength; a calibration curve of absorbance against known concentrations lets you find an unknown concentration (a ligand may be added to intensify the colour). *Example:* absorption at 600 nm corresponds to ΔE = 3.32 x 10^-19 J per ion.

#### 3.2.5.5 Variable oxidation states (A-level only)
Transition elements show variable oxidation states. Vanadium: reducing yellow VO2^+ (+5) with zinc in acid gives blue VO^2+ (+4), then green V^3+ (+3), then violet V^2+ (+2). The redox potential for a change to a lower oxidation state depends on pH (e.g. MnO4- is a stronger oxidising agent in acid) and on the ligand (e.g. Tollens' reagent [Ag(NH3)2]+ is reduced to silver by aldehydes, distinguishing them from ketones). Redox titrations with acidified KMnO4 are self-indicating (the first permanent pale pink shows the end point; acidify with dilute H2SO4, not HCl, which would be oxidised). MnO4- + 8H+ + 5Fe^2+ → Mn^2+ + 4H2O + 5Fe^3+ (1:5); 2MnO4- + 16H+ + 5C2O4^2- → 2Mn^2+ + 8H2O + 10CO2 (2:5). *Example:* 25.0 cm^3 of Fe^2+(aq) needs 22.50 cm^3 of 0.0200 mol dm^-3 KMnO4: n(MnO4-) = 4.50 x 10^-4, n(Fe^2+) = 0.00225 mol, [Fe^2+] = 0.0900 mol dm^-3.

#### 3.2.5.6 Catalysts (A-level only)
Heterogeneous catalysts are in a different phase from the reactants; reaction occurs at active sites on the surface (adsorption, reaction, desorption). A support medium (e.g. a ceramic honeycomb) maximises surface area and minimises the amount of expensive catalyst. Examples: V2O5 in the Contact process: SO2 + V2O5 → SO3 + V2O4, then V2O4 + (1/2)O2 → V2O5 (variable oxidation state lets vanadium be regenerated); iron in the Haber process. Poisoning: impurities (e.g. sulfur on iron) block active sites, reducing efficiency and adding cost. Homogeneous catalysts are in the same phase and work through an intermediate. Fe^2+ catalyses S2O8^2- + 2I- → 2SO4^2- + I2 (the uncatalysed reaction is slow because both ions are negative and repel): S2O8^2- + 2Fe^2+ → 2SO4^2- + 2Fe^3+; 2Fe^3+ + 2I- → 2Fe^2+ + I2. Autocatalysis: Mn^2+, a product of MnO4- with C2O4^2-, catalyses the reaction: 4Mn^2+ + MnO4- + 8H+ → 5Mn^3+ + 4H2O; 2Mn^3+ + C2O4^2- → 2Mn^2+ + 2CO2 (the rate increases after a slow start).

#### 3.2.6 Reactions of ions in aqueous solution (A-level only)
Metal-aqua ions: [Fe(H2O)6]^2+ (pale green), [Cu(H2O)6]^2+ (blue), [Al(H2O)6]^3+ (colourless), [Fe(H2O)6]^3+ (pale violet, looks yellow-brown in solution because of hydrolysis). The 3+ ions are more acidic: their higher charge-to-size ratio polarises the coordinated water more, weakening the O-H bond and releasing H+: [Fe(H2O)6]^3+ + H2O ⇌ [Fe(H2O)5(OH)]^2+ + H3O+. With OH- (NaOH) or NH3 (a little): hydroxide precipitates: Fe(OH)2(H2O)4 green (darkens to brown in air), Cu(OH)2(H2O)4 blue, Fe(OH)3(H2O)3 brown, Al(OH)3(H2O)3 white. Excess NaOH dissolves Al(OH)3 (amphoteric): → [Al(OH)4]^- colourless. Excess NH3 dissolves the copper hydroxide: → [Cu(NH3)4(H2O)2]^2+ deep blue. With CO3^2-: M^2+ give carbonates (FeCO3 green, CuCO3 blue-green precipitates); M^3+ are acidic enough to give hydroxides and CO2 (brown Fe(OH)3 or white Al(OH)3 with effervescence): 2[Fe(H2O)6]^3+ + 3CO3^2- → 2Fe(OH)3(H2O)3 + 3CO2 + 3H2O.

#### RP11 Required practical 11: test-tube reactions to identify transition metal ions in aqueous solution
**Method:** to separate samples of each solution, add NaOH(aq) dropwise then in excess; add NH3(aq) dropwise then in excess; add Na2CO3(aq). Record the colour of solutions and precipitates and any effervescence; use the results to identify Fe^2+, Fe^3+, Cu^2+ and Al^3+ in unknowns. Hazards: NaOH is corrosive (goggles); concentrated ammonia fumes (ventilation).
