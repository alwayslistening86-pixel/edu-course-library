# S01_Atomic_Structure - Lesson: Atomic structure, isotopes, mass spectrometry and electron configuration

## Goal
The learner describes the fundamental particles, uses mass number and isotopes, explains time-of-flight mass spectrometry and calculates relative atomic mass from its data, and writes electron configurations and explains ionisation energy trends.

## Syllabus items taught here
- 3.1.1.1 - Fundamental particles
- 3.1.1.2 - Mass number and isotopes
- 3.1.1.3 - Electron configuration

## How to teach this
Ask how anyone could 'weigh' a single atom, and let the learner speculate before introducing the mass spectrometer. Teach from the particles outwards: what the electrons and bonds are doing, then the equation or mechanism, then a worked example with units and sensible significant figures. Use relative atomic masses from AQA's Periodic Table (e.g. H 1.0, C 12.0, O 16.0, Na 23.0, Cl 35.5) and its data sheet values (N_A = 6.022 x 10^23 mol^-1, R = 8.31 J K^-1 mol^-1). Mechanisms are described in words: say where each curly arrow starts (a lone pair or a bond) and where it ends. Every numerical answer here was computed when the course was built.

#### 3.1.1.1 Fundamental particles
Protons (relative mass 1, charge +1), neutrons (1, 0) and electrons (1/1836, -1). The nucleus holds the protons and neutrons; electrons occupy shells (energy levels). The model developed from Dalton's solid spheres through Thomson's plum pudding and Rutherford's nuclear atom to Bohr's shells and the modern orbital model: each was replaced when new evidence could not be explained.

#### 3.1.1.2 Mass number and isotopes
Mass number A = protons + neutrons; atomic number Z = protons. Isotopes have the same Z but different numbers of neutrons: the same chemistry (same electron configuration) but different masses. **Time-of-flight (TOF) mass spectrometry:** (1) ionisation, by electrospray (sample in a volatile solvent forced through a charged needle; each molecule gains a proton, giving MH+) or by electron impact (high-energy electrons knock an electron off, giving X+); (2) acceleration by an electric field so all ions have the same kinetic energy; (3) ion drift through a flight tube, lighter ions moving faster; (4) detection, where ions gain an electron and create a current proportional to their abundance. The spectrum gives m/z and relative abundance. Relative atomic mass = Σ(m/z x abundance)/Σ abundance. *Example:* chlorine: 35 (75%) and 37 (25%) gives A_r = 35.5. (Extension: a spectrum of Cl2 shows molecular ion peaks at 70, 72 and 74 in the ratio 9:6:1.) The mass spectrometer can identify elements and find M_r from the molecular ion peak.

#### 3.1.1.3 Electron configuration
Sub-levels s (1 orbital, 2 electrons), p (3 orbitals, 6), d (5 orbitals, 10); each orbital holds 2 electrons with opposite spins. Fill in order 1s 2s 2p 3s 3p 4s 3d 4p (up to Z = 36). Exceptions: Cr [Ar]3d^5 4s^1 and Cu [Ar]3d^10 4s^1. Ions: remove 4s before 3d (Fe^2+ is [Ar]3d^6). **First ionisation energy:** the energy to remove one mole of electrons from one mole of gaseous atoms: X(g) → X+(g) + e-. Trends: decreases down a group (more shielding, larger radius, despite more protons); generally increases across a period (more protons, similar shielding). Evidence for sub-levels: the drop from Group 2 to 3 (e.g. Mg to Al: Al's electron is in a 3p sub-level, higher in energy) and from Group 5 to 6 (P to S: S's 3p electron is paired, and repulsion makes it easier to remove). Successive ionisation energies show big jumps when a new, inner shell is reached, identifying the group.
