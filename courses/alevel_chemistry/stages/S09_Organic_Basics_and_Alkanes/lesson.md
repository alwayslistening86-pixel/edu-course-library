# S09_Organic_Basics_and_Alkanes - Lesson: Nomenclature, mechanisms, isomerism and alkanes

## Goal
The learner names and draws organic compounds (IUPAC rules, formula types, homologous series), recognises mechanism types and curly-arrow conventions, identifies structural and E/Z isomers, and explains fractional distillation, cracking, combustion and pollution, and free-radical chlorination of alkanes.

## How to teach this
Ask the learner to draw every compound with formula C4H10 they can, and see if they find both. Teach from the particles outwards: what the electrons and bonds are doing, then the equation or mechanism, then a worked example with units and sensible significant figures. Use relative atomic masses from AQA's Periodic Table (e.g. H 1.0, C 12.0, O 16.0, Na 23.0, Cl 35.5) and its data sheet values (N_A = 6.022 x 10^23 mol^-1, R = 8.31 J K^-1 mol^-1). Mechanisms are described in words: say where each curly arrow starts (a lone pair or a bond) and where it ends. Every numerical answer here was computed when the course was built.

#### 3.3.1.1 Nomenclature
Formulae: empirical, molecular, general (CnH2n+2 for alkanes), structural (CH3CH2OH), displayed (all bonds shown) and skeletal (carbon chain as zig-zag lines, H on C omitted). A homologous series has the same general formula and functional group, with successive members differing by CH2. Functional groups: alkene C=C, halogenoalkane C-X, alcohol -OH, aldehyde -CHO, ketone C=O (in a chain), carboxylic acid -COOH, ester -COO-, amine -NH2, nitrile -CN, acyl chloride -COCl, amide -CONH2. IUPAC naming: longest chain (meth-, eth-, prop-, but-, pent-, hex-), lowest numbers for substituents, alphabetical prefixes (e.g. 2-bromo-3-methylbutane), suffixes for the main functional group (propan-2-ol, butanoic acid, but-1-ene).

#### 3.3.1.2 Reaction mechanisms
Reaction types: addition, elimination, substitution, oxidation, reduction, hydrolysis, polymerisation. Mechanisms: free-radical (homolytic fission, radicals shown with a dot, e.g. Cl•) and ionic (heterolytic fission). Curly arrows show the movement of a pair of electrons, starting from a lone pair or a bond and ending at an atom or the space for a new bond. Nucleophile: an electron-pair donor (OH-, CN-, NH3); electrophile: an electron-pair acceptor (H+, NO2+, Br2 as polarised).

#### 3.3.1.3 Isomerism
Structural isomers have the same molecular formula but different structural formulae: chain (butane, methylpropane), position (propan-1-ol, propan-2-ol) and functional group (propanal, propanone). Stereoisomers: the same structural formula but a different spatial arrangement. E/Z isomerism arises in alkenes because rotation about C=C is restricted and each C of the double bond has two different groups. Cahn-Ingold-Prelog priority: higher atomic number = higher priority; Z (zusammen) when the higher-priority groups are on the same side, E (entgegen) on opposite sides. *Example:* but-2-ene has E and Z isomers; but-1-ene does not.

#### 3.3.2.1 Fractional distillation of crude oil
Crude oil is a mixture mostly of alkanes (saturated hydrocarbons, CnH2n+2). Fractional distillation: vaporise, feed into a column hot at the bottom and cooler at the top; fractions condense at different heights by boiling point (chain length): refinery gases, petrol, naphtha, kerosene, diesel, fuel oil, bitumen.

#### 3.3.2.2 Modification of alkanes by cracking
Cracking breaks long-chain alkanes into shorter, more useful alkanes and alkenes (C-C bonds break). Thermal cracking: high temperature (up to 1000 °C) and pressure (up to 70 atm), producing a high proportion of alkenes. Catalytic cracking: a zeolite catalyst, slight pressure, about 450 °C, producing motor fuels and aromatic hydrocarbons. Economic reasons: demand for shorter fractions (petrol) exceeds supply, and alkenes are feedstock for polymers. *Example:* C10H22 → C8H18 + C2H4.

#### 3.3.2.3 Combustion of alkanes
Alkanes are used as fuels: complete combustion gives CO2 and H2O (C3H8 + 5O2 → 3CO2 + 4H2O); incomplete combustion gives CO (toxic: binds haemoglobin) or carbon (soot, particulates). Pollutants: sulfur in fuels gives SO2 (acid rain), removed from power station flue gases by CaO or CaCO3; high engine temperatures form NO and NO2 from N2 and O2; unburned hydrocarbons. Catalytic converters (Pt, Pd, Rh) remove CO, NOx and unburned hydrocarbons: 2CO + 2NO → 2CO2 + N2. CO2, methane and water vapour are greenhouse gases contributing to global warming.

#### 3.3.2.4 Chlorination of alkanes
Methane + chlorine in UV light: free-radical substitution. Initiation: Cl2 → 2Cl• (UV). Propagation: Cl• + CH4 → •CH3 + HCl; •CH3 + Cl2 → CH3Cl + Cl•. Termination: two radicals combine (Cl• + Cl• → Cl2; •CH3 + Cl• → CH3Cl; 2•CH3 → C2H6). Further substitution gives CH2Cl2, CHCl3 and CCl4, so the product is a mixture.
