# S10_Halogenoalkanes_and_Alkenes - Lesson: Halogenoalkanes, ozone depletion and alkenes

## Goal
The learner explains nucleophilic substitution and elimination of halogenoalkanes and their competition, the role of chlorine radicals in ozone depletion, the structure and electrophilic addition reactions of alkenes (including Markownikoff), and addition polymers.

## How to teach this
Ask why CFCs, once called 'perfectly safe', were banned. Teach from the particles outwards: what the electrons and bonds are doing, then the equation or mechanism, then a worked example with units and sensible significant figures. Use relative atomic masses from AQA's Periodic Table (e.g. H 1.0, C 12.0, O 16.0, Na 23.0, Cl 35.5) and its data sheet values (N_A = 6.022 x 10^23 mol^-1, R = 8.31 J K^-1 mol^-1). Mechanisms are described in words: say where each curly arrow starts (a lone pair or a bond) and where it ends. Every numerical answer here was computed when the course was built.

#### 3.3.3.1 Nucleophilic substitution
The C-X bond is polar (δ+ carbon), so nucleophiles attack. Nucleophilic substitution: with warm aqueous NaOH → alcohol; with KCN in ethanol → nitrile (one extra carbon); with excess ethanolic ammonia, heated under pressure → primary amine. Mechanism: the nucleophile's lone pair attacks the δ+ carbon; the C-X bond breaks heterolytically with X leaving as X-. Reactivity depends on bond enthalpy, not polarity: C-I (weakest) reacts fastest, C-F slowest (fluoroalkanes are very unreactive).

#### 3.3.3.2 Elimination
Elimination: with hot ethanolic KOH (OH- acting as a base), a halogenoalkane loses HX to form an alkene. Mechanism: OH- removes an H+ from the carbon next to the C-X carbon; the C-H electrons form C=C; X leaves as X-. Substitution vs elimination: aqueous, warm conditions favour substitution; ethanolic, hot conditions favour elimination. Elimination is used to make alkenes (e.g. from 2-bromopropane → propene).

#### 3.3.3.3 Ozone depletion
Ozone in the upper atmosphere absorbs harmful UV. CFCs are broken by UV to form chlorine radicals: CF2Cl2 → •CF2Cl + Cl•. Chlorine radicals catalyse ozone decomposition: Cl• + O3 → ClO• + O2; ClO• + O3 → 2O2 + Cl•; overall 2O3 → 3O2. One radical can destroy thousands of ozone molecules. Nitrogen oxides (NO•) act similarly. Scientists' evidence led to legislation (the Montreal Protocol) banning CFCs; chemists developed alternatives such as HFCs.

#### 3.3.4.1 Structure, bonding and reactivity
Alkenes are unsaturated (CnH2n) with a C=C: a σ bond and a π bond. The π electrons form a region of high electron density above and below the plane, attracting electrophiles; the π bond is weaker, so alkenes are reactive.

#### 3.3.4.2 Addition reactions of alkenes
Electrophilic addition: with HBr (→ bromoalkane), Br2 (→ dibromoalkane; decolourises bromine water, the test for C=C), and concentrated H2SO4 (→ alkyl hydrogensulfate, hydrolysed by water to an alcohol, with H2SO4 as a catalyst). Mechanism with HBr: the π electrons attack Hδ+; the H-Br bond breaks, giving Br- and a carbocation; Br- donates a lone pair to the carbocation. With Br2: the approaching C=C induces a dipole in Br2. Unsymmetrical alkenes give two products: the major one forms via the more stable carbocation (tertiary > secondary > primary, as alkyl groups release electrons: Markownikoff's rule). *Example:* propene + HBr → mainly 2-bromopropane.

#### 3.3.4.3 Addition polymers
Addition polymers form from alkenes: poly(ethene), poly(propene), poly(chloroethene) (PVC). Draw the repeating unit from the monomer (open the double bond, show extended bonds). Polyalkenes are saturated and unreactive (not biodegradable). PVC is hard and rigid; plasticisers (small molecules) get between the chains and weaken the forces between them, making PVC flexible (e.g. cable insulation). Recycling and disposal are considered in S22.
