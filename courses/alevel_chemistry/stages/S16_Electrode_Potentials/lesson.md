# S16_Electrode_Potentials - Lesson: Electrode potentials and electrochemical cells

## Goal
The learner describes the standard hydrogen electrode and standard conditions, writes cell representations, calculates cell emf from electrode potentials, predicts feasibility from the electrochemical series, explains commercial cells (non-rechargeable, rechargeable, fuel cells), and carries out required practical 8.

## Syllabus items taught here
- 3.1.11.1 - Electrode potentials and cells (A-level only)
- 3.1.11.2 - Commercial applications of electrochemical cells (A-level only)
- RP8 - Required practical 8: measuring the EMF of an electrochemical cell

## How to teach this
Ask why a lemon with copper and zinc strips can light an LED. Teach from the particles outwards: what the electrons and bonds are doing, then the equation or mechanism, then a worked example with units and sensible significant figures. Use relative atomic masses from AQA's Periodic Table (e.g. H 1.0, C 12.0, O 16.0, Na 23.0, Cl 35.5) and its data sheet values (N_A = 6.022 x 10^23 mol^-1, R = 8.31 J K^-1 mol^-1). Mechanisms are described in words: say where each curly arrow starts (a lone pair or a bond) and where it ends. Every numerical answer here was computed when the course was built.

#### 3.1.11.1 Electrode potentials and cells (A-level only)
Half-cells: a metal in a solution of its ions, or a platinum electrode in a solution of two ions of the same element (Fe^3+/Fe^2+). The standard electrode potential E° is measured against the standard hydrogen electrode (H2 at 100 kPa, 1.00 mol dm^-3 H+, 298 K, platinum electrode, E° = 0.00 V), with 1.00 mol dm^-3 ion concentrations. A salt bridge (filter paper soaked in KNO3) completes the circuit without adding reactive ions; a high-resistance voltmeter measures the emf (so no current flows). Cell representation: Zn(s)|Zn^2+(aq)||Cu^2+(aq)|Cu(s), with the more negative electrode on the left: E°cell = E°(right) - E°(left) = +0.34 - (-0.76) = 1.10 V. The electrochemical series lists half-equations as reductions in order of E°: the more positive, the stronger the oxidising agent on the left; the more negative, the stronger the reducing agent on the right. A reaction is predicted to be feasible if E°cell is positive (it may still be slow, and non-standard conditions change E).

#### 3.1.11.2 Commercial applications of electrochemical cells (A-level only)
Commercial cells: non-rechargeable (e.g. alkaline Zn/MnO2), rechargeable (lead-acid, nickel-cadmium, lithium ion: at the positive electrode Li+ + CoO2 + e- → Li+[CoO2]-, at the negative Li → Li+ + e-; recharging reverses the reactions), and fuel cells, which generate electricity from a continuous supply of fuel. The alkaline hydrogen-oxygen fuel cell: negative electrode H2 + 2OH- → 2H2O + 2e-; positive electrode O2 + 2H2O + 4e- → 4OH-; overall 2H2 + O2 → 2H2O (emf 1.23 V). Fuel cells produce only water, but the hydrogen is usually made using fossil fuels, and storage is difficult; lithium cells raise resource and disposal issues.

#### RP8 Required practical 8: measuring the EMF of an electrochemical cell
**Method:** clean the metal electrodes with emery paper; set up two half-cells (e.g. Cu in 1.00 mol dm^-3 CuSO4 and Zn in 1.00 mol dm^-3 ZnSO4) connected by a salt bridge of filter paper soaked in saturated KNO3, and a high-resistance voltmeter; record the emf. Change one half-cell to build a mini electrochemical series; compare with the data-book values. Hazards: CuSO4 is harmful, avoid skin contact.
