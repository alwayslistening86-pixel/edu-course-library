# S05_Kinetics - Lesson: Collision theory, Maxwell-Boltzmann distributions and catalysts

## Goal
The learner explains reaction rates by collision theory, draws and interprets Maxwell-Boltzmann distributions, explains the effects of temperature, concentration, pressure and catalysts, carries out required practical 3, and finds rates from tangents.

## How to teach this
Ask why food keeps longer in a fridge, and why a small rise in temperature speeds reactions so much. Teach from the particles outwards: what the electrons and bonds are doing, then the equation or mechanism, then a worked example with units and sensible significant figures. Use relative atomic masses from AQA's Periodic Table (e.g. H 1.0, C 12.0, O 16.0, Na 23.0, Cl 35.5) and its data sheet values (N_A = 6.022 x 10^23 mol^-1, R = 8.31 J K^-1 mol^-1). Mechanisms are described in words: say where each curly arrow starts (a lone pair or a bond) and where it ends. Every numerical answer here was computed when the course was built.

#### 3.1.5.1 Collision theory
Reactions happen only when particles collide with enough energy (at least the activation energy E_a, the minimum energy for a reaction) and the right orientation. Most collisions are unsuccessful.

#### 3.1.5.2 Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution
The Maxwell-Boltzmann distribution: number of molecules against energy for a gas. It starts at the origin (no molecules have zero energy), has a peak (the most probable energy), is skewed to the right and approaches the energy axis without meeting it (no maximum energy). The area under the curve is the total number of molecules. Molecules to the right of E_a can react.

#### 3.1.5.3 Effect of temperature on reaction rate
A higher temperature moves the peak to the right and lowers it (the area is unchanged, as the number of molecules is the same): many more molecules have energy ≥ E_a, so the rate rises steeply (roughly doubling for a 10 K rise near room temperature for many reactions). Collisions are also slightly more frequent, but the energy effect dominates.

#### 3.1.5.4 Effect of concentration and pressure
Higher concentration of solutions, or pressure of gases, means more particles per unit volume, so more frequent collisions and more successful collisions per unit time. The distribution's shape is unchanged. Larger surface area of a solid also increases collision frequency.

#### 3.1.5.5 Catalysts
A catalyst increases rate without being used up by providing an alternative reaction route of lower activation energy: more molecules exceed the lower E_a on the Maxwell-Boltzmann curve. It does not change ΔH or the position of equilibrium. On an enthalpy profile, the catalysed route has a lower hump.

#### RP3 Required practical 3: investigation of how the rate of a reaction changes with temperature
**Method:** sodium thiosulfate + hydrochloric acid produces a sulfur precipitate; time how long it takes a cross under the flask to disappear ("disappearing cross") at different temperatures (warm the solutions separately in a water bath, then mix). Rate ∝ 1/time. Plot 1/t against temperature: the rate increases more and more steeply. Hazard: sulfur dioxide is produced: work in a ventilated room; asthmatics take care.

#### MS 3.5 Draw and use the slope of a tangent to a curve as a measure of rate of change
For a curve of concentration (or volume of gas) against time, the rate at a moment is the gradient of the tangent at that point: draw the tangent touching the curve, take a large triangle, and compute Δy/Δx with units (e.g. mol dm^-3 s^-1). The initial rate is the tangent's gradient at t = 0.
