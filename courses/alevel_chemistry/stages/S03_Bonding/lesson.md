# S03_Bonding - Lesson: Bonding, structure, shapes and intermolecular forces

## Goal
The learner explains ionic, covalent, dative and metallic bonding, relates structure to physical properties, predicts shapes and bond angles by electron-pair repulsion, explains bond polarity from electronegativity, and explains the three types of intermolecular force and their effects.

## How to teach this
Ask why water boils at 100 °C when methane, a molecule of similar size, boils at -162 °C. Teach from the particles outwards: what the electrons and bonds are doing, then the equation or mechanism, then a worked example with units and sensible significant figures. Use relative atomic masses from AQA's Periodic Table (e.g. H 1.0, C 12.0, O 16.0, Na 23.0, Cl 35.5) and its data sheet values (N_A = 6.022 x 10^23 mol^-1, R = 8.31 J K^-1 mol^-1). Mechanisms are described in words: say where each curly arrow starts (a lone pair or a bond) and where it ends. Every numerical answer here was computed when the course was built.

#### 3.1.3.1 Ionic bonding
Ionic bonding: electrostatic attraction between oppositely charged ions in a lattice. Predict ion charges from group position; know compound ions: sulfate SO4^2-, hydroxide OH-, nitrate NO3-, carbonate CO3^2-, ammonium NH4+. *Example:* aluminium sulfate is Al2(SO4)3.

#### 3.1.3.2 Nature of covalent and dative covalent bonds
Covalent bond: a shared pair of electrons. Dative covalent (coordinate) bond: both electrons in the shared pair come from one atom, e.g. NH4+ (a lone pair on N bonds to H+) and H3O+; drawn as an arrow from donor to acceptor. Once formed, a dative bond is identical to other covalent bonds.

#### 3.1.3.3 Metallic bonding
Metallic bonding: attraction between a lattice of positive ions and delocalised electrons.

#### 3.1.3.4 Bonding and physical properties
Four crystal types: ionic (NaCl), metallic (Mg), macromolecular/giant covalent (diamond, graphite, SiO2) and molecular (I2, ice). Properties: ionic: high melting point (strong attractions throughout the lattice), conducts when molten or dissolved (mobile ions), brittle. Metallic: high melting point, conducts (delocalised electrons), malleable (layers slide). Giant covalent: very high melting point (many strong covalent bonds to break); graphite conducts (delocalised electrons between layers) and is soft (weak forces between layers). Molecular: low melting point (only weak intermolecular forces are overcome; covalent bonds don't break), no conduction. Draw the lattices of diamond, graphite, ice, iodine, magnesium and NaCl. Energy changes in melting and boiling depend on the forces overcome.

#### 3.1.3.5 Shapes of simple molecules and ions
Electron-pair repulsion theory: pairs arrange themselves as far apart as possible; lone pairs repel more than bonding pairs (lone-lone > lone-bond > bond-bond), reducing the angle by about 2.5° per lone pair. Shapes (bonding pairs + lone pairs): 2+0 linear 180° (BeCl2, CO2); 3+0 trigonal planar 120° (BF3); 4+0 tetrahedral 109.5° (CH4); 3+1 trigonal pyramidal 107° (NH3); 2+2 bent 104.5° (H2O); 5+0 trigonal bipyramidal 90° and 120° (PCl5); 4+2 square planar 90° (XeF4); 6+0 octahedral 90° (SF6). Double bonds count as one region. For ions, adjust the electron count for the charge (NH4+ tetrahedral 109.5°).

#### 3.1.3.6 Bond polarity
Electronegativity: the power of an atom to attract the pair of electrons in a covalent bond (Pauling scale: F 4.0, O 3.5, N 3.0, Cl 3.0, C 2.5, H 2.1). It increases across a period and up a group. A difference makes the bond polar (δ+ and δ-). Symmetrical molecules with polar bonds (CO2, CCl4) have no overall dipole because the dipoles cancel.

#### 3.1.3.7 Forces between molecules
Intermolecular forces: **induced dipole-dipole (van der Waals, London)** forces act between all molecules: temporary dipoles from uneven electron distribution induce dipoles in neighbours; stronger with more electrons (larger molecules) and greater surface contact (straight chains boil higher than branched isomers). **Permanent dipole-dipole** forces act between polar molecules (e.g. HCl, propanone). **Hydrogen bonding** occurs between an H atom bonded to N, O or F and a lone pair on N, O or F of another molecule (bond angle about 180° at the H). Hydrogen bonding explains anomalous boiling points of H2O, NH3 and HF compared with other hydrides in their groups, and the lower density of ice (open lattice). Strength order: hydrogen bond > permanent dipole > induced dipole for molecules of similar size.

#### MS 4.1 Use angles and shapes in regular 2D and 3D structures
Use the angles in regular 3D shapes: tetrahedral 109.5°, trigonal planar 120°, octahedral 90°, linear 180°.

#### MS 4.2 Visualise and represent 2D and 3D forms, including two-dimensional representations of 3D objects
Represent 3D molecules in 2D: wedge (towards you), dashed (away) and plain (in the plane) bonds, e.g. CH4 as two plain bonds, one wedge and one dash.
