# S14_Rate_Equations - Lesson: Rate equations, orders, the Arrhenius equation and mechanisms

## Goal
The learner writes rate equations with orders and rate constants and their units, deduces orders from initial-rate data and concentration-time graphs, uses the Arrhenius equation (including ln k against 1/T), relates rate equations to mechanisms and the rate-determining step, carries out required practical 7, and uses calculators, gradients and intercepts.

## How to teach this
Ask whether doubling the concentration of every reactant always doubles the rate, and look for a reaction where it doesn't. Teach from the particles outwards: what the electrons and bonds are doing, then the equation or mechanism, then a worked example with units and sensible significant figures. Use relative atomic masses from AQA's Periodic Table (e.g. H 1.0, C 12.0, O 16.0, Na 23.0, Cl 35.5) and its data sheet values (N_A = 6.022 x 10^23 mol^-1, R = 8.31 J K^-1 mol^-1). Mechanisms are described in words: say where each curly arrow starts (a lone pair or a bond) and where it ends. Every numerical answer here was computed when the course was built.

#### 3.1.9.1 Rate equations (A-level only)
Rate = k[A]^m[B]^n: m and n are the orders (0, 1 or 2), found by experiment, not from the equation; overall order m + n; k is the rate constant, with units that depend on overall order (first: s^-1; second: mol^-1 dm^3 s^-1; third: mol^-2 dm^6 s^-1). k increases with temperature: the Arrhenius equation k = Ae^(-Ea/RT) (A is the pre-exponential factor; Ea in J mol^-1). ln k = ln A - Ea/(RT): a graph of ln k against 1/T is a straight line of gradient -Ea/R. *Example:* a gradient of -6500 K gives Ea = 6500 x 8.31 = 54000 J mol^-1 (54.0 kJ mol^-1). Rate-determining step: the slowest step of a mechanism; species in the rate equation appear in (or before) the rate-determining step. *Example:* 2-bromo-2-methylpropane + OH-: rate = k[(CH3)3CBr], so the slow step is (CH3)3CBr → (CH3)3C+ + Br- (SN1).

#### 3.1.9.2 Determination of rate equation (A-level only)
Initial rates: vary one concentration at a time: doubling [A] doubles the rate (first order), quadruples it (second order), or has no effect (zero order). Concentration-time graphs: zero order gives a straight line; rate-concentration graphs: zero order horizontal, first order straight through the origin, second order a curve (parabola). Clock reactions: the time t for a fixed amount of product to form, rate ∝ 1/t. *Example:* rates for [A] = 0.10, 0.20 mol dm^-3 are 2.0 x 10^-4 and 8.0 x 10^-4 mol dm^-3 s^-1: second order in A.

#### RP7 Required practical 7: measuring the rate of reaction by an initial rate method and by a continuous monitoring method
**Initial rate (iodine clock):** mix solutions containing peroxodisulfate, iodide, a small fixed amount of thiosulfate and starch; time how long until the blue-black colour appears (when the thiosulfate is used up); repeat with different concentrations of one reactant (keeping the total volume constant with water). Rate ∝ 1/t. **Continuous monitoring:** e.g. propanone + iodine in acid: take samples at intervals, quench with sodium hydrogencarbonate and titrate the iodine with thiosulfate (or use a colorimeter); plot [I2] against time: a straight line shows zero order with respect to iodine.

#### MS 0.4 Use calculators to find and use power, exponential and logarithmic functions
Calculators: ln, e^x, log and powers. *Example:* k = Ae^(-Ea/RT) with A = 4.0 x 10^10 s^-1, Ea = 80 kJ mol^-1, T = 300 K: k = 4.63 x 10^-4 s^-1.

#### MS 3.3 Determine the slope and intercept of a linear graph
Slope and intercept of a straight line: from ln k against 1/T, gradient = -Ea/R and intercept = ln A.

#### MS 3.4 Calculate rate of change from a graph showing a linear relationship
Rate of change from a linear graph: a straight concentration-time line (zero order) has a constant gradient, the rate.
