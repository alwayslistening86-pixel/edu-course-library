# S21_Carboxylic_Acids_and_Aromatics - Lesson: Carboxylic acids, esters, acylation and aromatic chemistry

## Goal
The learner describes carboxylic acids, esters (formation, hydrolysis, uses, fats and biodiesel), nucleophilic addition-elimination of acyl chlorides and acid anhydrides, the bonding and stability of benzene, and electrophilic substitution (nitration and Friedel-Crafts acylation).

## How to teach this
Ask why aspirin is made with ethanoic anhydride rather than ethanoyl chloride on an industrial scale. Teach from the particles outwards: what the electrons and bonds are doing, then the equation or mechanism, then a worked example with units and sensible significant figures. Use relative atomic masses from AQA's Periodic Table (e.g. H 1.0, C 12.0, O 16.0, Na 23.0, Cl 35.5) and its data sheet values (N_A = 6.022 x 10^23 mol^-1, R = 8.31 J K^-1 mol^-1). Mechanisms are described in words: say where each curly arrow starts (a lone pair or a bond) and where it ends. Every numerical answer here was computed when the course was built.

#### 3.3.9.1 Carboxylic acids and esters (A-level only)
Carboxylic acids are weak acids; they react with carbonates to give CO2: 2CH3COOH + Na2CO3 → 2CH3COONa + H2O + CO2. Esterification: acid + alcohol ⇌ ester + water, with a concentrated H2SO4 catalyst, warm; name as alkyl alkanoate (ethyl ethanoate). Uses of esters: solvents, plasticisers, perfumes and flavourings. Acid hydrolysis of esters is reversible; alkaline hydrolysis (NaOH) gives the carboxylate salt and alcohol, and goes to completion. Vegetable oils and animal fats are esters of propane-1,2,3-triol (glycerol); hydrolysis with NaOH gives soap (sodium salts of long-chain acids) and glycerol. Biodiesel is a mixture of methyl esters of long-chain carboxylic acids, made by reacting vegetable oils with methanol in the presence of a catalyst.

#### 3.3.9.2 Acylation (A-level only)
Acylation: acyl chlorides (RCOCl) and acid anhydrides ((RCO)2O) react with water (→ carboxylic acid), alcohols (→ esters), ammonia (→ primary amides) and primary amines (→ N-substituted amides), by nucleophilic addition-elimination: the nucleophile's lone pair attacks the δ+ carbonyl carbon (addition), the C=O reforms and Cl- leaves (elimination), then H+ is lost. Acyl chlorides are more reactive and give HCl (misty fumes); anhydrides give a carboxylic acid. Industrially, aspirin is made from 2-hydroxybenzoic acid and ethanoic anhydride: it is cheaper, less corrosive, reacts less violently, and does not produce HCl gas.

#### 3.3.10.1 Bonding (A-level only)
Benzene C6H6: a planar ring of six carbons; each carbon uses three electrons in σ bonds and contributes one p electron to a ring of delocalised π electrons above and below the plane. All C-C bonds are the same length, between single and double. Evidence of extra stability: the enthalpy of hydrogenation of benzene (-208 kJ mol^-1) is much less exothermic than three times that of cyclohexene (3 x -120 = -360 kJ mol^-1), so benzene is about 152 kJ mol^-1 more stable than the Kekulé structure (delocalisation energy). Benzene does not decolourise bromine water; it undergoes substitution rather than addition to keep its stable delocalised ring.

#### 3.3.10.2 Electrophilic substitution (A-level only)
Electrophilic substitution: the delocalised π electrons attack an electrophile, forming an intermediate (the ring's delocalisation is partly broken); then H+ is lost and the ring is restored. Nitration: concentrated HNO3 and concentrated H2SO4 at about 50 °C; the electrophile NO2+ forms by HNO3 + 2H2SO4 → NO2+ + H3O+ + 2HSO4-. Nitration is an important step in making explosives (TNT) and aromatic amines (dyes). Friedel-Crafts acylation: an acyl chloride with AlCl3 catalyst (heated under reflux) gives an aromatic ketone: the electrophile RCO+ forms by RCOCl + AlCl3 → RCO+ + AlCl4-; AlCl4- + H+ → AlCl3 + HCl regenerates the catalyst.
