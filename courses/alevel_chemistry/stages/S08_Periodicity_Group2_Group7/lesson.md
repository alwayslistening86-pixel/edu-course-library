# S08_Periodicity_Group2_Group7 - Lesson: Periodicity, Group 2 and the halogens

## Goal
The learner classifies elements into s, p and d blocks, explains trends across Period 3, describes the trends and reactions of Group 2 and the halogens, the uses of their compounds and of chlorine, and carries out required practical 4.

## How to teach this
Ask why chlorine is added to swimming pools, and why that decision involves weighing risks. Teach from the particles outwards: what the electrons and bonds are doing, then the equation or mechanism, then a worked example with units and sensible significant figures. Use relative atomic masses from AQA's Periodic Table (e.g. H 1.0, C 12.0, O 16.0, Na 23.0, Cl 35.5) and its data sheet values (N_A = 6.022 x 10^23 mol^-1, R = 8.31 J K^-1 mol^-1). Mechanisms are described in words: say where each curly arrow starts (a lone pair or a bond) and where it ends. Every numerical answer here was computed when the course was built.

#### 3.2.1.1 Classification
The Periodic Table is divided into blocks by the sub-level of the highest-energy electron: s block (Groups 1 and 2), p block (Groups 3-0), d block (transition metals), f block.

#### 3.2.1.2 Physical properties of Period 3 elements
Across Period 3 (Na to Ar): atomic radius decreases (more protons, same shielding); first ionisation energy generally increases (with dips at Al and S, S01). Melting points: rise from Na to Al (metallic bonding strengthens: more delocalised electrons and smaller ions with higher charge), peak at Si (giant covalent: many strong bonds), fall to P4, S8, Cl2 and Ar (simple molecular: only van der Waals forces; S8 > P4 > Cl2 > Ar by size).

#### 3.2.2 Group 2, the alkaline earth metals
Group 2 (Mg to Ba): atomic radius increases; first ionisation energy decreases; melting point generally decreases (larger ions, weaker metallic bonding). Reactions with water: Mg reacts slowly with water, but with steam: Mg + H2O(g) → MgO + H2; Ca, Sr, Ba react increasingly vigorously with water: M + 2H2O → M(OH)2 + H2. Mg is used to extract titanium: TiCl4 + 2Mg → Ti + 2MgCl2. Hydroxide solubility increases down the group: Mg(OH)2 is sparingly soluble (antacid, milk of magnesia); Ca(OH)2 is used to neutralise acid soils. Sulfate solubility decreases down the group: BaSO4 is insoluble (barium meal for X-rays is safe because it isn't absorbed); acidified BaCl2 tests for sulfate ions (white precipitate; acid removes carbonate). CaO or CaCO3 removes SO2 from flue gases: CaO + SO2 → CaSO3.

#### 3.2.3.1 Trends in properties
Halogens: electronegativity decreases down the group (larger atoms, more shielding); boiling point increases (more electrons, stronger van der Waals forces). Oxidising ability decreases down the group: displacement reactions (Cl2 + 2Br- → 2Cl- + Br2; colours: Cl2 pale green, Br2 orange, I2 brown in water, purple in a hydrocarbon solvent). Reducing ability of halide ions increases down the group, shown by reactions of solid sodium halides with concentrated H2SO4: NaCl gives HCl (misty fumes) only; NaBr gives HBr, then Br2 (orange fumes) and SO2 (Br- reduces S from +6 to +4); NaI gives HI, I2 (purple/black), SO2, S (yellow solid, +6 → 0) and H2S (bad-egg smell, +6 → -2). Testing halide ions: add nitric acid (to remove carbonate or hydroxide), then silver nitrate: AgCl white (dissolves in dilute NH3), AgBr cream (dissolves in concentrated NH3), AgI yellow (insoluble in NH3).

#### 3.2.3.2 Uses of chlorine and chlorate(I)
Chlorine and water: Cl2 + H2O ⇌ HCl + HClO (a disproportionation: Cl goes from 0 to -1 and +1). In sunlight: 2Cl2 + 2H2O → 4HCl + O2. Chlorine kills bacteria in water treatment; the benefits to health outweigh the toxic effects of chlorine and the risks from chlorinated organic compounds, but this is a societal judgement. Chlorine with cold dilute NaOH: Cl2 + 2NaOH → NaClO + NaCl + H2O (bleach, sodium chlorate(I)).

#### RP4 Required practical 4: test-tube reactions to identify Group 2 cations and NH4+, and halide, OH-, CO3 2- and SO4 2- anions
**Cations:** Group 2 ions with NaOH(aq): Mg^2+ white precipitate (Mg(OH)2), Ca^2+ slight white, Sr^2+ and Ba^2+ little or none; with dilute H2SO4: Ba^2+ white precipitate, Sr^2+ slight, Ca^2+ very slight, Mg^2+ none. Flame tests are an alternative. NH4+: warm with NaOH(aq), the gas (ammonia) turns damp red litmus blue. **Anions:** halides with HNO3 then AgNO3 (as above); OH- turns red litmus blue (pH > 7); CO3^2- with dilute HCl fizzes, and the gas turns limewater milky; SO4^2- with HCl then BaCl2 gives a white precipitate. Test for carbonate before sulfate, and sulfate before halide, to avoid false positives (Ag2SO4 and BaCO3 can precipitate).
