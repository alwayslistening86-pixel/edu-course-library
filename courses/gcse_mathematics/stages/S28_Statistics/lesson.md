# S28_Statistics - Lesson: Sampling, charts, averages, scatter graphs and data quality

## Goal
The learner judges sampling and bias, draws and reads charts and grouped-data diagrams, calculates and compares summary statistics, draws scatter graphs with lines of best fit, and identifies misleading presentation and outliers.

## Syllabus items taught here
- 12.01a - Populations, samples, random sampling and bias: judge how data are collected and sampled
- 12.02a - Charts for categorical and time-series data: read and draw standard charts
- 12.02b - Diagrams for grouped data (Higher): draw and read histograms and cumulative frequency graphs
- 12.03a - Summary statistics; comparing distributions with quartiles (Higher): calculate and compare averages and spread
- 12.03b - Misleading graphs (bad scales or labels): spot misleading presentation
- 12.03c - Scatter diagrams; correlation vs causation: draw scatter graphs and judge relationships
- 12.03d - Outliers and data errors: identify and treat outliers

## How to teach this
Give a small dataset and ask: 'What single number best represents it, and what could make that number misleading?' It raises averages, spread and outliers together.

#### 12.01a Populations, samples and bias
A population is everyone or everything of interest; a sample is the part surveyed. A random sample gives every member an equal chance of selection and avoids bias. A survey of only people in a shopping centre on a weekday morning is biased because it excludes people at work. Larger samples are usually more reliable. Higher: stratified sampling takes a sample in proportion to the size of each group: for 120 girls and 80 boys, a sample of 50 needs (120/200) x 50 = 30 girls and 20 boys. Capture-recapture estimates a population: if 40 fish are tagged and a later sample of 30 has 6 tagged, the population is about 40 x 30 / 6 = 200.

#### 12.02a Charts for categorical and time-series data
Bar charts, pictograms and pie charts show categorical data. In a pie chart the angle for a category is (frequency / total) x 360. Line graphs show time-series data and trends, with the horizontal axis as time. Include titles and labels, and choose scales that use the space. Read values from a chart and compare categories using fractions or percentages.

#### 12.02b Diagrams for grouped data
Higher only. Cumulative frequency graphs plot the running total against the upper class boundary, joined by a smooth curve; read the median at half the total and the quartiles at a quarter and three quarters. Histograms with unequal class widths use frequency density = frequency / class width, so the area (not the height) of each bar represents the frequency. Draw and read both, and label axes fully.

#### 12.03a Summary statistics and comparing distributions
Mean = sum / number of values; median is the middle value when ordered (average of the two middle values if there is an even number); mode is the most common; range = largest - smallest. From a grouped frequency table, estimate the mean using midpoints: sum of (midpoint x frequency) / total frequency; it is only an estimate because the exact values are unknown. Higher: quartiles divide the ordered data into four; interquartile range (IQR) = upper quartile - lower quartile. Compare distributions with one measure of average and one of spread (for example: 'class A has a higher median but a bigger IQR so its marks are less consistent'). Box plots show minimum, lower quartile, median, upper quartile and maximum.

#### 12.03b Misleading graphs
Watch for a vertical axis that does not start at zero, uneven scales, missing labels, three-dimensional effects that distort, or pictograms where the symbol size is increased in both dimensions. Explain the effect: a truncated axis makes small differences look big. Suggest how to redraw honestly.

#### 12.03c Scatter diagrams, correlation and causation
A scatter diagram plots pairs of values. Positive correlation: both increase. Negative correlation: one increases as the other decreases. No correlation: no pattern. A line of best fit passes through the general trend with roughly equal points on either side, and can be used to estimate values within the data range (interpolation) but is unreliable outside it (extrapolation). Correlation does not prove causation: ice cream sales and sunburn cases both rise in summer, but neither causes the other.

#### 12.03d Outliers and data errors
An outlier is a value well outside the pattern of the rest. Check whether it is a genuine value or an error (a height of 17.5 metres for a person, or a mistyped 105 for 10.5). Genuine outliers should not be discarded without saying so; if it is an error, correct or remove it and explain. Outliers strongly affect the mean and range but hardly move the median.
