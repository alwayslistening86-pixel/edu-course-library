# S26_Probability_Basics - Lesson: Probability basics, relative frequency and counting

## Goal
The learner uses the probability scale, calculates theoretical probabilities, estimates probabilities from experiments, and lists outcomes systematically and counts combined outcomes.

## Syllabus items taught here
- 11.01a - Probability scale 0 to 1: place probabilities on a 0 to 1 scale
- 11.01b - Relative frequency recorded in tables and trees: record and use relative frequencies
- 11.01c - Estimating probability from repeated trials: estimate probabilities from experiments
- 11.01d - Equally likely outcomes; expected outcomes: calculate theoretical probabilities and expected counts
- 11.02a - Outcome tables and grids: list outcomes systematically
- 11.02b - Systematic listing and the product rule for counting: count combined outcomes

## How to teach this
Ask: 'If a bag has 3 red and 2 blue counters, what is the chance of blue and how could you check by experiment?' Compare the theory with the results.

#### 11.01a The probability scale
Probability is a number from 0 (impossible) to 1 (certain), written as a fraction, decimal or percentage. Mark events on a 0 to 1 scale: an event with probability 0.5 is an even chance. Probabilities can never be negative or greater than 1. Words such as unlikely or likely should be linked to numbers.

#### 11.01b Recording and using relative frequency
Relative frequency = frequency of an outcome / total number of trials. Record results in a frequency table and convert to fractions or decimals. If a drawing pin lands point up 37 times out of 100, the relative frequency is 0.37. Use tables (and trees) to organise experimental results.

#### 11.01c Estimating probability from repeated trials
The more trials, the better the relative frequency estimates the true probability. To estimate the probability that a biased dice lands on 6 from 240 throws with 52 sixes, use 52/240 = 0.217. Do not assume a fair outcome if the data suggest bias. Compare estimates from small and large samples to explain reliability.

#### 11.01d Equally likely outcomes and expected outcomes
For equally likely outcomes, P(event) = number of favourable outcomes / total number of outcomes. A fair dice has P(prime) = 3/6 = 1/2. The probabilities of all mutually exclusive outcomes add to 1, so P(not A) = 1 - P(A). Expected number = probability x number of trials: on 300 rolls a fair dice is expected to show a 5 on 50 rolls.

#### 11.02a Tables and grids for outcomes
Use a two-way table or sample-space grid to list all outcomes systematically. For the sum of two dice, the grid has 36 outcomes; the sum 7 appears 6 times, so P(7) = 6/36 = 1/6. Use the grid to answer 'at least' or 'more than' questions by counting cells.

#### 11.02b Systematic listing and the product rule for counting
List outcomes in an organised way to avoid missing any: three-letter codes from A, B, C without repeats are ABC, ACB, BAC, BCA, CAB, CBA (6). The product rule: if there are m ways to do one thing and n ways to do another, there are m x n ways to do both. A meal with 3 starters, 4 mains and 2 desserts has 3 x 4 x 2 = 24 combinations. Higher: apply with restrictions, such as a 4-digit code using the digits 1 to 9 with no repeats: 9 x 8 x 7 x 6 = 3024.
