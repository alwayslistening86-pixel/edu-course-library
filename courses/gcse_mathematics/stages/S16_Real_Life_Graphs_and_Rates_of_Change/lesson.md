# S16_Real_Life_Graphs_and_Rates_of_Change - Lesson: Real-life graphs, rates of change and areas under graphs

## Goal
The learner builds and interprets graphs in real contexts, interprets and estimates gradients as rates of change, and estimates and interprets areas under graphs.

## Syllabus items taught here
- 7.04a - Real-life graphs; proportion relationships: construct and interpret graphs in context
- 7.04b - Gradient as rate of change: interpret and estimate gradients as rates
- 7.04c - Area under a graph: estimate and interpret areas under graphs

## How to teach this
Show a distance-time graph and ask: 'What is happening in each section of this journey, and how do you know?' Only afterwards introduce gradient as speed.

#### 7.04a Constructing and interpreting real-life graphs
In a distance-time graph a horizontal line means stationary, a steeper line means faster and a downward line means returning. In a speed-time graph a horizontal line means constant speed and a sloping line means changing speed. Conversion graphs (miles to km) are straight lines through the origin, showing direct proportion. Inverse proportion gives a curve that falls as x increases. Filling-container graphs steepen or flatten depending on the width of the container. Always read the axis labels and units, and describe what each section means in the context.

#### 7.04b Gradient as rate of change
The gradient of a distance-time graph is speed; of a speed-time graph, acceleration; of a cost graph, cost per unit. Use units from the axes: a change of 30 km in 0.5 h is 60 km/h. For a curve, the gradient at a point is estimated by drawing a tangent that just touches the curve there and finding the gradient of that line. The gradient of a chord between two points gives the average rate of change between them.

#### 7.04c Areas under graphs
The area under a speed-time graph is the distance travelled. Split the shape into triangles, rectangles and trapezia. For a train that accelerates from 0 to 12 m/s in 6 s, travels at 12 m/s for 10 s and decelerates to rest in 4 s: distance = 1/2 x 6 x 12 + 12 x 10 + 1/2 x 4 x 12 = 36 + 120 + 24 = 180 m. For a curved graph, estimate by dividing into strips (trapezia) and say whether the estimate is an over- or under-estimate depending on whether the curve is above or below the chords. Always interpret the answer with units.
