# S05_Indices_Roots_Standard_Form_and_Surds - Lesson: Powers, roots, standard form and surds

## Goal
The learner evaluates powers and roots, applies the laws of indices, works in standard form, and (Higher) uses fractional indices, simplifies surds and rationalises denominators.

## Syllabus items taught here
- 3.01a - Positive and negative indices; fractional indices (Higher): interpret and use whole-number and fractional powers
- 3.01b - Integer powers and exact roots; recognising simple powers: evaluate powers and roots and recall squares, cubes and their roots
- 3.01c - Laws of indices (Higher): combine powers of one base by adding, subtracting and multiplying indices
- 3.02a - Standard form: interpret, order, convert: write and read very large and very small numbers in standard form
- 3.02b - Arithmetic with standard form (Higher): calculate with numbers in standard form
- 3.03a - Exact calculation with fractions, multiples of pi and surds: leave answers exact instead of rounding
- 3.03b - Simplify surds; rationalise denominators (Higher): simplify surd expressions and remove surds from denominators

## How to teach this
Ask: 'What does 2^-1 actually mean?' Build the sequence 2^3, 2^2, 2^1, 2^0, 2^-1 so the learner sees each step halve the number, then state the rule.

#### 3.01a Positive, negative and fractional indices
a^n means n copies of a multiplied together. a^0 = 1 for any a other than 0. A negative index means a reciprocal: 5^-2 = 1/5^2 = 1/25, and (2/3)^-1 = 3/2. Higher: a fractional index is a root: a^(1/2) is the square root of a and a^(1/3) is the cube root; a^(m/n) is the nth root of a, raised to the power m. So 8^(2/3) = (cube root of 8)^2 = 2^2 = 4, and 16^(-3/4) = 1 / (fourth root of 16)^3 = 1/8. Take the root first: it keeps numbers small.

#### 3.01b Integer powers, exact roots and recalling simple powers
Recall squares to 15^2, cubes of 1 to 5 and 10, powers of 2 up to 2^10, and powers of 10. Evaluate square and cube roots of these exactly (sqrt(169) = 13, cube root of 125 = 5). Evaluate expressions like 2^4 + 3^3 without a calculator. Higher: estimate powers and roots of non-perfect numbers by bracketing them between known values.

#### 3.01c Laws of indices
For the same base: a^m x a^n = a^(m+n); a^m / a^n = a^(m-n); (a^m)^n = a^(mn). Illustrate with a^3 x a^2 = (aaa)(aa) = a^5 so the rule is not arbitrary. Applying them: 3^7 / 3^4 = 3^3 = 27; (2^3)^2 x 2^-1 = 2^5 = 32; (x^4)^3 = x^12. The laws do not apply to different bases (2^3 x 3^2 stays as it is or is evaluated as 8 x 9 = 72). Higher: combine with fractional and negative indices, e.g. (27x^6)^(2/3) = 9x^4.

#### 3.02a Standard form
A number in standard form is a x 10^n where 1 <= a < 10 and n is an integer. 4 560 000 = 4.56 x 10^6; 0.000 072 = 7.2 x 10^-5. To convert, count how many places the decimal point moves. To order numbers, compare the powers of 10 first, then the a values. Calculators show standard form as, for example, 4.56E6; the answer must be written properly with the multiplication and the power of ten.

#### 3.02b Arithmetic with standard form
Multiply the a values and add the powers, dividing the a values and subtracting the powers, then re-standardise if a is not between 1 and 10: (3 x 10^5)(4 x 10^3) = 12 x 10^8 = 1.2 x 10^9. To add or subtract, first write both with the same power of 10: 5.2 x 10^4 + 3 x 10^3 = 5.2 x 10^4 + 0.3 x 10^4 = 5.5 x 10^4. Practise sensible contexts such as distances between planets or the mass of a molecule.

#### 3.03a Exact calculation with fractions, multiples of pi and surds
When a question asks for an exact answer, leave results as fractions, in terms of pi (a circle of radius 3 has area 9 pi), or as surds (the diagonal of a unit square is sqrt(2)); do not round early. Combine like terms: 3 pi + 2 pi = 5 pi, and 4 sqrt(2) - sqrt(2) = 3 sqrt(2). Keep the exact form throughout a multi-step calculation and only convert to a decimal at the end if asked.

#### 3.03b Simplifying surds and rationalising denominators
Simplify by pulling out square factors: sqrt(72) = sqrt(36 x 2) = 6 sqrt(2). Multiply: sqrt(a) x sqrt(b) = sqrt(ab); sqrt(a) x sqrt(a) = a. Expand brackets: (2 + sqrt(3))(2 - sqrt(3)) = 4 - 3 = 1 (a difference of two squares, so the surds vanish). To rationalise a denominator, multiply top and bottom by the surd: 6 / sqrt(3) = 6 sqrt(3) / 3 = 2 sqrt(3). For a denominator like 1 / (3 + sqrt(2)), multiply top and bottom by the conjugate (3 - sqrt(2)) to give (3 - sqrt(2)) / 7.
