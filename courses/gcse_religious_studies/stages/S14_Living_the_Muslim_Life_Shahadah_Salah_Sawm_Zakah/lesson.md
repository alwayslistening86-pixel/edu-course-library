# S14_Living_the_Muslim_Life_Shahadah_Salah_Sawm_Zakah - Lesson: Living the Muslim life: Shahadah, Salah, Sawm and the Night of Power, and Zakah

## Goal
The learner can explain the Shahadah's role and significance, explain how and why Salah is performed, explain fasting during Ramadan and the Night of Power's significance, and explain Zakah's nature, role and significance.

## Syllabus items taught here
- I-LIV.1 - Islam: Shahadah: Explain the nature, role and significance of the Shahadah
- I-LIV.2 - Islam: Salah: Explain how and why Salah is performed, including in the mosque
- I-LIV.3 - Islam: Sawm and the Night of Power: Explain fasting during Ramadan and the significance of the Night of Power
- I-LIV.4 - Islam: Zakah: Explain the nature, role and significance of Zakah

## How to teach this
Open with: 'The Five Pillars are the framework of Muslim daily, yearly and lifetime practice. Which one do you think happens most often: daily, yearly, or once in a lifetime? We will meet all three types.'

#### I-LIV.1 Islam: Shahadah
The Shahadah is the Muslim declaration of faith: 'There is no god but Allah, and Muhammad is the messenger of Allah', affirming both Tawhid (Allah's absolute oneness) and Risalah (Muhammad's role as Allah's final prophet) in a single statement. It is the first of the Five Pillars for Sunni Muslims and is also central for Shi'a Muslims, though expressed within their own Five Roots framework; sincerely reciting it is the act by which a person formally becomes a Muslim. The Shahadah is recited in daily prayer, whispered into a newborn baby's ear as one of their first words, and ideally spoken as a Muslim's final words before death, showing its role as the foundational statement running through a Muslim's entire life from birth to death.

#### I-LIV.2 Islam: Salah
Salah, ritual prayer, is performed five times daily at set times (dawn, midday, afternoon, sunset and evening), preceded by wudu (ritual washing, including hands, mouth, face, arms and feet) to ensure physical and spiritual purity before prayer. Prayer is performed facing the Qibla, the direction of the Ka'aba in Makkah, using a set sequence of movements (standing, bowing, prostrating, sitting) called rak'ahs, each paired with set recitations from the Qur'an, including the opening chapter, al-Fatiha. Salah can be performed individually at home or work, or communally, especially the important Friday midday prayer (Jummah), usually performed together at the mosque with a sermon (khutbah). Salah structures a Muslim's entire day around regular, disciplined remembrance of and submission to Allah.

#### I-LIV.3 Islam: Sawm and the Night of Power
Sawm is fasting during the month of Ramadan, abstaining from food, drink and other physical needs from dawn until sunset, undertaken to build self-discipline, empathy with the poor and hungry, and closeness to Allah; certain groups (including the sick, travellers, pregnant or breastfeeding women, and young children) are exempted, sometimes required to make up missed days later or provide charity instead. Laylat al-Qadr, the Night of Power, commemorates the night the Qur'an's revelation to Muhammad began, believed to fall on one of the last ten odd nights of Ramadan; it is considered the holiest night of the Islamic calendar, when prayers and worship are held to be especially valuable, and many Muslims spend it in intensive prayer and Qur'an recitation.

#### I-LIV.4 Islam: Zakah
Zakah is obligatory almsgiving, typically calculated as 2.5% of a Muslim's savings and qualifying wealth above a minimum threshold (nisab), given annually to support those in need and specific other purposes set out in Islamic teaching, such as helping the poor, those in debt and travellers in difficulty. Its role is both practical, redistributing wealth and reducing poverty within the community, and spiritual, purifying the giver's remaining wealth and expressing gratitude to Allah and responsibility towards others, since the word Zakah itself relates to purification and growth. As one of the Five Pillars, it establishes charitable giving not as optional generosity but as a core, structured religious obligation for financially able Muslims, reflecting Islam's strong emphasis on social justice and communal responsibility.
