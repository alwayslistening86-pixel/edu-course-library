# S12_Muslim_Beliefs_Part_2_and_Crime_and_Sin - Lesson: Muslim beliefs: predestination and the afterlife; Islamic teaching on crime and punishment's purpose

## Goal
The learner can explain the importance of predestination for Muslims, explain Muslim teaching on judgement, paradise and hell, explain Muslim teaching on crime and sin, and explain the purposes of punishment in Islam.

## Syllabus items taught here
- I-BEL.5 - Islam: al-Qadr, predestination: Explain the nature and importance of predestination for Muslims
- I-BEL.6 - Islam: Akhirah, life after death: Explain Muslim teaching on judgement, paradise and hell
- I-CP.1 - Islam: Muslim teachings about crime and sin: Explain the Qur'anic basis and significance of Muslim teaching on crime and sin
- I-CP.2 - Islam: the aims and purposes of punishment: Explain Muslim teaching on punishment as deterrence, reform and protection

## How to teach this
Open with: 'If Allah already knows everything that will happen, are humans still free to choose right from wrong? And if we are, how does Islam expect wrongdoing to be dealt with?' Use this to bridge belief into ethics.

#### I-BEL.5 Islam: al-Qadr, predestination
Al-Qadr is the belief that Allah, being all-knowing, already knows everything that will happen, including human choices, since Allah exists outside and beyond human time. Most Muslims hold this alongside belief in genuine human free will and moral responsibility: Allah's foreknowledge does not force or cause a person's choices, it simply knows them in advance, so people remain accountable for their actions and will be judged accordingly. Al-Qadr is significant for Muslims because it offers reassurance that nothing happens outside Allah's knowledge and control, encouraging trust and acceptance ('Insha'Allah', 'if Allah wills') particularly at times of hardship, while still requiring Muslims to strive, choose rightly and take responsibility for their own conduct in daily life.

#### I-BEL.6 Islam: Akhirah, life after death
Akhirah is belief in life after death, including the Day of Judgement, when every person's deeds will be assessed by Allah with perfect fairness. Muslims believe this life is a test, and that a person's record of good and bad deeds will determine their fate in the afterlife: paradise (Jannah), often described in the Qur'an with rich imagery of gardens, peace and closeness to Allah, for those judged worthy, or hell (Jahannam), a place of punishment and separation from Allah's mercy, for those who reject faith or persist in serious wrongdoing without repentance. Belief in Akhirah gives Muslim life ultimate moral seriousness and purpose, encouraging righteous conduct, worship and repentance in this life in preparation for judgement in the next.

#### I-CP.1 Islam: Muslim teachings about crime and sin
Islam distinguishes crime, breaking the law of the state or Islamic legal codes, from sin, wrongdoing against Allah's moral commands, though the two overlap significantly in traditional Islamic teaching, since Shari'ah (Islamic law, derived from the Qur'an and the Prophet's example) covers both religious and social conduct. The Qur'an provides the basis for identifying serious wrongs, and Muslim teaching holds that all sin, whether or not it is also a crime under civil law, matters because it damages the sinner's relationship with Allah and with the wider community. Understandings of exactly which acts count as the most serious crimes, and how strictly traditional legal penalties should be applied today, vary between and within Sunni and Shi'a traditions and across different Muslim-majority countries with different legal systems.

#### I-CP.2 Islam: the aims and purposes of punishment
Muslim teaching holds that punishment should serve several purposes together, not retribution alone: deterrence, discouraging the offender and others in the community from committing similar wrongs; reform, giving the offender a genuine opportunity to repent and change their behaviour; and protection, safeguarding the wider community from further harm. Surah 2:178-179 discusses the principle of proportionate retribution (qisas) in the context of taking a life, while emphasising that accepting compensation and showing mercy is better where possible, illustrating how Islamic teaching values mercy and reform alongside justice. Muslims broadly agree punishment should be fair and proportionate to the offence, but understandings of exactly how these purposes should be balanced in modern legal practice differ across Muslim communities and countries.
