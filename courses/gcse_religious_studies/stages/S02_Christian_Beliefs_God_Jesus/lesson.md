# S02_Christian_Beliefs_God_Jesus - Lesson: Christian beliefs: the Trinity, creation, the incarnation and the last days of Jesus

## Goal
The learner can explain the nature and significance of the Trinity, explain the biblical creation account(s) and their importance, explain the incarnation, and narrate and explain the significance of the last days of Jesus' earthly life.

## Syllabus items taught here
- C-BEL.1 - Christianity: the Trinity: Explain the nature and significance of the Trinity
- C-BEL.2 - Christianity: creation: Explain the biblical creation account(s) and their importance today
- C-BEL.3 - Christianity: the incarnation: Explain the incarnation and its significance
- C-BEL.4 - Christianity: the last days of Jesus' earthly life: Narrate and explain the significance of the Last Supper, arrest, trial, crucifixion, resurrection and ascension

## How to teach this
Open with: 'Christians say there is only one God, yet also speak of the Father, the Son and the Holy Spirit. How can both be true?' Use the answer to introduce the Trinity as this stage's anchor idea.

#### C-BEL.1 Christianity: the Trinity
The Trinity is the Christian belief that God is one being who exists eternally as three persons: the Father, the Son (Jesus Christ) and the Holy Spirit, each fully and equally God, yet together only one God, not three gods. This is expressed in the Nicene Creed, a statement of core Christian belief used in worship. Christians hold that the oneness of God (there is only one divine being) and the distinctness of each person (each has a different role: the Father as creator, the Son as redeemer, the Spirit as sustainer and guide) must both be held together, even though this is acknowledged as a mystery beyond full human understanding. The Trinity shapes Christian worship and prayer today, for example in the common blessing 'in the name of the Father, and of the Son, and of the Holy Spirit'.

#### C-BEL.2 Christianity: creation
Genesis 1-3 gives two creation accounts: God creating the world in six days and resting on the seventh, and God forming Adam from dust and Eve from Adam's rib in the Garden of Eden. Christians interpret these accounts differently: some read them literally, as a historical account of how the world was made; others read them metaphorically, as a theological account of why the world exists and humanity's relationship to God, compatible with scientific accounts such as evolution. John 1:1-18 describes creation through the divine 'Word' (Christ) and Spirit, reinforcing the Trinity's role in creation. For most Christians today, the creation accounts matter chiefly for what they teach about God's power, purpose and care for the world, and humanity's responsibility within it, whichever way they are read.

#### C-BEL.3 Christianity: the incarnation
The incarnation is the Christian belief that God the Son became fully human in Jesus Christ while remaining fully God, so Jesus has two complete natures, divine and human, united in one person. John 1:1-18 ('the Word became flesh and made his dwelling among us') and 1 Timothy 3:16 are key biblical texts. The incarnation is significant because it shows God entering human experience directly, including suffering, to make salvation possible; it grounds Christmas, celebrating Jesus' birth, and underlies later Christian belief that Jesus' death and resurrection can save humanity, since only a being who is both divine and human could bridge the gap between God and people. It also gives Christians a model of how to live, since Jesus' human life shows God's character in action.

#### C-BEL.4 Christianity: the last days of Jesus' earthly life
Luke 22-24 narrates Jesus' final days: the Last Supper, where Jesus shared bread and wine with his disciples, instituting what became the Eucharist; his betrayal by Judas Iscariot and arrest in the Garden of Gethsemane; his trial before Jewish and Roman authorities; his crucifixion, a Roman execution by nailing to a cross, at Golgotha; his resurrection, rising from death on the third day, discovered by women visiting his empty tomb; and his ascension, returning to heaven forty days later. These events are central to Christian belief: the crucifixion is understood as the means by which sin is atoned for, and the resurrection as proof of Jesus' divine identity and victory over death, underpinning Christian hope for eternal life and shaping the whole liturgical year, especially Holy Week and Easter.
