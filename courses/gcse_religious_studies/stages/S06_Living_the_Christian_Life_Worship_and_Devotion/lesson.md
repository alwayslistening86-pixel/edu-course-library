# S06_Living_the_Christian_Life_Worship_and_Devotion - Lesson: Living the Christian life: worship, sacraments, prayer and pilgrimage

## Goal
The learner can explain and contrast forms of Christian worship, explain baptism and the Eucharist across denominations, explain types of Christian prayer, and explain the purpose of pilgrimage and divergent views on its importance.

## Syllabus items taught here
- C-LIV.1 - Christianity: liturgical, non-liturgical and informal worship: Explain and contrast forms of Christian worship and their significance
- C-LIV.2 - Christianity: sacraments in two denominations: Explain baptism and the Eucharist and divergent attitudes across denominations
- C-LIV.3 - Christianity: prayer: Explain types of Christian prayer and when each is used
- C-LIV.4 - Christianity: pilgrimage: Explain the nature and purpose of pilgrimage and divergent views on its importance

## How to teach this
Open with: 'If you visited a Catholic Mass, an Anglican Communion service and a Pentecostal service on the same Sunday, what might look completely different, and what would stay the same?'

#### C-LIV.1 Christianity: liturgical, non-liturgical and informal worship
Liturgical worship follows a fixed, structured order of service, often written down (for example in the Book of Common Prayer), giving consistency and a shared, tradition-rooted pattern, valued for its stability and continuity with the wider Church across time. Non-liturgical worship follows a looser, more flexible structure, often built around a sermon, with more room for spontaneity. Informal and individual worship includes activities outside formal services, such as personal Bible study, private prayer, or small informal gatherings. Some denominations, including some Pentecostal churches, worship with far less fixed structure, emphasising spontaneous prayer, singing and perceived direct movement of the Holy Spirit. Christians disagree about which form is most meaningful: some value liturgy's order and tradition, others value informal worship's immediacy and personal engagement.

#### C-LIV.2 Christianity: sacraments in two denominations
Sacraments (or, in some traditions, ordinances) are rites believed to convey God's grace through a visible sign. Baptism, using water, marks entry into the Christian faith and community; the Eucharist (also called Holy Communion or Mass), using bread and wine, commemorates Jesus' Last Supper and death. Across denominations, meaning and celebration diverge considerably: Catholic teaching holds transubstantiation, that the bread and wine actually become Christ's body and blood in substance, referenced in the 39 Articles' critique (Articles XXV-XXXVI set out the more symbolic Anglican Protestant position); many Protestant traditions instead understand the Eucharist symbolically, as a memorial and spiritual, not literal, communion with Christ. Infant baptism (common in Catholic and Anglican practice) is likewise contrasted with believer's baptism (practised by Baptists and others), given only to those old enough to make a personal declaration of faith.

#### C-LIV.3 Christianity: prayer
Christian prayer takes several forms. Set (or liturgical) prayers use fixed, traditional words, including the Lord's Prayer (Matthew 6:5-14, which Jesus taught his disciples as a model prayer), used in both personal and communal worship for their shared, memorised familiarity. Informal prayer uses the believer's own spontaneous words, for thanksgiving, confession, request or simply conversation with God, valued for its personal, immediate character. Different types suit different situations: set prayers for formal, communal worship and moments needing shared, careful words; informal prayer for personal, everyday communication with God. Christians differ on which they consider most important: some value set prayer's discipline and shared tradition, others value informal prayer's spontaneity and personal honesty.

#### C-LIV.4 Christianity: pilgrimage
Pilgrimage is a journey to a place of religious significance, undertaken for spiritual growth, devotion or as an act of penance; Luke 2:41-43 records Jesus' own family undertaking a religious journey to Jerusalem. Sites of Christian pilgrimage include Jerusalem (site of Jesus' death and resurrection), Iona (a Scottish island linked to early Christian mission), Taize (an ecumenical community in France drawing young pilgrims for prayer and reflection) and Walsingham (an English Marian shrine, especially significant to Catholic and some Anglican pilgrims). Christians diverge on pilgrimage's importance: some, especially in Catholic and Orthodox tradition, value it highly as a physical act of devotion and a way of deepening faith through sacrifice and encounter with sacred places; others, especially in more Protestant traditions, regard it as spiritually optional, holding that God can be encountered anywhere and that pilgrimage risks becoming empty ritual rather than genuine faith.
