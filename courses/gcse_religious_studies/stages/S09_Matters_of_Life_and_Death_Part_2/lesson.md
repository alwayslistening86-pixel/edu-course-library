# S09_Matters_of_Life_and_Death_Part_2 - Lesson: Matters of life and death: life after death, euthanasia, and the natural world

## Goal
The learner can explain Christian beliefs supporting life after death, explain why Christians reject named non-religious arguments against an afterlife, explain divergent Christian and non-religious responses to euthanasia, and explain Christian teaching on stewardship and environmental and animal issues.

## Syllabus items taught here
- C-MLD.5 - Christianity: life after death: Explain Christian beliefs and arguments supporting life after death
- C-MLD.6 - Christianity: responses to non-religious arguments against an afterlife: Explain why Christians reject specific non-religious arguments against an afterlife
- C-MLD.7 - Christianity: euthanasia: Explain divergent Christian and non-religious responses to euthanasia
- C-MLD.8 - Christianity: stewardship and the natural world: Explain Christian teaching on stewardship, pollution, global warming and animal rights

## How to teach this
Open with: 'Christians believe in life after death partly because of one historical claim. What is it, and why does it matter so much to the argument?'

#### C-MLD.5 Christianity: life after death
Christian belief in life after death rests centrally on Jesus' own resurrection, understood as historical proof that death is not final; Ephesians 2:1-10 speaks of believers being made spiritually alive with Christ. Christians offer several supporting arguments: remembered lives (near-death or after-death experiences some interpret as evidence), paranormal experiences (a minority appeal to reported contact with the deceased, though this is theologically controversial for many Christians), logical arguments (that a just, loving God would not allow good and evil to go permanently unaddressed without some final reckoning), reward (that a God of justice must ultimately reward faithfulness and punish wrongdoing), comfort (belief in an afterlife eases grief and fear of death) and the hope of meeting loved ones again. Belief in life after death gives Christian life meaning, moral seriousness and hope beyond mortal existence.

#### C-MLD.6 Christianity: responses to non-religious arguments against an afterlife
Non-religious critics raise several objections to belief in an afterlife: that it functions mainly as a source of comfort invented to cope with fear of death rather than reflecting reality; that there is a lack of scientific evidence for any afterlife; that reported paranormal or after-death experiences are often fraudulent or explicable by natural causes; and that belief in judgement after death has sometimes served as a tool of social control, encouraging obedience through fear. Christians, drawing on 1 Peter 3:18-22's teaching on Christ's victory over death, respond that Jesus' resurrection is a distinct historical claim, not merely a comforting story, that faith is a reasonable response to evidence and testimony even without full scientific proof, and that belief in ultimate justice need not be reduced to mere social control simply because it also encourages moral behaviour.

#### C-MLD.7 Christianity: euthanasia
Euthanasia is deliberately ending a person's life to relieve suffering, usually at their request, in the case of terminal or incurable illness. Christian teaching, again rooted in sanctity of life, generally opposes euthanasia, holding that only God has the right to give and take life, referencing Job 2:1-10's example of enduring suffering rather than seeking death; many Christian communities instead strongly support hospice care, which manages pain and provides dignified, compassionate end-of-life support without deliberately ending life. Some more liberal Christians, however, argue that compassion and situation ethics can justify euthanasia in exceptional cases of extreme, unrelievable suffering. Non-religious, including atheist and Humanist, arguments typically emphasise personal autonomy (the right to choose the manner and timing of one's own death) and the relief of unnecessary suffering, positions many Christians respond to by defending sanctity of life and the value of properly resourced palliative care instead.

#### C-MLD.8 Christianity: stewardship and the natural world
Christian teaching on the environment centres on stewardship: the belief that humans are given responsibility by God to care for and manage creation wisely on God's behalf, not to own or exploit it without limit, expressed in statements such as the Christian Declaration on Nature (Assisi, 1986). This informs Christian responses to modern threats including pollution, global warming and the overuse of natural resources, which many Christians see as a failure of proper stewardship requiring active response, such as reducing consumption or supporting environmental campaigns. Christian attitudes to animal rights, animal experimentation and eating animals for food also diverge: some Christians, applying stewardship strictly, argue for minimising animal suffering and reducing meat consumption; others hold that scripture permits responsible use of animals for legitimate human need, sometimes reasoning through utilitarian arguments (weighing overall benefit and harm) alongside specifically Christian teaching.
