# S15_Living_the_Muslim_Life_Hajj_Obligatory_Acts_Jihad_Festivals - Lesson: Living the Muslim life: Hajj, the Ten Obligatory Acts, the meaning of Jihad, and festivals

## Goal
The learner can explain how and why Hajj is performed, explain the Ten Obligatory Acts of Shi'a Islam, explain lesser and greater Jihad, and explain the main Sunni and Shi'a festivals.

## Syllabus items taught here
- I-LIV.5 - Islam: Hajj: Explain how and why Hajj is performed
- I-LIV.6 - Islam: the Ten Obligatory Acts of Shi'a Islam: Explain the nature and importance of the Ten Obligatory Acts
- I-LIV.7 - Islam: the meaning of Jihad: Explain lesser and greater Jihad and the conditions for lesser Jihad
- I-LIV.8 - Islam: festivals in Sunni and Shi'a Islam: Explain Id-ul-Fitr, Id-ul-Adha, Ashura and Id-ul-Ghadeer

## How to teach this
Open with: 'Every physically and financially able Muslim is expected to make one journey once in their lifetime. What do you already know about it, and why might a shared journey to one place matter so much to a global religion?'

#### I-LIV.5 Islam: Hajj
Hajj is the pilgrimage to Makkah, required at least once in a lifetime of every Muslim who is physically and financially able, performed during a set period in the Islamic month of Dhul Hijjah. Rites include wearing simple white garments (ihram) symbolising equality and purity, circling the Ka'aba seven times (tawaf), walking between the hills of Safa and Marwah (recalling Hagar's search for water), standing in prayer on the plain of Arafat (wuquf, considered the pilgrimage's spiritual high point), and a symbolic stoning of pillars representing rejection of temptation. Hajj's origins are linked to the Prophet Ibrahim and his family. Its importance lies in uniting Muslims worldwide, regardless of nationality or wealth, in shared worship and equality before Allah, and in offering spiritual renewal through obedience, humility and remembrance.

#### I-LIV.6 Islam: the Ten Obligatory Acts of Shi'a Islam
Shi'a Islam structures religious practice through Ten Obligatory Acts (furu al-din, 'branches of religion'), which include Salah, Sawm, Zakah, Khums (an additional religious tax on certain income, distinct from Zakah), Hajj, Jihad, and further duties including commanding what is right and forbidding what is wrong, and expressing love and loyalty towards the Prophet's family and rightful successors (tawalla) and disassociation from their enemies (tabarra). These overlap significantly with Sunni practice (Salah, Sawm, Zakah, Hajj) while reflecting Shi'a Islam's own distinct emphases, particularly around the Imamate and khums. Their importance for Shi'a Muslims lies in providing a comprehensive framework, alongside the Five Roots of belief, structuring both correct belief and correct practice as complementary, necessary parts of a faithful religious life.

#### I-LIV.7 Islam: the meaning of Jihad
Jihad literally means 'struggle' or 'striving' and has two main forms. Greater Jihad refers to the internal, spiritual struggle to live righteously, resist temptation and grow closer to Allah, considered by many Muslims the more important and demanding form of Jihad. Lesser Jihad refers to external struggle, including, in specific circumstances, physical struggle or fighting in defence of the faith or community. Traditional teaching sets strict conditions for lesser Jihad to be legitimate: it must be defensive rather than for conquest or personal gain, declared by a properly recognised religious or community authority, and conducted according to strict ethical rules protecting non-combatants. Divergent understandings exist within Islam about how these conditions apply today, and many Muslims stress that Jihad is very frequently misused or misunderstood, especially in Western media, as meaning simply 'holy war'.

#### I-LIV.8 Islam: festivals in Sunni and Shi'a Islam
Sunni Islam's two major festivals are Id-ul-Fitr, celebrating the end of the Ramadan fast with communal prayer, charity and feasting, and Id-ul-Adha, the 'festival of sacrifice', commemorating Ibrahim's willingness to sacrifice his son in obedience to Allah (before Allah provided a ram instead) and coinciding with the end of Hajj, marked by animal sacrifice and sharing meat with family, friends and the poor. Shi'a Islam observes these too, alongside distinctively Shi'a commemorations: Ashura, a solemn day of mourning marking the martyrdom of Husayn, grandson of the Prophet, at the Battle of Karbala, central to Shi'a religious identity and often marked with processions and mourning rituals, and Id-ul-Ghadeer, celebrating the Prophet's declaration at Ghadir Khumm, understood by Shi'a Muslims as designating Ali as his rightful successor.
