# S16_Peace_and_Conflict_Teachings_Causes_and_Holy_War - Lesson: Peace and conflict: Muslim teachings on peace, causes of conflict, holy war, and weapons of mass destruction

## Goal
The learner can explain the importance of peace for Muslims, explain Muslim responses to the causes of conflict, explain Muslim teaching on holy war and the conditions for declaring it, and explain divergent Muslim responses to weapons of mass destruction.

## Syllabus items taught here
- I-PC.1 - Islam: Muslim teachings about peace: Explain the Qur'anic basis and importance of peace for Muslims
- I-PC.2 - Islam: Muslim responses to the causes of conflict: Explain Muslim responses to greed, power, injustice and territorial disputes as causes of conflict
- I-PC.3 - Islam: holy war, the conditions for Jihad in war: Explain Muslim teaching on holy war and the conditions for declaring it
- I-PC.4 - Islam: weapons of mass destruction: Explain divergent Muslim responses to WMD

## How to teach this
Open with: 'The word "Islam" is related to the Arabic word for peace. Yet the specification also asks us to study holy war. How can a religion that values peace also have teaching about fighting?' Hold this tension through the stage.

#### I-PC.1 Islam: Muslim teachings about peace
The word 'Islam' shares its root with 'salaam', peace, and 'as-Salaam' ('the source of peace') is one of Allah's names, showing peace's deep importance within Islamic teaching. The Qur'an teaches that peace between individuals and communities is the ideal state, encouraging reconciliation, kindness and justice as the normal basis of Muslim life, and Muslims greet one another with 'as-salaamu alaikum' ('peace be upon you'). Peace is significant for Muslims today both as a personal spiritual state, inner peace found through submission to Allah, and as a social and political goal, with many Muslims and Muslim organisations actively involved in peacebuilding, humanitarian work and interfaith dialogue, understanding conflict and violence as a departure from Islam's core values rather than its natural expression.

#### I-PC.2 Islam: Muslim responses to the causes of conflict
Muslim teaching identifies several recurring causes of conflict: greed, the pursuit of wealth or resources at others' expense; the abuse of power, when rulers or groups act unjustly or oppressively; injustice more broadly, including oppression and denial of basic rights; and territorial disputes over land or resources. Islamic teaching generally holds that such conflicts arise from departing from Allah's guidance on justice and fair dealing, and that addressing root causes (poverty, oppression, inequality) is itself a religious duty and a more effective long-term path to peace than force alone. Sunni and Shi'a Muslims broadly share this analysis, though specific historical and political conflicts, including some conflicts within the Muslim world itself, are understood and explained differently depending on each community's particular history and perspective.

#### I-PC.3 Islam: holy war, the conditions for Jihad in war
Traditional Islamic teaching permits fighting only under strict conditions, set out with reference to passages including Surah 2:190-194 ('fight in the way of Allah those who fight you, but do not transgress limits') and Surah 22:39 (permission to fight granted to those who have been wronged). Conditions for legitimate holy war traditionally include: it must be defensive, in response to aggression or persecution, rather than aimed at conquest or forced conversion; it must be declared by a legitimate authority, not undertaken by individuals acting alone; it must be a last resort after peaceful alternatives have failed; and it must be conducted proportionately, sparing non-combatants, and stopped as soon as the threat ends. Muslims diverge on how strictly and how widely these traditional conditions should be interpreted and applied to specific modern conflicts.

#### I-PC.4 Islam: weapons of mass destruction
Weapons of mass destruction (nuclear, chemical and biological weapons capable of killing very large numbers of people, including civilians, indiscriminately) raise acute problems for Islamic just-war principles, which require proportionality and the protection of non-combatants. Many Muslims argue such weapons cannot be used in keeping with Islamic teaching precisely because their indiscriminate, catastrophic effects make sparing innocent civilians and proportionate response impossible in principle, so their use, and often their development or stockpiling, is condemned as contrary to Islamic ethics of war. Other Muslims, and some Muslim-majority states, argue limited possession may be justified defensively, as a deterrent against being attacked by others who hold such weapons, while still generally opposing their actual use, reflecting a real and continuing divergence within Muslim ethical and political thought on this issue.
