# S11_Muslim_Beliefs_Part_1 - Lesson: Muslim beliefs: the Six Beliefs, the Five Roots, the nature of Allah, and prophethood

## Goal
The learner can explain the Six Beliefs of Sunni Islam and how belief differs between Sunni and Shi'a Islam, explain the Five Roots of Shi'a Islam, explain named characteristics of Allah, and explain the importance of prophethood and the Muslim holy books.

## Syllabus items taught here
- I-BEL.1 - Islam: the Six Beliefs and Sunni/Shi'a Islam: Explain the Six Beliefs of Sunni Islam and how belief is understood across Sunni and Shi'a Islam
- I-BEL.2 - Islam: the Five Roots of 'Usul ad-Din in Shi'a Islam: Explain the Five Roots of Shi'a Islam
- I-BEL.3 - Islam: the nature of Allah: Explain Tawhid, immanence, transcendence, omnipotence, beneficence, mercy, fairness and justice
- I-BEL.4 - Islam: Risalah, prophethood and the holy books: Explain the importance of prophethood and the Muslim holy books

## How to teach this
Open with: 'Islam has two main branches, Sunni and Shi'a. Both share core beliefs but also have their own distinct lists of them. What might cause a split like that in a religion's early history?' Use the answer to introduce the Sunni/Shi'a division before the content itself.

#### I-BEL.1 Islam: the Six Beliefs and Sunni/Shi'a Islam
Sunni Islam, the majority tradition worldwide, traditionally summarises core belief in six articles: belief in the oneness of Allah (Tawhid), the angels, the holy books, the prophets, the Day of Judgement, and predestination (al-Qadr). Shi'a Islam, which developed from an early disagreement over the rightful succession of leadership after the Prophet Muhammad's death (Shi'a Muslims holding that leadership, the Imamate, should have passed to Muhammad's descendants through Ali), shares much core belief with Sunni Islam but expresses its own distinct core beliefs through the Five Roots (covered next) rather than the Sunni Six Beliefs list. Both traditions affirm the absolute oneness of Allah and the authority of the Qur'an, but differ in religious authority structures and some later theological emphasis.

#### I-BEL.2 Islam: the Five Roots of 'Usul ad-Din in Shi'a Islam
Shi'a Islam expresses its foundational beliefs through the Five Roots ('Usul ad-Din): Tawhid, the oneness of Allah, affirming there is only one God with no partners; 'Adl, Divine Justice, the belief that Allah is perfectly just and does only what is right; Nubuwwah, Prophethood, belief in Allah's prophets sent to guide humanity, of whom Muhammad is the last; Imamah, belief in the divinely guided succession of Imams (leaders descended from the Prophet through Ali) as rightful spiritual and community leaders after Muhammad; and Mi'ad, belief in the Day of Judgement and bodily resurrection. The Five Roots overlap substantially with the Sunni Six Beliefs (Tawhid, prophethood, judgement) but add the distinctively Shi'a doctrines of 'Adl and Imamah.

#### I-BEL.3 Islam: the nature of Allah
The Qur'an describes Allah through several key characteristics. Tawhid, oneness, is the most fundamental: Allah is one, indivisible, with no equal or partner, and associating anything else with Allah's divinity (shirk) is considered the gravest sin in Islam. Allah is immanent (close to and involved in creation and human life) and transcendent (beyond and greater than creation, not limited by it) at the same time. Allah is omnipotent (all-powerful), beneficent (endlessly generous and good) and merciful (forgiving towards those who sincerely repent), reflected in the phrase 'Bismillah ir-Rahman ir-Rahim' ('in the name of Allah, the most gracious, the most merciful') that opens most chapters of the Qur'an. Allah is also fair and just, rewarding good and punishing wrong precisely and impartially, especially at the final judgement.

#### I-BEL.4 Islam: Risalah, prophethood and the holy books
Risalah refers to the belief in and importance of prophethood: Allah has sent a long line of prophets (including figures also recognised in Judaism and Christianity, such as Ibrahim/Abraham and Musa/Moses) to guide humanity, with Muhammad honoured as the final prophet ('Seal of the Prophets'), through whom the Qur'an, Islam's central holy book, was revealed. Muslims also recognise earlier holy books as originally revealed by Allah: the Tawrat (given to Musa), the Zabur (given to Dawud/David), the Injil (given to Isa/Jesus) and the Sahifah (scrolls given to Ibrahim), though Muslims believe only the Qur'an has been preserved perfectly, unaltered, in its original form, making it the primary and final source of guidance for Muslims today.
