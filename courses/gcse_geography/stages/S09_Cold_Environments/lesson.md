# S09_Cold_Environments - Lesson: Cold environments: characteristics, interdependence, adaptations and the Alaska case study

## Goal
The learner describes the physical characteristics of a cold environment, explains interdependence and adaptations, and uses Alaska to evaluate opportunities and challenges of development, and strategies to balance development and conservation.

## Syllabus items taught here
- 3.1.2.4a - Physical characteristics of a cold environment: polar and tundra climate, permafrost
- 3.1.2.4b - Interdependence of climate, permafrost, soils, plants, animals and people: explain links
- 3.1.2.4c - Adaptations of cold-environment plants and animals: named adaptations
- 3.1.2.4d - Cold-environment biodiversity issues: explain fragility
- 3.1.2.4e - Case study: Alaska, development opportunities: minerals, energy, fishing, tourism
- 3.1.2.4f - Case study: Alaska, challenges of development: temperature, inaccessibility, buildings and infrastructure
- 3.1.2.4g - Value of cold environments as wilderness and why to protect them: explain value and fragility
- 3.1.2.4h - Balancing economic development and conservation in cold environments: technology, governments, agreements, conservation groups

## How to teach this
Ask: 'Oil companies want to drill in Alaska because it is rich in resources, but building anything there is extremely difficult and expensive. Why?'

#### 3.1.2.4a Physical characteristics of a cold environment
Cold environments include polar regions (permanent ice, extremely low temperatures, very low precipitation as snow) and tundra (found at high latitudes and high altitudes, with a short summer growing season, very cold winters, and low, sparse vegetation). A defining feature of tundra is permafrost: ground that stays frozen year-round, with only a thin 'active layer' at the surface that thaws briefly each summer.

#### 3.1.2.4b Interdependence of climate, permafrost, soils, plants, animals and people
The extreme cold climate maintains the permafrost; permafrost in turn restricts drainage, creating waterlogged soils and boggy ground in summer, and limits plant roots to the shallow active layer, so only low-growing vegetation (mosses, lichens, small shrubs) can survive. This vegetation supports grazing animals such as caribou, which in turn support predators and, traditionally, indigenous peoples whose hunting and movement patterns follow the animals and seasons.

#### 3.1.2.4c Adaptations of cold-environment plants and animals
Plants: low, ground-hugging growth to avoid wind chill and to use the shallow soil layer; small, dark leaves to absorb maximum light and heat during the short growing season. Animals: thick fur or blubber for insulation (polar bear, Arctic fox); white or seasonally changing fur for camouflage; hibernation or migration to avoid the harshest winter conditions; large feet or paws that spread body weight over snow.

#### 3.1.2.4d Cold-environment biodiversity issues
Cold environments have relatively low biodiversity (few species) compared with warmer biomes, because the extreme climate limits what can survive there. However, the species present are often highly specialised for these conditions, and the ecosystem is fragile: because there are few species and simple food chains, the loss or disruption of even one species can seriously destabilise the whole system.

#### 3.1.2.4e Case study: Alaska, development opportunities
Alaska (a US state in the Arctic and sub-Arctic) offers development opportunities including: oil and gas (notably around Prudhoe Bay, transported via the Trans-Alaska Pipeline), mineral resources (gold, zinc and other metals), fishing (a major industry in Alaskan waters), and tourism (cruises, wildlife viewing and wilderness tourism), all of which provide jobs and income both locally and for wider markets.

#### 3.1.2.4f Case study: Alaska, challenges of development
Challenges include: extreme cold making construction and outdoor working difficult and expensive; permafrost, which can thaw and destabilise foundations if heat from buildings or pipelines is allowed to reach it (the Trans-Alaska Pipeline is raised above ground on supports with heat-dissipating devices for this reason); remoteness and poor transport links raising the cost of moving materials, workers and goods; and a short daylight winter season limiting the working period.

#### 3.1.2.4g Value of cold environments as wilderness and why to protect them
Cold environments are valued as some of the world's last large wilderness areas: they support unique, specially adapted species, act as an indicator of global climate change (since they warm faster than the global average and their ice loss is highly visible), and hold cultural and spiritual importance for indigenous peoples. Their fragility means damage (such as an oil spill) can take decades to recover, if it recovers at all.

#### 3.1.2.4h Balancing economic development and conservation in cold environments
Balancing strategies include: using technology to reduce environmental impact (elevated, insulated pipelines; ice roads that melt away rather than leaving permanent scars); government regulation restricting where and how development can take place; involvement of indigenous and local communities in decisions; and conservation areas and international agreements that protect the most sensitive habitats from development altogether.
