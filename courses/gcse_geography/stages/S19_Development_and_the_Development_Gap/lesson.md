# S19_Development_and_the_Development_Gap - Lesson: Classifying development, measuring it, the Demographic Transition Model and closing the development gap

## Goal
The learner classifies countries by development, evaluates measures of development, links the Demographic Transition Model to development, and explains causes, consequences and strategies to reduce the development gap.

## Syllabus items taught here
- 3.2.2a - Classifying parts of the world by development and quality of life: HIC, LIC, NEE, North-South
- 3.2.2b - Economic and social measures of development: GNI, birth and death rates, IMR, life expectancy, people per doctor, literacy, safe water, HDI
- 3.2.2c - Limitations of development measures: evaluate measures
- 3.2.2d - Demographic Transition Model and development: link stages to development
- 3.2.2e - Causes of uneven development: physical, economic, historical
- 3.2.2f - Consequences of uneven development: wealth and health disparities, migration
- 3.2.2g - Strategies to reduce the development gap: investment, industry, tourism, aid, intermediate technology, fairtrade, debt relief, microfinance

## How to teach this
Ask: 'If you wanted to compare how developed two countries are, would you just look at their wealth (income), or does development mean more than money?'

#### 3.2.2a Classifying parts of the world by development and quality of life
Countries are often grouped by their level of development and quality of life: higher-income countries (HICs), which are wealthy and highly developed; lower-income countries (LICs), which are poorer and less developed; and newly emerging economies (NEEs), countries experiencing rapid economic growth and industrialisation, moving from LIC towards HIC status (such as Nigeria, India and Brazil). This is related to, but not identical to, the historic idea of a 'North-South divide' between wealthier northern and poorer southern countries globally.

#### 3.2.2b Economic and social measures of development
Economic measures include Gross National Income (GNI) per head, showing average income. Social measures include birth rate and death rate, infant mortality rate (deaths of infants under one per 1,000 live births), life expectancy, the number of people per doctor, literacy rate, and the percentage of the population with access to safe water. The Human Development Index (HDI) is a composite measure combining income, education and life expectancy into a single score.

#### 3.2.2c Limitations of development measures
Single measures like GNI per head can be misleading, since they give only an average and hide inequality within a country (a small wealthy elite can raise the average while most people remain poor). Social measures can be affected by factors other than development (such as disease prevalence or cultural factors). Even composite measures like HDI cannot capture everything about quality of life, such as happiness, freedom or environmental quality, so geographers usually use several measures together rather than relying on just one.

#### 3.2.2d Demographic Transition Model and development
The Demographic Transition Model (DTM) shows how birth rates, death rates and total population typically change as a country develops, in five stages: Stage 1 (high birth and death rates, low, stable population, associated with very low-income countries), Stage 2 (death rates fall due to improved healthcare and food supply while birth rates stay high, so population grows rapidly, typical of many LICs), Stage 3 (birth rates start falling as development improves, e.g. more education and family planning, typical of many NEEs), Stage 4 (low birth and death rates, slow growth, typical of most HICs), and Stage 5 (birth rate falls below death rate in some HICs, causing population decline).

#### 3.2.2e Causes of uneven development
Uneven development between countries has physical causes (climate affecting agriculture, availability of natural resources, landlocked or remote locations restricting trade, susceptibility to natural hazards or disease), economic causes (unfair historic trade relationships, debt, limited access to investment and technology), and historical causes (the legacy of colonialism, including borders and economic structures designed to benefit colonial powers rather than the colonised country, and conflict that has disrupted development).

#### 3.2.2f Consequences of uneven development
Uneven development causes significant disparities in wealth and health between countries: differences in income, life expectancy, access to healthcare and education, and vulnerability to hazards and disease. It is also a major driver of international migration, as people move from less-developed to more-developed regions in search of better economic opportunities and living conditions, which can create both benefits and pressures for both sending and receiving countries.

#### 3.2.2g Strategies to reduce the development gap
Strategies include: investment (foreign companies or governments funding infrastructure and industry), industrial development (building manufacturing to create jobs and exports), growing the tourism sector, international aid (money, resources or expertise given to support development), intermediate (appropriate) technology (simple, low-cost, locally maintainable technology suited to local conditions), fairtrade schemes (guaranteeing producers a fairer price for goods such as coffee or cocoa), debt relief (cancelling or reducing debt so more money can be spent on development), and microfinance (small loans to individuals or small businesses who could not otherwise access credit).
