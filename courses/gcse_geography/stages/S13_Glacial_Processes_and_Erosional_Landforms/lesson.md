# S13_Glacial_Processes_and_Erosional_Landforms - Lesson: Glacial processes and the landforms of glacial erosion, transportation and deposition

## Goal
The learner explains freeze-thaw weathering and glacial erosion, movement and deposition, and describes and explains the formation of corries, arêtes, pyramidal peaks, truncated spurs, glacial troughs, ribbon lakes, hanging valleys, erratics, drumlins and moraines.

#### 3.1.3.4a Maximum extent of ice cover in the UK in the last ice age
During the last glacial period (ending around 11,700 years ago), ice sheets and glaciers covered most of Scotland, northern England (including the Lake District and the Pennines) and Wales, extending as far south as roughly the Bristol Channel and the Thames Valley at their maximum. This explains why the classic glacial landforms studied in the UK, such as those in the Lake District, are concentrated in the north and west.

#### 3.1.3.4b Freeze-thaw weathering
As in coastal environments, freeze-thaw weathering breaks up exposed rock in glacial areas: water enters cracks in rock (particularly around and above a glacier), freezes and expands, widening the crack, and repeated cycles eventually shatter the rock into angular fragments. In glacial landscapes this weathered rock often becomes debris embedded in and carried by the glacier, feeding its erosive power.

#### 3.1.3.4c Glacial erosion: abrasion and plucking
Abrasion is where rock fragments embedded in the base and sides of a glacier scrape and grind against the surrounding rock as the ice moves, smoothing and scouring the landscape, similar to sandpaper. Plucking occurs where meltwater beneath the glacier freezes onto surrounding rock; as the glacier continues to move, it pulls (plucks) chunks of that rock away with it, leaving a jagged, freshly broken surface, typically on the downhill (lee) side of a feature.

#### 3.1.3.4d Glacial movement and transportation: rotational slip and bulldozing
Glaciers move slowly downhill under gravity. Rotational slip describes the curved, rotating movement of ice within a corrie (a hollow on a mountainside), which helps deepen and shape its characteristic armchair form. Glaciers also transport huge quantities of eroded rock debris (moraine), either by bulldozing loose material ahead of the ice as it advances, or by carrying debris on, within, or beneath the ice itself.

#### 3.1.3.4e Glacial deposition: till and outwash
When a glacier melts, it deposits the material it has been carrying. Till (or boulder clay) is unsorted material (a mix of clay, sand and boulders of all sizes) deposited directly by the ice itself. Outwash is material deposited by glacial meltwater streams flowing away from the ice front; because it is transported by moving water, outwash material is sorted (larger, heavier material dropped first, closer to the ice) and typically layered.

#### 3.1.3.4f Corries, arêtes and pyramidal peaks
A corrie is a deep, rounded, armchair-shaped hollow on a mountainside, formed where snow accumulates and compresses into ice, which then erodes the hollow by abrasion, plucking and rotational slip; corries often contain a small lake (tarn) once the ice has melted. Where two corries form back-to-back on either side of a ridge, continued erosion narrows the ridge between them into a sharp-edged arête. Where three or more corries erode around a single peak, they can leave a sharply pointed pyramidal peak.

#### 3.1.3.4g Truncated spurs, glacial troughs, ribbon lakes and hanging valleys
As a glacier flows down a former river valley, it erodes straight through the interlocking spurs that a river would have wound around, cutting them off (truncating them) to leave steep triangular cliffs. The glacier widens and deepens the valley into a distinctive flat-floored, steep-sided U-shaped glacial trough. Deeper, more heavily eroded sections of the trough floor can fill with water after the ice melts, forming long, narrow ribbon lakes. Smaller tributary glaciers erode less deeply than the main glacier, so their valleys are left 'hanging' above the main trough, often marked by a waterfall.

#### 3.1.3.4h Erratics, drumlins and moraines
Erratics are individual boulders carried by a glacier and deposited far from their original source rock type, sometimes many kilometres away, allowing geologists to trace former ice movement. Drumlins are smooth, elongated, egg-shaped mounds of till deposited beneath moving ice, typically found in groups (a 'basket of eggs' landscape), with their long axis showing the direction of ice flow. Moraine is the general term for glacially deposited debris, forming as distinct ridges at the sides (lateral moraine), where two glaciers join (medial moraine), or at the glacier's furthest point of advance (terminal moraine).
