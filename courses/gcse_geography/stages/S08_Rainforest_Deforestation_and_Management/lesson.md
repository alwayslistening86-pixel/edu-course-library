# S08_Rainforest_Deforestation_and_Management - Lesson: Amazon deforestation: rates, causes, impacts, value and sustainable management

## Goal
The learner describes changing deforestation rates, explains the causes and impacts of Amazon deforestation, and evaluates strategies for sustainable rainforest management.

## Syllabus items taught here
- 3.1.2.2e - Changing rates of deforestation: describe and explain trends
- 3.1.2.2f - Case study: Amazon rainforest, causes of deforestation: causes with figures
- 3.1.2.2g - Case study: Amazon rainforest, impacts of deforestation: economic and environmental impacts
- 3.1.2.2h - Value of tropical rainforests to people and the environment: explain value
- 3.1.2.2i - Strategies to manage the rainforest sustainably: selective logging, conservation, ecotourism, agreements, debt reduction

## How to teach this
Ask: 'Who benefits, and who loses, when a patch of Amazon rainforest is cleared for cattle ranching?' Use this to surface the tension between economic development and environmental value.

#### 3.1.2.2e Changing rates of deforestation
Deforestation rates in tropical rainforests have varied considerably over time rather than following one steady trend. Rates rose sharply through the later twentieth century as roads, farming and settlement expanded; conservation policy and monitoring reduced the rate for a period in some countries; but rates have since risen again in places where farming and mining pressure and weaker enforcement of environmental protections have increased. Satellite monitoring now allows fairly accurate year-on-year tracking of forest loss.

#### 3.1.2.2f Case study: Amazon rainforest, causes of deforestation
The Amazon (spanning Brazil and neighbouring countries) is the world's largest tropical rainforest. Causes of its deforestation include: cattle ranching (the single largest driver, clearing land for grazing to supply beef and leather), commercial logging (for timber), road building (such as the Trans-Amazonian Highway, which opens up previously inaccessible forest to settlement), commercial farming (notably soy), mineral extraction (gold and other minerals), and dam building for hydro-electric power, which floods large forest areas.

#### 3.1.2.2g Case study: Amazon rainforest, impacts of deforestation
Economic impacts: deforestation creates jobs and income from farming, logging and mining, and contributes to national economic growth. Environmental impacts: loss of biodiversity and habitat, soil erosion once tree roots no longer bind the soil, disruption to the water cycle (less evapotranspiration can reduce regional rainfall), release of stored carbon dioxide (contributing to climate change), and loss of land and livelihoods for indigenous communities.

#### 3.1.2.2h Value of tropical rainforests to people and the environment
Rainforests are valuable because they: act as a major carbon store, helping to regulate the global climate; contain immense biodiversity, including many species not found anywhere else and many plants used in modern medicines; regulate regional and global water cycles; provide resources such as timber, rubber and food; and support the livelihoods and cultures of indigenous and local communities.

#### 3.1.2.2i Strategies to manage the rainforest sustainably
Strategies include: selective logging (cutting only mature trees of certain species, leaving the rest of the forest intact) and replanting; conservation areas and national parks that legally protect forest from clearance; international agreements and debt-for-nature swaps (where a country's debt is reduced in exchange for protecting forest); and ecotourism, which can generate income from the forest while keeping it standing, so that local communities have an economic incentive to conserve rather than clear it.
