# S29_Atlas_Maps_and_OS_Map_Basics - Lesson: Geographical skills: atlas maps, and OS map scale, grid references and measuring

## Goal
The learner uses latitude and longitude, reads atlas maps for distributions and associations, and uses OS map scale, four- and six-figure grid references, distance and direction.

## Syllabus items taught here
- 3.4.1a - Atlas maps: latitude and longitude coordinates: use coordinates
- 3.4.1b - Atlas maps: describing distributions and patterns of human and physical features: describe patterns
- 3.4.1c - Atlas maps: global and other scales, significant features: identify and describe features
- 3.4.1d - Atlas maps: interrelationship of physical and human factors, associations on thematic maps: analyse associations
- 3.4.1e - OS maps: use at 1:50 000 and 1:25 000 and other scales: read map scales and key
- 3.4.1f - OS maps: four- and six-figure grid references: give and read references
- 3.4.1g - OS maps: scale, distance and direction, straight and curved lines: measure

## How to teach this
Ask: 'If someone gave you only the words "north of the river", could you find the exact spot on a map? What extra information would pin it down exactly?' Use this to introduce coordinates and grid references as ways of giving an exact, unambiguous location.

#### 3.4.1a Atlas maps: latitude and longitude coordinates
Lines of latitude run east-west and measure distance north or south of the Equator (0 degrees), up to 90 degrees at the poles. Lines of longitude run north-south and measure distance east or west of the Prime Meridian (0 degrees) at Greenwich, up to 180 degrees. A location is given as latitude then longitude, for example a point at 51 degrees N, 0 degrees W is near London. Practise reading whole and part-degree values from an atlas grid and locating named places by their coordinates.

#### 3.4.1b Atlas maps: describing distributions and patterns of human and physical features
A distribution is how a feature is spread across an area. When describing one from an atlas map (for example, population density, or the distribution of volcanoes), state the general pattern (clustered, scattered, linear, concentrated in one region), name specific locations shown, and use directions (north, coastal, along a plate boundary) and comparative words (denser in, sparser towards) rather than vague statements like 'spread out'.

#### 3.4.1c Atlas maps: global and other scales, significant features
Atlases show maps at very different scales, from a whole-world map to a single country or city. At global scale, only very large or significant features are shown (major mountain ranges, capital cities, ocean currents); at a national scale, more detail appears (regional cities, rivers). Learners should recognise that scale controls what detail can be shown, and select the right scale of atlas map to answer a given question.

#### 3.4.1d Atlas maps: interrelationship of physical and human factors, associations on thematic maps
A thematic map shows a single theme (for example, rainfall, or GDP per capita) using colour or symbols across an area. Comparing two thematic maps of the same area can reveal an association: for example, areas of high rainfall often being associated with dense vegetation, or areas of low income being associated with high population growth rates. State the association, give the map evidence for it, and suggest a plausible geographical reason linking the physical and human factors.

#### 3.4.1e OS maps: use at 1:50 000 and 1:25 000 and other scales
Ordnance Survey maps commonly come at 1:50 000 (1 cm on the map represents 500 m on the ground; used for wider area overviews) and 1:25 000 (1 cm represents 250 m; shows more field boundaries and footpaths, used for detailed local study such as fieldwork). Learners should be able to state what a given scale means and choose the appropriate scale for a task, for example the more detailed 1:25 000 for planning a fieldwork route.

#### 3.4.1f OS maps: four- and six-figure grid references
A four-figure grid reference identifies a 1 km grid square: read the eastings (vertical lines, left to right) first, then the northings (horizontal lines, bottom to top), giving four digits, for example 3456. A six-figure grid reference pinpoints a location within that square by estimating tenths across and up: for example 345562. The rule to teach is 'along the corridor, up the stairs' — eastings before northings, always.

#### 3.4.1g OS maps: scale, distance and direction, straight and curved lines
Using the map's scale (or scale bar), measure straight-line distance with a ruler and convert using the scale; measure a curved route (such as a winding road or river) by laying a piece of string or the edge of paper along the curve and marking it off in short straight sections, then measuring the total against the scale. Direction between two points is given as a compass bearing or compass point (for example, 'the church is north-east of the bridge').
