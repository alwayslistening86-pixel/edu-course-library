# S07_Ecosystems_and_Rainforest_Characteristics - Lesson: Ecosystems, global biome distribution, and the physical characteristics of tropical rainforests

## Goal
The learner explains food chains, food webs and nutrient cycling in a small-scale UK ecosystem, describes the global distribution of biomes, and describes and explains the physical characteristics of a tropical rainforest.

## Syllabus items taught here
- 3.1.2.1a - Small-scale UK ecosystem, food chains, food webs and nutrient cycling: producers, consumers, decomposers, nutrient cycle
- 3.1.2.1b - Balance between ecosystem components and impact of change: explain the effect of changing one component
- 3.1.2.1c - Distribution and characteristics of large-scale global ecosystems: biomes overview
- 3.1.2.2a - Physical characteristics of a tropical rainforest: climate, layers, soils
- 3.1.2.2b - Interdependence of climate, water, soils, plants, animals and people in the rainforest: explain links
- 3.1.2.2c - Adaptations of rainforest plants and animals: named adaptations
- 3.1.2.2d - Rainforest biodiversity issues: explain biodiversity and threats

## How to teach this
Ask: 'If all the rabbits in a UK woodland suddenly disappeared, what would happen to the foxes, and to the grass?' Build the idea of an ecosystem as a connected system before naming its parts.

#### 3.1.2.1a Small-scale UK ecosystem, food chains, food webs and nutrient cycling
An ecosystem is a community of living things interacting with each other and their physical environment. In a small-scale UK example (such as woodland), producers (plants, using sunlight for photosynthesis) are eaten by primary consumers (herbivores, such as rabbits), which are eaten by secondary consumers (carnivores, such as foxes). A food chain shows one path of energy transfer; a food web links many food chains together. Decomposers (fungi, bacteria) break down dead matter, returning nutrients to the soil in the nutrient cycle, which plants then reuse.

#### 3.1.2.1b Balance between ecosystem components and impact of change
Ecosystems exist in a delicate balance. Removing or adding one component has knock-on effects throughout the system: removing a predator can cause its prey species to overpopulate and overgraze plants; introducing a new species can outcompete native ones. Because components are interdependent, a change to climate, soil, water or one species can disrupt the whole ecosystem, not just the part directly affected.

#### 3.1.2.1c Distribution and characteristics of large-scale global ecosystems
Large-scale ecosystems (biomes) are distributed in broad bands linked mainly to latitude and climate: tropical rainforests near the equator, hot deserts around 15-30 degrees latitude, temperate forests and grasslands in the mid-latitudes, and tundra and polar ecosystems near the poles. Each biome has a distinctive climate, vegetation and soil type suited to those conditions.

#### 3.1.2.2a Physical characteristics of a tropical rainforest
Tropical rainforests have a hot, wet climate year-round: high temperatures (around 26-28 degrees Celsius) with little seasonal variation, and high rainfall (over 2000 mm a year, often daily). Vegetation is structured in layers: the emergent layer (the tallest trees, poking above the canopy), the canopy (a dense, continuous layer of tree tops that blocks most light), the under canopy/shrub layer, and the dark, sparsely vegetated forest floor. Soils are nutrient-poor, since heavy rain leaches nutrients quickly and most nutrients are held in the vegetation itself.

#### 3.1.2.2b Interdependence of climate, water, soils, plants, animals and people in the rainforest
The rainforest's components are tightly interdependent: the warm, wet climate supports dense plant growth; plants' rapid decay recycles what few nutrients the soil has; the dense canopy affects local rainfall through evapotranspiration; and diverse plant life supports an enormous range of animal species. Indigenous people have traditionally lived sustainably within this system, using its resources without permanently damaging the balance.

#### 3.1.2.2c Adaptations of rainforest plants and animals
Plants: buttress roots for extra support in shallow, nutrient-poor soil; drip-tip leaves that shed the heavy rainfall quickly to avoid fungal growth; lianas (woody vines) that climb existing trees to reach light. Animals: monkeys with long limbs and prehensile tails for moving through the canopy; camouflage (such as tree frogs and insects that mimic leaves) to avoid predators in the dense vegetation.

#### 3.1.2.2d Rainforest biodiversity issues
Tropical rainforests, though covering a small proportion of the Earth's land surface, contain an extremely high proportion of the world's plant and animal species (high biodiversity). This makes them ecologically critical, but also means that deforestation there causes disproportionately large species loss and habitat destruction compared with clearing an equivalent area of a less biodiverse biome.
