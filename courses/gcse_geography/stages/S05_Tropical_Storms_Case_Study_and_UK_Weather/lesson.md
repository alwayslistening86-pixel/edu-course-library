# S05_Tropical_Storms_Case_Study_and_UK_Weather - Lesson: Typhoon Haiyan, monitoring tropical storms, and UK weather hazards including Storm Desmond

## How to teach this
Ask: 'The Philippines gets hit by tropical storms almost every year, yet Typhoon Haiyan in 2013 was unusually devastating. What made it different?'

#### 3.1.1.3i Named example: Typhoon Haiyan 2013
Typhoon Haiyan struck the Philippines in November 2013 as one of the most powerful tropical storms ever recorded at landfall, with sustained winds estimated above 300 km/h. It caused a storm surge several metres high that devastated the city of Tacloban. Official figures record over 6,300 deaths and more than four million people displaced, with damage estimated at several billion US dollars, making it one of the deadliest storms in the country's history.

#### 3.1.1.3j Monitoring, prediction, protection and planning for tropical storms
Satellites and aircraft reconnaissance track a storm's position, size and intensity, allowing forecasters to predict its likely path several days ahead. Protection measures include building storm shelters and raising buildings above expected surge levels. Planning includes evacuation routes and drills, and early-warning systems, although in low-income areas warnings may not always reach remote communities in time or people may be unable to evacuate.

#### 3.1.1.3k Types of weather hazard in the UK
The UK experiences several weather hazards, including strong winds and storms (often the remnants of Atlantic depressions), heavy rainfall and river/surface flooding, thunderstorms and lightning, heavy snow and ice, drought, and occasional heatwaves. These are generally far less intense than tropical storms, but can still cause significant damage and disruption.

#### 3.1.1.3l UK extreme weather event: Storm Desmond 2015, causes
Storm Desmond struck the UK, especially Cumbria and northern England, in early December 2015. It was caused by a deep area of low pressure that drew an exceptionally warm, moisture-laden 'atmospheric river' of air from the tropical Atlantic across the UK, combined with the storm stalling over Cumbria's mountains, which forced the moist air to rise, cool and release record-breaking rainfall.

#### 3.1.1.3m UK extreme weather event: Storm Desmond, social, economic and environmental impacts
A new UK 24-hour rainfall record was set at Honister Pass in Cumbria (over 340 mm). Social impacts: thousands of homes flooded, people evacuated, some without power for days after a substation flooded. Economic impacts: damage estimated at several hundred million pounds, disrupted businesses, transport and roads. Environmental impacts: riverbanks eroded, farmland flooded and polluted by floodwater.

#### 3.1.1.3n UK extreme weather event: Storm Desmond, management strategies
Responses included the Environment Agency issuing flood warnings, the emergency services carrying out rescues, and the army assisting with flood defences and clean-up. Longer term, the government invested further in flood defences (raised walls, improved drainage) in Cumbria and reviewed flood-risk planning, though debate continued about whether existing defences had been sufficient for a storm of this scale.

#### 3.1.1.3o Evidence that UK weather is becoming more extreme
Evidence used to suggest UK weather is becoming more extreme includes: an increasing frequency of named storms and record-breaking rainfall events in recent decades, more frequent heatwaves and record high temperatures, and scientific projections (linked to climate change) of wetter, stormier winters and hotter, drier summers. Some of this pattern reflects natural year-to-year variability, so scientists are cautious about attributing any single event solely to climate change.
