# S33_Numerical_and_Statistical_Skills - Lesson: Geographical skills: numerical calculation, fieldwork data reliability, and statistical skills

## Goal
The learner calculates using number, area, scale, proportion and ratio, evaluates fieldwork data reliability, calculates measures of central tendency and spread, percentage change, and describes bivariate relationships, drawing informed conclusions from numerical and statistical data.

## Syllabus items taught here
- 3.4.3a - Number, area, scales and units: calculate with quantitative relationships
- 3.4.3b - Fieldwork data sheets, accuracy, sample size, control groups and reliability: design and reason
- 3.4.3c - Proportion, ratio, magnitude and frequency: use correctly
- 3.4.3d - Drawing informed conclusions from numerical data: conclude
- 3.4.4a - Central tendency, spread and cumulative frequency: mean, median, mode, modal class, range, quartiles, IQR
- 3.4.4b - Percentage change and percentiles: calculate
- 3.4.4c - Bivariate data: scatter graphs, lines of best fit, interpolation and extrapolation: describe relationships
- 3.4.4d - Weaknesses in selective statistical presentation: spot bias

## How to teach this
Ask: 'A news report says "crime has risen sharply" and shows a bar chart where the y-axis starts at 90 instead of 0. Why might that make a small change look dramatic?' Use this to introduce the idea that numbers and statistics can mislead as well as inform.

#### 3.4.3a Number, area, scales and units
Geographical numerical skills include working confidently with standard form for very large or small numbers (for example, population figures), calculating area from a map (counting grid squares, or length times width for a regular shape), using map scale to convert map distance to real distance, and using consistent units throughout a calculation (converting km to m, or vice versa, before combining values).

#### 3.4.3b Fieldwork data sheets, accuracy, sample size, control groups and reliability
A good fieldwork data sheet records exactly what, where, when and how each measurement was taken, so results can be checked or repeated. Accuracy depends on using suitable, correctly calibrated equipment and consistent technique. A larger sample size generally increases reliability, since results are less affected by one unusual reading, though it takes more time to collect. A control (an unaffected comparison site or measurement) helps isolate whether a difference is really caused by the factor being investigated.

#### 3.4.3c Proportion, ratio, magnitude and frequency
A proportion expresses part of a total (for example, 30 percent of respondents), while a ratio compares two quantities directly (for example, 3:2 male to female). Magnitude refers to the size of an event or value (a magnitude 7 earthquake is far larger than magnitude 5, on a logarithmic scale). Frequency is how often an event occurs in a given time period (for example, a 1-in-100-year flood has a low frequency but the term does not guarantee even spacing between occurrences).

#### 3.4.3d Drawing informed conclusions from numerical data
A conclusion from numerical data should state the overall pattern the numbers show, cite specific figures as evidence, and, where relevant, explain any anomaly or limitation before making a final judgement. Avoid conclusions that go beyond what the numbers actually support, and avoid vague statements ('the numbers were different') in place of a specific, evidenced statement.

#### 3.4.4a Central tendency, spread and cumulative frequency
The mean is the sum of values divided by how many there are; the median is the middle value once data is ordered (or the average of the two middle values for an even count); the mode is the most frequently occurring value, and the modal class is the most frequent group in grouped data. The range is the highest value minus the lowest. Quartiles divide ordered data into four equal parts; the interquartile range (IQR) is the upper quartile minus the lower quartile, showing the spread of the middle 50 percent, less affected by extreme values than the range. Cumulative frequency adds up running totals, often plotted as a curve to find the median and quartiles graphically.

#### 3.4.4b Percentage change and percentiles
Percentage change is calculated as (new value minus original value) divided by original value, multiplied by 100; a positive result is an increase, a negative result a decrease. A percentile states the value below which a given percentage of the data falls (for example, the 90th percentile is the value below which 90 percent of results lie), useful for comparing an individual result against a wider distribution.

#### 3.4.4c Bivariate data: scatter graphs, lines of best fit, interpolation and extrapolation
Bivariate data has two variables measured for each case (for example, distance from a coastal defence and beach gradient). A scatter graph plots one variable against the other to reveal any relationship (positive: both increase together; negative: one increases as the other decreases; no correlation: no clear pattern), and a line of best fit is drawn through the data to show the overall trend. Interpolation reads an estimated value from within the range of plotted data (generally reliable); extrapolation extends the line beyond the plotted data to estimate outside that range (less reliable, since the trend may not continue).

#### 3.4.4d Weaknesses in selective statistical presentation
Statistics can mislead through selective presentation: a truncated axis (not starting at zero) exaggerates small differences; cherry-picking a short or unrepresentative time period can hide a longer-term trend; using an average alone can hide a wide spread or an outlier; and comparing different-sized samples or areas without accounting for that difference can distort a comparison. Learners should be able to spot and explain these weaknesses in a given example.
