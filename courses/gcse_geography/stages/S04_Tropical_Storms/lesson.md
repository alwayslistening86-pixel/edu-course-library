# S04_Tropical_Storms - Lesson: How tropical storms form, their structure, and their effects

## How to teach this
Ask: 'Tropical storms only ever form over warm tropical oceans, never over land and never far from the equator or right at it. Why might that be?' Build towards the conditions needed for formation.

#### 3.1.1.3a Global atmospheric circulation: pressure belts and surface winds
The three-cell model divides each hemisphere into Hadley, Ferrel and Polar cells. Warm air rises at the equator (low pressure), moves towards the poles, cools and sinks around 30 degrees latitude (high pressure), creating the trade winds that blow back towards the equator. This pattern of rising and sinking air creates the belts of high and low pressure, and the winds, that drive global weather.

#### 3.1.1.3b Global distribution of tropical storms
Tropical storms (called hurricanes in the Atlantic, typhoons in the Pacific, and cyclones in the Indian Ocean) form in a band roughly between 5 and 30 degrees north and south of the equator, over warm tropical oceans, mainly in later summer and autumn when sea temperatures peak. They never form exactly at the equator, because the Coriolis effect (needed to make the storm spin) is too weak there.

#### 3.1.1.3c Tropical storms and atmospheric circulation
Tropical storms form within the trade wind belt and are steered by the prevailing winds of the Hadley cell, generally moving from east to west before curving polewards. Their formation and track are therefore directly linked to the global pattern of atmospheric circulation described in the three-cell model.

#### 3.1.1.3d Causes and formation sequence of tropical storms
Formation needs: sea surface temperatures above 26-27 degrees Celsius to a depth of at least 50 metres (providing energy and moisture), low wind shear (so the storm structure is not torn apart), and enough distance from the equator for the Coriolis effect to create rotation. Warm, moist air rises rapidly, condenses and releases latent heat, which powers further rising air, drawing in more warm moist air below and intensifying the system into an organised, rotating storm.

#### 3.1.1.3e Structure and features of a tropical storm
A mature tropical storm has a calm, low-pressure 'eye' at its centre (often 30-60 km across), surrounded by the eye wall, a ring of the most intense wind and rainfall where air spirals rapidly upward. Beyond the eye wall, spiralling rain bands bring heavy rain and strong winds outward from the centre. Wind speeds and the danger of the storm surge are usually highest in the eye wall.

#### 3.1.1.3f Climate change and tropical storms
As sea surface temperatures rise with climate change, there is more energy available to fuel tropical storms. Most scientific evidence suggests this could make future tropical storms more intense (stronger winds, heavier rainfall) even if the total number of storms does not clearly increase, and could expand the range of latitudes and seasons in which they can form.

#### 3.1.1.3g Primary and secondary effects of tropical storms
Primary effects: buildings destroyed by high winds, deaths and injuries from collapsing structures and flying debris, and roads and power lines brought down. Secondary effects: flooding from the storm surge and heavy rain, contaminated water supplies leading to disease, and disruption to food supply, businesses and transport that can last for months.

#### 3.1.1.3h Immediate and long-term responses to tropical storms
Immediate responses: evacuation before landfall, emergency shelters, search and rescue, and distributing food, water and medical aid. Long-term responses: rebuilding homes and infrastructure to withstand future storms, restoring the economy, and improving storm-tracking, early-warning systems and evacuation planning for the next storm season.
