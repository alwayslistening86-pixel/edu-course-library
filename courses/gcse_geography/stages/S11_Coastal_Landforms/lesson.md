# S11_Coastal_Landforms - Lesson: Landforms of coastal erosion and deposition, and the Purbeck coastline

## Goal
The learner describes and explains the formation of headlands and bays, cliffs and wave-cut platforms, caves-arches-stacks, beaches, sand dunes and spits and bars, using the Purbeck coast as a located UK example.

## Syllabus items taught here
- 3.1.3.2h - Headlands and bays: formation
- 3.1.3.2i - Cliffs and wave-cut platforms: formation
- 3.1.3.2j - Caves, arches and stacks: formation

- 3.1.3.2l - Sand dunes: formation and succession

## How to teach this
Ask: 'A sea arch always eventually collapses. What is it turning into when it does?' Use the answer (a stack) to introduce the full erosional sequence.

#### 3.1.3.2h Headlands and bays
On a discordant coastline, bands of harder rock erode more slowly and project out to sea as headlands, while the surrounding softer rock erodes faster and is worn back into bays. Because headlands stick out, they are exposed to wave energy from more directions and are eroded on both sides, while bays, being more sheltered, often develop gentler beaches from deposited material.

#### 3.1.3.2i Cliffs and wave-cut platforms
A cliff forms where waves erode the base of coastal rock (mainly by hydraulic power and abrasion), undercutting it to form a wave-cut notch. As the notch deepens, the unsupported rock above eventually collapses, and the cliff retreats landward over time, leaving behind a gently sloping wave-cut platform of exposed rock at the former cliff base, visible at low tide.

#### 3.1.3.2j Caves, arches and stacks
On a headland, waves exploit lines of weakness (such as joints and cracks) through hydraulic power and abrasion, widening them into a cave. If the cave erodes all the way through the headland, it becomes an arch. Continued erosion and weathering widen the arch until its roof can no longer support itself and collapses, leaving an isolated pillar of rock called a stack, separated from the headland. Further erosion at the base of a stack can reduce it to a low stump.

#### 3.1.3.2k Beaches
Beaches are depositional landforms formed mainly by constructive waves depositing sand and/or shingle in sheltered areas, such as bays. A beach profile is typically gently sloping with a berm (a ridge marking the highest point normal waves reach). Shingle beaches tend to be steeper than sandy beaches because the larger particles allow water to drain through more quickly, reducing backwash erosion.

#### 3.1.3.2l Sand dunes
Sand dunes form where a wide, sandy beach and onshore wind allow dry sand to be blown inland, where it accumulates around an obstacle (such as driftwood or vegetation). Pioneer plants such as marram grass, with deep, extensive roots, stabilise the sand, allowing dunes to grow taller and further inland over time, creating a sequence from young, mobile embryo dunes near the sea to older, more stable and vegetated dunes further inland.

#### 3.1.3.2m Spits and bars
A spit forms where longshore drift carries sediment along the coast until the coastline changes direction sharply (for example at a river mouth), so deposition continues out into open water, building a long ridge of sand or shingle with one end still attached to the coast. Changing wind direction can curve or hook the far end. A bar forms when a spit grows all the way across a bay, cutting it off from the sea and often trapping a lagoon behind it.

#### 3.1.3.2n UK coastline example: Purbeck, Dorset
The Isle of Purbeck on Dorset's Jurassic Coast is a well-documented located example of coastal erosion and deposition landforms. Discordant geology at Durdle Door has produced a natural limestone arch; at nearby Lulworth Cove, the sea has broken through a resistant limestone band to erode a rounded bay in the softer clay and sand behind it; and Old Harry Rocks, near Swanage, are chalk stacks formed as the headland there has been eroded back over time.
