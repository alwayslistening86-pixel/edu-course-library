# S20_Nigeria_Case_Study - Lesson: Jamaica's tourism and Nigeria: an NEE case study of changing industry, trade, aid and environment

## Goal
The learner explains how tourism has reduced Jamaica's development gap, and locates and explains Nigeria's importance, industrial structure, TNCs, trading relationships, aid, environmental impacts and quality of life.

## How to teach this
Ask: 'Nigeria has huge oil wealth, yet many Nigerians live in poverty. How can a country be resource-rich and still have a large development gap?'

#### 3.2.2h Tourism reducing the development gap: Jamaica
Jamaica, a Caribbean NEE, has used tourism (all-inclusive resorts, cruise ship visits, and cultural tourism linked to music and heritage) as a major strategy to close its development gap: tourism contributes a substantial share of Jamaica's GDP and employment. However, much of the profit from foreign-owned resorts and cruise lines 'leaks' out of the local economy rather than benefiting Jamaicans directly, and many tourism jobs are seasonal and low-paid, which is why community-based and locally-owned tourism schemes have been promoted as a way to keep more benefit within Jamaica.

#### 3.2.2i Nigeria: location and importance
Nigeria is located in West Africa, bordering the Gulf of Guinea, with Abuja as its capital and Lagos as its largest city and commercial centre. It is Africa's most populous country, with a population of well over 200 million, and one of Africa's largest economies, giving it major regional importance in West Africa and growing international significance, particularly as a major oil producer.

#### 3.2.2j Nigeria: political, social, cultural and environmental context
Nigeria is a federal republic with over 250 ethnic groups and many languages, reflecting a highly diverse society; it has significant religious diversity too, broadly a mainly Muslim north and mainly Christian south. Politically, Nigeria has faced periods of instability and conflict (including insurgency in parts of the north-east). Environmentally, Nigeria ranges from tropical coastal wetlands and rainforest in the south to savanna and semi-arid conditions further north.

#### 3.2.2k Nigeria: changing industrial structure and manufacturing
Nigeria's economy has been shifting structure: a large agricultural (primary) sector still employs many people, but oil and gas extraction dominates export earnings and government revenue, and the manufacturing (secondary) and service (tertiary) sectors, including finance, telecommunications and a fast-growing 'Nollywood' film industry, have been growing, broadening the economy beyond reliance on oil alone.

#### 3.2.2l Nigeria: role of transnational corporations
Transnational corporations (TNCs), especially in oil extraction (such as Shell), play a major role in Nigeria's economy: advantages include investment, jobs, technology transfer and tax/export revenue for the government. Disadvantages include profits largely leaving the country rather than staying to benefit local communities, environmental damage from extraction (including oil spills), and limited direct benefit reaching communities in the oil-producing Niger Delta region itself.

#### 3.2.2m Nigeria: changing political and trading relationships
Nigeria's trading and political relationships include membership of OPEC (the Organization of the Petroleum Exporting Countries, coordinating oil production and pricing among member states), the Commonwealth (a legacy of British colonial history, offering trade and diplomatic links), and ECOWAS (the Economic Community of West African States, promoting regional trade and cooperation). Nigeria has also developed growing trade and investment links with China in recent decades.

#### 3.2.2n Nigeria: international aid
Nigeria has received international aid in various forms: bilateral aid (government to government, for example from the UK), multilateral aid (through organisations such as the World Bank), and NGO-led aid (targeting specific issues such as health, including disease prevention programmes). Aid has funded infrastructure, health and education projects, though its long-term effectiveness is debated, particularly where corruption or poor governance limits how far aid reaches those most in need.

#### 3.2.2o Nigeria: environmental impacts of economic development
Environmental impacts of Nigeria's development include oil spills in the Niger Delta, which damage farmland, fishing grounds and drinking water; gas flaring (burning off waste gas from oil extraction), which releases pollutants and contributes to climate change; land degradation and waste problems linked to rapid urban and industrial growth; and pressure on natural habitats as agriculture and settlement expand.

#### 3.2.2p Nigeria: effects of development on quality of life
Development has raised average incomes and, for some, access to jobs, education and healthcare, but the benefits have been very unevenly distributed: a wealthy elite and growing urban middle class exist alongside widespread poverty, especially in rural areas and parts of the oil-producing Niger Delta, where environmental damage from oil extraction has, in places, worsened rather than improved local livelihoods and quality of life.
