# S12_Coastal_Management - Lesson: Hard engineering, soft engineering and managed retreat, including the Cleveleys scheme

## Goal
The learner evaluates the costs and benefits of hard engineering, soft engineering and managed retreat, and explains the reasons for, and effects of, the Cleveleys coastal management scheme.

## Syllabus items taught here
- 3.1.3.2o - Hard engineering: costs and benefits: sea walls, rock armour, gabions, groynes
- 3.1.3.2p - Soft engineering: costs and benefits: beach nourishment and reprofiling, dune regeneration
- 3.1.3.2q - Managed retreat (coastal realignment): costs and benefits: explain and evaluate
- 3.1.3.2r - UK coastal management scheme: Cleveleys (with Medmerry contrast): reasons, strategy, effects and conflicts

## How to teach this
Ask: 'Cleveleys is a seaside town on the Fylde coast. Before we look at what was actually built there, what would you build to protect a seaside town from erosion and flooding, and why?'

#### 3.1.3.2o Hard engineering: costs and benefits
Hard engineering uses artificial structures to directly control natural coastal processes. Sea walls (concrete or stone barriers, often with a curved face to reflect wave energy) effectively protect the coast behind them but are expensive to build and maintain, and can look unattractive. Rock armour (large boulders placed at the base of a cliff or wall) absorbs wave energy cheaply but can be unstable and restrict beach access. Gabions (wire cages filled with rocks) are relatively cheap and effective at absorbing wave energy but have a short lifespan as the wire corrodes.

#### 3.1.3.2p Soft engineering: costs and benefits
Soft engineering works with natural processes rather than against them. Beach nourishment and reprofiling (adding new sand or shingle to a beach, and reshaping it) rebuilds a natural, attractive buffer against waves, but is relatively expensive because it must be repeated as material is naturally removed again. Dune regeneration (planting marram grass, restricting access) rebuilds sand dunes cheaply as a natural buffer, but can take years to establish and dunes remain vulnerable to storm damage while young.

#### 3.1.3.2q Managed retreat (coastal realignment): costs and benefits
Managed retreat deliberately allows the sea to flood or erode an area of lower-value land (often removing or breaching an existing defence), rather than defending it. Benefits: relatively low cost, can create valuable new habitats such as salt marsh, and reduces pressure and flood risk elsewhere along the coast. Costs: loss of existing farmland, property or habitats in the realigned area, and compensation or resettlement issues for affected owners.

#### 3.1.3.2r UK coastal management scheme: Cleveleys (with Medmerry contrast)
Cleveleys, on the Fylde coast in Lancashire, has used mainly hard and soft engineering to protect a densely populated seaside town: a rock armour revetment and reprofiled, raised sea wall (opened in stages from the 2000s) combined with beach recycling, chosen because the high property value and population density behind the defences made full-scale managed retreat impractical there. This can be contrasted with Medmerry in West Sussex, where a new flood bank was built further inland and the old shingle bank deliberately breached in 2013, allowing the sea to flood low-value farmland and create salt marsh, protecting the nearby town of Selsey at much lower cost than continuing to defend the original line.
