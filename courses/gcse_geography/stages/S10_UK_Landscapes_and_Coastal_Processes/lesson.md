# S10_UK_Landscapes_and_Coastal_Processes - Lesson: The UK's diverse landscapes and the processes that shape the coast

## Goal
The learner locates the UK's main upland, lowland and river landscapes, and explains coastal weathering, mass movement, erosion, transportation and deposition processes.

## How to teach this

#### 3.1.3.1a The UK's diverse landscapes: uplands, lowlands and river systems
The UK has a varied physical landscape. Uplands (higher, more rugged relief, often formed from older, harder rock) are concentrated in the north and west: the Scottish Highlands, the Lake District, the Pennines and much of Wales. Lowlands (flatter, lower relief, often younger, softer rock) dominate the south and east, such as East Anglia. The UK also has many river systems draining from the uplands towards the coasts, shaping valleys as they flow.

#### 3.1.3.2a Coastal wave types and characteristics
Waves are generated by wind blowing over the sea surface; their size depends on wind speed, duration and fetch (the distance of open water the wind blows across). Constructive waves are low and long, with a strong swash (the wave pushing water up the beach) and a weak backwash, so they deposit more material than they remove, building up beaches. Destructive waves are steeper and more frequent, with a weak swash but a strong backwash, so they erode more material than they deposit.

#### 3.1.3.2b Coastal weathering: mechanical and chemical
Weathering breaks down rock in place, without moving it. Mechanical (physical) weathering includes freeze-thaw: water enters cracks in rock, freezes and expands, widening the crack until the rock breaks apart, a process that repeats with each freeze-thaw cycle. Chemical weathering includes carbonation: rainwater, slightly acidic from dissolved carbon dioxide, reacts with and slowly dissolves certain rock types such as limestone and chalk.

#### 3.1.3.2c Coastal mass movement: sliding, slumping and rock falls
Mass movement is the movement of weathered material down a slope under gravity. Sliding is where a mass of material moves down a slope along a flat surface. Slumping is a rotational movement, common in softer clay cliffs saturated by heavy rain, where the material slips downward and outward along a curved surface. Rock falls occur on steeper cliffs, where fragments of rock break off (often following freeze-thaw weathering) and fall directly to the base of the cliff.

#### 3.1.3.2d Coastal erosion: hydraulic power, abrasion and attrition
Hydraulic power is the sheer force of waves striking the coast, compressing air in cracks and gradually forcing rock apart. Abrasion (or corrasion) is where waves pick up sand, shingle and rock fragments and hurl them against the cliff, wearing it away like sandpaper. Attrition is where rock particles and pebbles carried by the sea collide with each other, breaking into smaller, smoother and more rounded fragments over time.

#### 3.1.3.2e Coastal transportation and longshore drift
Material is moved along the coast mainly by longshore drift: waves approach the beach at an angle (determined by the prevailing wind), so the swash carries material up the beach at that same angle, but gravity pulls the backwash straight back down the beach, moving material gradually along the coast in a zig-zag path over time.

#### 3.1.3.2f Coastal deposition
Deposition happens where the energy of waves or currents falls (for example, in sheltered bays or where the coastline widens), so the water can no longer carry all the material it holds, and sediment is dropped. Constructive waves, with their strong swash and weak backwash, are the main driver of coastal deposition, gradually building up beaches and other depositional landforms.

#### 3.1.3.2g Geological structure and rock type and coastal forms
Coastal shape depends heavily on rock structure and type. A concordant coastline has rock layers running parallel to the coast, producing a fairly straight coastline with few bays. A discordant coastline has alternating bands of hard and soft rock running perpendicular to the coast; because softer rock erodes faster than hard rock, this creates an alternating pattern of headlands (hard rock) and bays (soft rock).
