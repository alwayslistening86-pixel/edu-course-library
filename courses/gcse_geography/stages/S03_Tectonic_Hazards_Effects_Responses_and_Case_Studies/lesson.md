# S03_Tectonic_Hazards_Effects_Responses_and_Case_Studies - Lesson: Effects of, and responses to, tectonic hazards, comparing Chile 2010 and Nepal 2015

## Goal
The learner distinguishes primary from secondary effects and immediate from long-term responses, and compares a higher-income and a lower-income tectonic hazard case study.

## Syllabus items taught here
- 3.1.1.2f - Primary and secondary effects of a tectonic hazard: distinguish primary from secondary effects
- 3.1.1.2g - Immediate and long-term responses to a tectonic hazard: distinguish immediate from long-term responses
- 3.1.1.2h - Case study: Chile earthquake 2010 (higher-income area): named example with figures
- 3.1.1.2i - Case study: Nepal earthquake 2015 (lower-income area): named example with figures
- 3.1.1.2j - How wealth changes effects and responses: compare two areas of contrasting wealth
- 3.1.1.2k - Why people live in areas at risk from tectonic hazards: give reasons people stay
- 3.1.1.2l - Monitoring and prediction of tectonic hazards: explain how monitoring and prediction reduce risk
- 3.1.1.2m - Protection and planning for tectonic hazards: explain protection and planning measures

## How to teach this
Ask: 'Chile (2010) and Nepal (2015) both had major earthquakes. Chile's was actually the stronger earthquake, yet Nepal had many more deaths. Why might that be?' Let the learner hypothesise before giving figures.

#### 3.1.1.2f Primary and secondary effects of a tectonic hazard
Primary effects happen immediately as a direct result of the ground shaking or eruption: collapsed buildings, deaths and injuries from falling debris, and damaged roads and bridges. Secondary effects happen afterwards as a knock-on consequence: fires from broken gas lines, disease from contaminated water, landslides triggered by shaking, and economic losses from disrupted trade.

#### 3.1.1.2g Immediate and long-term responses to a tectonic hazard
Immediate responses happen in the hours and days after the event: search and rescue, providing emergency food, water and shelter, and setting up field hospitals. Long-term responses happen over months and years: rebuilding homes and infrastructure to better standards, restoring the economy, and improving early-warning systems and building codes to reduce the impact of the next event.

#### 3.1.1.2h Case study: Chile earthquake 2010 (higher-income area)
On 27 February 2010, a magnitude 8.8 earthquake struck off the coast of Chile near Maule, one of the largest earthquakes ever recorded, and triggered a tsunami. Around 525 people died. Damage was estimated at around US$30 billion. Chile's strict, seismically-engineered building codes (in place since the 1960s), together with a rapid, well-organised government and emergency response, meant the death toll was low relative to the earthquake's huge magnitude.

#### 3.1.1.2i Case study: Nepal earthquake 2015 (lower-income area)
On 25 April 2015, a magnitude 7.8 earthquake struck near Gorkha, close to Kathmandu. Around 9,000 people died, over 22,000 were injured, and roughly 600,000 homes were destroyed or damaged. The earthquake also triggered deadly avalanches, including on Mount Everest. Nepal's poorer infrastructure, weaker building regulations, and mountainous, hard-to-reach terrain slowed the emergency response and increased casualties.

#### 3.1.1.2j How wealth changes effects and responses
Comparing the two: although Chile's earthquake released roughly as much or more energy, Nepal (a lower-income country) suffered far more deaths, more building collapse, and a slower recovery. Wealthier countries can afford earthquake-resistant construction, better-trained emergency services, stockpiled supplies and faster international coordination; poorer countries often rely more heavily on international aid and take longer to rebuild.

#### 3.1.1.2k Why people live in areas at risk from tectonic hazards
Despite the risk, people live in hazardous areas because: volcanic soils are extremely fertile for farming, geothermal energy and minerals provide jobs and income, tourism draws visitors to volcanic and scenic areas, land may be cheap, and many people have long-standing family, cultural or economic ties to the area and cannot easily leave.

#### 3.1.1.2l Monitoring and prediction of tectonic hazards
Volcanoes can often be monitored using seismometers (detecting small tremors as magma rises), tiltmeters and satellites (detecting ground bulging), and gas emission sensors, giving some warning before an eruption. Earthquakes, by contrast, cannot be reliably predicted in advance; scientists can only state a probability of an earthquake occurring in a given area over a given time period, based on historical patterns and fault studies.

#### 3.1.1.2m Protection and planning for tectonic hazards
Protection includes earthquake-resistant building design (base isolators, cross-bracing, flexible materials) and evacuation drills. Planning includes hazard-mapping to restrict building in the highest-risk zones, developing and rehearsing emergency response plans, and public education. Together, monitoring, prediction, protection and planning reduce (but cannot eliminate) the impact of tectonic hazards.
