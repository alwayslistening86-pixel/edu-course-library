# S23_Global_Water_Resource_Option - Lesson: Global water resource management: patterns, insecurity and strategies, including Lesotho and Kenya

## Goal
The learner explains global patterns of water surplus and deficit, rising demand, factors affecting availability, and the impacts of water insecurity, and evaluates strategies to increase supply and move towards sustainability, using the Lesotho Highlands and Kenyan sand dam examples.

## Syllabus items taught here
- 3.2.3.3a - Water: global patterns of surplus and deficit: describe and explain
- 3.2.3.3b - Water: reasons for increasing consumption: economic development and population
- 3.2.3.3c - Water: factors affecting availability: climate, geology, pollution, over-abstraction, infrastructure, poverty
- 3.2.3.3d - Water: impacts of water insecurity: disease, food, industry, conflict
- 3.2.3.3e - Water: strategies to increase supply: dams, reservoirs, transfers, desalination
- 3.2.3.3f - Water: large-scale transfer scheme, Lesotho Highlands Water Project: advantages and disadvantages
- 3.2.3.3g - Water: moving towards a sustainable water future: conservation, groundwater management, recycling, grey water
- 3.2.3.3h - Water: local scheme in an LIC or NEE, sand dams in Kenya: sustainable supply example

## How to teach this
Ask: 'One water scheme moves millions of litres of water between two entire countries using giant dams. Another uses sand in a dry riverbed to store water for one village. Which do you think is more sustainable, and why might both still be useful?'

#### 3.2.3.3a Water: global patterns of surplus and deficit
Global water availability is very unevenly distributed: some regions (such as parts of the tropics and temperate zones) have a water surplus, with rainfall exceeding demand, while other regions, especially parts of the Middle East, North Africa and Central Asia, face water deficit, where demand exceeds reliably available supply, due to a combination of low rainfall, high temperatures (increasing evaporation) and, increasingly, rising demand from growing populations.

#### 3.2.3.3b Water: reasons for increasing consumption
Global water consumption is rising due to: economic development (industry and higher living standards use much more water than subsistence-level lifestyles), and population growth (more people directly means more total demand for drinking, sanitation, food production and industry). Both factors are expected to keep increasing global water demand significantly.

#### 3.2.3.3c Water: factors affecting availability
Water availability is affected by: climate (rainfall amount and reliability, and temperature affecting evaporation), geology (permeable rock types allow water to be stored underground in aquifers, while impermeable rock encourages surface run-off), pollution (contaminated water is unsafe to use even where it is physically present), over-abstraction (taking more water from rivers or aquifers than is naturally replaced), and poverty (limiting a country's ability to build the infrastructure needed to treat, store and distribute water reliably).

#### 3.2.3.3d Water: impacts of water insecurity
Water insecurity has serious impacts: disease (from using contaminated water when safe alternatives are unavailable), reduced food production (agriculture is highly water-dependent, so water shortages threaten food security), damage to industry (many industries need large, reliable water supplies), and even conflict, since water insecurity can heighten tension, particularly between countries or regions sharing a river or aquifer.

#### 3.2.3.3e Water: strategies to increase supply
Strategies to increase water supply include: building dams and reservoirs to store water and even out seasonal variation in supply, water transfer schemes to move water from surplus to deficit areas, and desalination (removing salt from seawater to produce fresh water), which is effective but energy-intensive and expensive, making it most viable for wealthier, water-scarce countries.

#### 3.2.3.3f Water: large-scale transfer scheme, Lesotho Highlands Water Project
The Lesotho Highlands Water Project transfers water from dams (including the Katse and Mohale dams) in the mountainous, water-surplus country of Lesotho, through tunnels, to the water-deficit Gauteng region of neighbouring South Africa, including Johannesburg. Advantages: it generates income and hydro-electric power for Lesotho, and secures water supply for South Africa's economically vital Gauteng region. Disadvantages: it displaced some local communities and farmland, caused significant environmental change in Lesotho, and required an extremely high construction cost, leaving Lesotho with considerable debt.

#### 3.2.3.3g Water: moving towards a sustainable water future
A more sustainable water future relies on strategies beyond simply increasing supply: water conservation (reducing waste and using water more efficiently in homes, farming and industry), better groundwater management (avoiding over-abstraction that permanently depletes aquifers), and water recycling and 'grey water' reuse (reusing gently-used water, for example from sinks or showers, for purposes such as toilet flushing or irrigation that do not require fully treated drinking water).

#### 3.2.3.3h Water: local scheme in an LIC or NEE, sand dams in Kenya
In parts of semi-arid Kenya, communities (often supported by NGOs) build sand dams: low walls built in stages across seasonal riverbeds, which trap sand carried by intermittent floodwater. Water is stored within the pore spaces of this trapped sand, below the surface, where evaporation losses are much lower than from an open reservoir. This low-cost, low-technology, community-built and community-managed scheme greatly improves local access to water, reducing the distance (often walked by women and children) to fetch water, and is considered a strongly sustainable, appropriate approach for a low-income rural setting.
