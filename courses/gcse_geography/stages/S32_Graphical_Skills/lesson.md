# S32_Graphical_Skills - Lesson: Geographical skills: selecting, constructing, completing and interpreting graphs, charts and thematic maps

## Goal
The learner selects and constructs appropriate graphs and charts, completes choropleth, isoline, dot, desire line, proportional symbol and flow line maps, plots data accurately, and interprets a range of graphical and cartographic data representations.

## Syllabus items taught here
- 3.4.2a - Graphs and charts: selecting and constructing: line, bar, pie, pictogram, histogram, divided bar, scatter, population pyramid
- 3.4.2b - Suggesting an appropriate form of graphical representation: choose the right chart
- 3.4.2c - Completing choropleth, isoline, dot, desire line, proportional symbol and flow line maps: complete
- 3.4.2d - Gradient, contour and value on isoline maps: interpret
- 3.4.2e - Plotting information on graphs when axes and scales are provided: plot accurately
- 3.4.2f - Interpreting population pyramids, choropleth maps, flow-line maps and dispersion graphs: extract information

## How to teach this
Ask: 'You have a set of rainfall figures for each month of the year. Would a pie chart or a line graph show the pattern better — and why?'

#### 3.4.2a Graphs and charts: selecting and constructing
The main graph and chart types are: line graphs (show change over time or along a transect, for example a beach profile), bar charts (compare separate categories), pie charts (show proportions of a whole), pictograms (use repeated symbols to represent quantity), histograms (show frequency of continuous, grouped data with no gaps between bars), divided bar charts (show proportions within multiple categories, stacked in one bar per category), scatter graphs (show the relationship between two variables), and population pyramids (show age-sex structure of a population). Each must be constructed with a title, correctly labelled and scaled axes (or key), and accurately plotted values.

#### 3.4.2b Suggesting an appropriate form of graphical representation
Choosing the right graph depends on the data type: use a line graph for continuous change over time or distance; a bar chart for comparing discrete categories; a pie chart for proportions of a single whole that sum to 100 percent; a scatter graph to test whether two variables are related; and a population pyramid specifically for age-sex population structure. A common exam skill is justifying the choice, not just naming it, for example 'a scatter graph is appropriate because we want to test the relationship between two continuous variables.'

#### 3.4.2c Completing choropleth, isoline, dot, desire line, proportional symbol and flow line maps
A choropleth map shades areas by a banded value using a graduated colour scheme (darker usually meaning higher). An isoline map joins points of equal value with a smooth line (for example, rainfall or pressure). A dot map uses one dot per fixed quantity to show density. A desire line map draws straight lines between origin and destination to show movement, with line thickness sometimes showing volume. A proportional symbol map sizes a symbol (circle, square) according to a value. A flow line map is similar to desire lines but often follows actual routes, with width showing volume of flow.

#### 3.4.2d Gradient, contour and value on isoline maps
On an isoline map, closely spaced isolines indicate a steep gradient (rapid change in value over a short distance), just as with contours on an OS map, while widely spaced isolines show a gentle gradient. The value at any point between two isolines can be estimated by interpolation, assuming a roughly even rate of change between the two known isoline values.

#### 3.4.2e Plotting information on graphs when axes and scales are provided
When axes and scales are already provided, plotting is a precise skill: read the value carefully against the given scale, mark the point accurately (not just approximately), and for a line graph join points with straight lines unless told otherwise; for a bar chart, draw bars to the exact height with equal width and even spacing. Small plotting errors (misreading a scale interval) are a common and avoidable cause of lost marks.

#### 3.4.2f Interpreting population pyramids, choropleth maps, flow-line maps and dispersion graphs
To interpret a population pyramid, compare the width of bars at different age groups between males (left) and females (right): a wide base indicates a high birth rate (often a lower-income country's pyramid), a narrower base and bulging middle indicates an ageing population (often a higher-income country's pyramid). To interpret a choropleth map, identify the darkest and lightest shaded areas and describe the spatial pattern (for example, concentrated in the centre, or increasing towards the coast) using the key's value bands. A dispersion graph shows the spread of individual data values.
