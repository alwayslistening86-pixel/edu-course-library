# S34_Data_Sources_Enquiry_Writing_and_SPaG - Lesson: Geographical skills: qualitative and quantitative data sources, formulating and writing up enquiry, literacy and SPaG

## Goal
The learner uses qualitative and quantitative data from primary and secondary sources including ICT and GIS, formulates enquiry questions, writes descriptively/analytically/critically, communicates for different audiences, and applies accurate spelling, punctuation, grammar and terminology.

- 3.4.5b - Data types: maps, fieldwork data, GIS, satellite imagery, digital sources: use ICT and GIS sources

## How to teach this
Ask: 'If you wanted to find out how people feel about a new regeneration scheme, would you use a questionnaire with tick-box answers, or open-ended interviews? What kind of data would each give you?'

#### 3.4.5a Qualitative and quantitative data from primary and secondary sources
Quantitative data is numerical (counts, measurements, survey scores) and can be statistically analysed and easily compared or graphed. Qualitative data is non-numerical (opinions, descriptions, photographs, interview responses) and captures depth and detail that numbers cannot. Primary data is collected first-hand by the learner; secondary data comes from existing published sources. Learners should obtain, illustrate (with an example), interpret (state what it shows) and evaluate (judge its usefulness and limitations) data of each type.

#### 3.4.5b Data types: maps, fieldwork data, GIS, satellite imagery, digital sources
Beyond data collected directly, geographers use a wide range of secondary sources: maps of various scales and types, published fieldwork data sets, Geographic Information Systems (GIS — digital systems that store, display and analyse layers of spatial data, such as overlaying flood risk on population density), satellite imagery (showing land use, vegetation or weather patterns from space), and other digital sources (government statistics websites, online census data). Each source should be selected because it suits the specific enquiry question being asked.

#### 3.4.6a Formulating enquiry: questions and sequences of enquiry
A good enquiry question is specific, geographically focused, and answerable with evidence that can realistically be collected (for example, 'How does beach gradient change with distance from a groyne at Cleveleys?' rather than a vague 'What is the beach like?'). A full enquiry follows a logical sequence: forming the question or hypothesis, planning data collection, collecting and presenting data, analysing results, and reaching an evidenced conclusion.

#### 3.4.6b Writing descriptively, analytically and critically
Descriptive writing states what is shown, without explaining why ('the graph shows an increase from 2010 to 2020'). Analytical writing breaks a pattern down and explains causes and links ('the increase is linked to X because...'). Critical writing goes further, weighing evidence, considering limitations or alternative explanations, and reaching a reasoned judgement ('however, this trend may be affected by..., so the conclusion should be treated with some caution'). Strong GCSE answers usually need to move beyond description into analysis and, for higher marks, critical evaluation.

#### 3.4.6c Communicating ideas effectively
Effective geographical communication states ideas clearly and in a logical order, uses specific place names, data and case-study detail rather than generalisations, and uses appropriate geographical terminology precisely (for example, 'longshore drift' rather than 'sand moving along the beach'). Structuring an answer with a clear line of argument, one point per paragraph, makes ideas easier for an examiner to follow and credit.

#### 3.4.6d Developing an extended written argument
An extended argument (needed for higher-mark exam questions and the issue evaluation decision) should have an introduction that sets out the question, a series of points each backed by specific evidence, consideration of an opposing or alternative view where relevant, and a conclusion that directly answers the question by weighing the points made, rather than simply repeating the introduction.

#### 3.4.6e Drawing well-evidenced conclusions about geographical questions and issues
A well-evidenced conclusion states a clear final judgement, explicitly refers back to specific evidence used earlier in the answer (data, case-study facts, map evidence), and, where the question demands a decision (as in issue evaluation), explains why that option was chosen over the alternatives rather than simply restating the options.

#### 3.4.7a Literacy: communicating for different audiences
The same geographical information may need to be communicated differently depending on the audience: a technical report for a council uses precise data and formal terminology, while a leaflet for local residents about a regeneration scheme uses simpler language and a more persuasive or informative tone. Learners should be able to adapt their writing style and level of technical detail to a stated audience and purpose.

#### 4.4-SPaG Spelling, punctuation, grammar and specialist terminology (SPaG)
AQA assesses spelling, punctuation and grammar, plus the accurate use of specialist geographical terminology, on designated extended-response questions: worth 3 marks each on Papers 1 and 2, and 6 marks on Paper 3 (reflecting its greater weighting of extended writing in the issue evaluation). Learners should proofread extended answers, use full sentences and correct punctuation, spell key geographical terms correctly (for example, 'deposition', 'urbanisation'), and use them accurately in context, not just include them.
