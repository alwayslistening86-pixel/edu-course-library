## How to teach this
Ask: 'The Lake District is both a working landscape (farms, quarries) and England's most-visited National Park. What kinds of conflict might that create?'

#### 3.1.3.4i UK glaciated upland example: the Lake District landforms
The Lake District in Cumbria, England's largest National Park, is a well-documented located example of glacial erosion and deposition. It contains corries such as Red Tarn on Helvellyn, sharp arêtes such as Striding Edge, glacial troughs now containing ribbon lakes such as Windermere (England's largest natural lake) and Ullswater, and evidence of glacial deposition including drumlins and moraine across the lower valleys.

#### 3.1.3.4j Economic activities in glaciated uplands
Glaciated uplands like the Lake District support several economic activities: farming (mainly sheep grazing on the steep, thin-soiled fell slopes, unsuitable for arable crops), forestry (conifer plantations on some hillsides), quarrying (extracting slate and other stone, historically important and still active in places), and tourism, which has become the dominant activity, supporting hotels, outdoor activity businesses and visitor attractions.

#### 3.1.3.4k Land-use conflicts and development versus conservation
Different land uses can conflict: farmers may resent walkers crossing land or dogs disturbing livestock; quarrying and forestry can damage the scenic, conservation value that draws tourists in the first place; new housing or tourist infrastructure can be opposed by conservationists wanting to protect the landscape; and heavy tourist traffic can congest narrow roads used by farm vehicles and local residents. Balancing continued economic use of the land with protecting the National Park's landscape and habitats is an ongoing challenge.

#### 3.1.3.4l Lake District tourism: attractions
Tourists are drawn to the Lake District by its dramatic glacial scenery (lakes, mountains, valleys), outdoor activities (walking, climbing, watersports on the lakes, cycling), its literary and cultural heritage (associated with writers such as William Wordsworth and Beatrix Potter), and picturesque market towns such as Ambleside, Keswick and Windermere town, which provide accommodation, food and shops for visitors.

#### 3.1.3.4m Lake District tourism: social, economic and environmental impacts
Social impacts: local house prices can rise (from second homes and holiday lets), and popular ('honeypot') sites become overcrowded, especially in peak season. Economic impacts: tourism creates significant employment and income for the area, though much of it is seasonal or low-paid. Environmental impacts: footpath erosion from heavy foot traffic, litter, traffic congestion and pollution on narrow rural roads, and pressure on water and waste infrastructure at peak times.

#### 3.1.3.4n Lake District tourism: management strategies
Management strategies include: park-and-ride schemes and traffic management to reduce congestion on narrow roads; footpath repair and reinforcement schemes (such as those run by the charity Fix the Fells) to tackle erosion; visitor education and signage to spread visitors away from the most overcrowded honeypot sites; and National Park Authority planning controls balancing new development against landscape and habitat protection.
