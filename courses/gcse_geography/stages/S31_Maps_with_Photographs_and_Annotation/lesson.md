# S31_Maps_with_Photographs_and_Annotation - Lesson: Geographical skills: inferring human activity from OS maps, using and comparing maps with photographs, and annotating

## Goal
The learner infers human activity including tourism from OS maps, compares maps with photographs, draws and interprets sketch maps, uses ground/aerial/satellite photographs, and labels and annotates geographical images.

## Syllabus items taught here
- 3.4.1o - OS maps: inferring human activity including tourism: infer from symbols
- 3.4.1p - Maps with photographs: comparing maps: compare
- 3.4.1q - Maps with photographs: sketch maps: draw, label and interpret
- 3.4.1r - Maps with photographs: ground, aerial and satellite photographs: use and interpret
- 3.4.1s - Maps with photographs: describing landscapes and phenomena from photographs: describe
- 3.4.1t - Maps with photographs: drawing sketches from photographs: sketch
- 3.4.1u - Labelling and annotating diagrams, maps, graphs, sketches and photographs: annotate

## How to teach this
Ask: 'A map uses symbols; a photograph shows the real view. What can a photograph tell you that a map cannot, and what can a map tell you that a photograph cannot?'

#### 3.4.1o OS maps: inferring human activity including tourism
Map symbols and features reveal human activity: a cluster of campsite and picnic-site symbols near a coast or lake, footpaths, car parks and a visitor centre together suggest tourism; a dense grid of terraced streets suggests older urban housing; large regular fields with few footpaths suggest arable farming. Tourism specifically can be inferred from symbols for viewpoints, museums, information centres, camp/caravan sites and picturesque named features (a waterfall, a named beach).

#### 3.4.1p Maps with photographs: comparing maps
Comparing two maps of the same place (for example, an older and a more recent OS map, or maps at two different scales) reveals change or added detail: new housing estates, a new road, or a changed coastline. State exactly what has changed, using grid references, and suggest a reason (urban growth, coastal erosion, new infrastructure).

#### 3.4.1q Maps with photographs: sketch maps
A sketch map is a simplified, hand-drawn map showing only the features relevant to a task, drawn roughly to scale with a title, key, and north arrow. To draw one from a photograph or a real map, identify the main features (a road, a river, a settlement edge), draw their approximate shape and position, and label or key them clearly rather than including unnecessary fine detail.

#### 3.4.1r Maps with photographs: ground, aerial and satellite photographs
A ground photograph is taken at eye level and shows detail and vertical features clearly (building height, vegetation type) but a limited area. An aerial photograph is taken from above (often obliquely, at an angle) and shows a wider area and spatial layout more clearly. A satellite image is taken from much further away, usually vertically downward, and is best for showing very large-scale patterns (a whole river system, a city's shape) but with less fine detail.

#### 3.4.1s Maps with photographs: describing landscapes and phenomena from photographs
When describing a landscape or phenomenon from a photograph, work systematically from foreground to background (or near to far), naming specific features (a meandering river, terraced housing, a scree slope) and using geographical vocabulary rather than everyday description, and note scale, season or weather clues where relevant to the question.

#### 3.4.1t Maps with photographs: drawing sketches from photographs
A field sketch is a simplified line drawing of a photograph or a real view, showing only the geographically significant features (not every building or tree), with clear, neat labels and annotations pointing to specific features. Practise by first drawing the skyline and main lines of the landscape lightly, then adding and labelling the key features, keeping proportions roughly accurate.

#### 3.4.1u Labelling and annotating diagrams, maps, graphs, sketches and photographs
A label simply names a feature (for example, an arrow pointing to 'beach' or 'meander'). An annotation goes further, adding a short explanatory note (for example, 'beach — deposition of sediment where wave energy is reduced'). Exam answers that ask learners to annotate a diagram, map, graph, sketch or photograph expect this explanatory detail, not just a bare label, and marks are usually awarded for the quality and accuracy of the explanation given.
