# S21_UK_Economic_Change - Lesson: UK economic change: deindustrialisation, the post-industrial economy, rural change, infrastructure and regional divides

## Goal
The learner explains the causes of UK economic change, the shift to a post-industrial economy, examples of sustainable industry and rural change, UK infrastructure improvements, and strategies addressing the north-south divide and the UK's global links.

## Syllabus items taught here
- 3.2.2q - UK economic change: causes: deindustrialisation, globalisation, policy
- 3.2.2r - UK economy: moving towards a post-industrial economy: IT, services, finance, research, science and business parks
- 3.2.2s - UK industry and the environment: a more sustainable industrial example: impacts and example
- 3.2.2t - UK rural change: one growth area and one decline area: social and economic change
- 3.2.2u - UK road, rail, port and airport infrastructure: improvements and developments
- 3.2.2v - The north-south divide and regional strategies: evaluate strategies
- 3.2.2w - The UK in the wider world: trade, culture, transport, communication, EU and Commonwealth: links

## How to teach this
Ask: 'The UK used to be known as the "workshop of the world" for manufacturing. Today, far fewer people work in factories. Where do you think those jobs went, and what replaced them?'

#### 3.2.2q UK economic change: causes
The UK's economy has changed dramatically since the mid-twentieth century due to: deindustrialisation (the decline of traditional heavy industries such as coal mining, steel and shipbuilding, partly because these could be produced more cheaply abroad), globalisation (increased international trade and investment, moving some manufacturing to lower-cost countries), and government policy (including privatisation of former state industries and decisions to close uncompetitive industries), which together sharply reduced UK manufacturing employment.

#### 3.2.2r UK economy: moving towards a post-industrial economy
As manufacturing declined, the UK economy shifted towards a post-industrial, service-based structure: growth in information technology, financial and business services (notably centred on London), scientific research, and dedicated science and business parks (such as Cambridge Science Park) that cluster high-value, knowledge-based industries together to share expertise and infrastructure.

#### 3.2.2s UK industry and the environment: a more sustainable industrial example
Modern UK industrial developments increasingly aim to reduce environmental impact: examples include car manufacturing plants (such as Nissan's Sunderland factory) that have invested in energy efficiency, waste reduction and on-site renewable energy generation, showing how manufacturing can continue in the UK while reducing its environmental footprint compared with older, more polluting industrial methods.

#### 3.2.2t UK rural change: one growth area and one decline area
Rural areas have changed unevenly: some, such as parts of the South East and East Anglia within commuting distance of major cities, have grown as commuters and remote workers move in, raising house prices and changing the local economy and population profile. Others, such as parts of the far south-west (Cornwall) or remote Scottish and Welsh areas, have experienced decline, with limited local job opportunities (beyond seasonal tourism), an ageing population as younger people move away, and reduced local services as population falls.

#### 3.2.2u UK road, rail, port and airport infrastructure
Infrastructure improvements aim to support economic growth and connectivity: major road schemes and motorway upgrades reduce journey times; rail investment (including high-speed rail projects and electrification) improves connections between cities and regions; ports such as Felixstowe and Southampton handle the large majority of the UK's container trade; and airport capacity (including debate over expanding major hub airports such as Heathrow) affects the UK's international connectivity for trade and travel.

#### 3.2.2v The north-south divide and regional strategies
The 'north-south divide' describes a persistent economic gap between a generally more prosperous South (especially London and the South East) and a generally less prosperous North, Midlands and other regions, in terms of income, house prices, job opportunities and investment. Strategies to address this include the 'Northern Powerhouse' initiative (aiming to boost transport links and investment between northern cities) and devolution of some powers and funding to city-regions and metro mayors, intended to let regions make more of their own economic decisions.

#### 3.2.2w The UK in the wider world: trade, culture, transport, communication, EU and Commonwealth
The UK remains closely linked to the wider world through trade (importing and exporting goods and services globally), cultural exchange (media, tourism, migration), transport links (major ports and airports connecting the UK internationally), digital communication, its historical relationship with the European Union (a major trading partner, though the UK left the EU in 2020), and the Commonwealth (a voluntary association of mostly former British territories, including Nigeria, that maintains trade, diplomatic and cultural links).
