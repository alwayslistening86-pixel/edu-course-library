# S26_Fieldwork_Data_Types_and_Sampling - Lesson: Enquiry strands 2-3: selecting and collecting data, sampling methods, and presenting results

## Goal
The learner distinguishes primary and secondary and physical and human data, explains and justifies sampling methods, and selects and justifies methods to present fieldwork findings.

## Syllabus items taught here
- 3.3.2.2a - Enquiry strand 2: primary and secondary data: define and distinguish
- 3.3.2.2b - Enquiry strand 2: identifying and selecting appropriate physical and human data: choose data
- 3.3.2.2c - Enquiry strand 2: measuring and recording data with sampling methods: random, systematic, stratified sampling
- 3.3.2.2d - Enquiry strand 2: describing and justifying data collection methods: justify choices
- 3.3.2.3a - Enquiry strand 3: range of visual, graphical and cartographic presentation methods: know the options
- 3.3.2.3b - Enquiry strand 3: selecting and accurately using presentation methods: construct accurately
- 3.3.2.3c - Enquiry strand 3: describing, explaining and adapting presentation methods: justify and improve

## How to teach this
Ask: 'You cannot measure every single point on Cleveleys beach or survey every single street in Salford Quays. How do you choose which few points to measure so your results are still fair?'

#### 3.3.2.2a Enquiry strand 2: primary and secondary data
As introduced earlier, primary data is collected first-hand by the learner (beach profile measurements, an environmental quality survey), while secondary data comes from existing sources (historical maps, council or Environment Agency reports). A strong enquiry usually combines both: primary data provides direct, up-to-date evidence, while secondary data provides context and a way to check findings against a wider or longer-term picture.

#### 3.3.2.2b Enquiry strand 2: identifying and selecting appropriate physical and human data
Physical data relates to the natural environment (for example, beach gradient, sediment size, wave height at Cleveleys). Human data relates to people and their activities (for example, land use, traffic counts, or an environmental quality survey at Salford Quays). Selecting appropriate data means choosing measurements that will actually help answer the enquiry question, rather than collecting data simply because it is easy to gather.

#### 3.3.2.2c Enquiry strand 2: measuring and recording data with sampling methods
Since it is rarely possible to measure everywhere, sampling selects representative points to collect data from. Random sampling selects points using no fixed pattern (for example, using random number generation), avoiding bias but possibly missing important areas. Systematic sampling selects points at regular, fixed intervals (for example, every 10 metres along a beach), giving even coverage. Stratified sampling divides the area into different categories or zones first (for example, different land-use zones in Salford Quays) and then samples proportionally within each, ensuring all key sub-areas are represented.

#### 3.3.2.2d Enquiry strand 2: describing and justifying data collection methods
A strong write-up does not just describe what was measured, but justifies why that method was chosen: for example, explaining that systematic sampling was used along a Cleveleys beach transect because it gives even coverage of the whole profile, or that a stratified approach suited Salford Quays because the area contains genuinely different zones (residential, commercial, leisure) that needed separate representation.

#### 3.3.2.3a Enquiry strand 3: range of visual, graphical and cartographic presentation methods
Fieldwork results can be presented using a wide range of methods: annotated photographs and field sketches, graphs and charts (bar charts, line graphs, scatter graphs), and cartographic methods such as annotated maps, choropleth maps or located pie charts/bar charts on a base map, each suited to showing different kinds of pattern in the data.

#### 3.3.2.3b Enquiry strand 3: selecting and accurately using presentation methods
The choice of presentation method should match the type of data: a beach profile is best shown as a cross-section/line graph; the distribution of an environmental quality score across different zones might best be shown as a located bar chart on a map. Whichever method is chosen, it must be constructed accurately, with a clear title, correctly labelled axes or key, and an appropriate scale.

#### 3.3.2.3c Enquiry strand 3: describing, explaining and adapting presentation methods
A strong answer can describe what a chosen presentation method shows, explain why it was an appropriate choice for that particular data, and suggest how it could be adapted or improved, for example combining two graph types, or adding an extra category to a key, to communicate the pattern in the data more clearly.
