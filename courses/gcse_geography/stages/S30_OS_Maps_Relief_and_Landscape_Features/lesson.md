# S30_OS_Maps_Relief_and_Landscape_Features - Lesson: Geographical skills: OS map relief, cross-sections and drawing inferences about the landscape

## Goal
The learner reads contours, gradient and spot heights, draws and interprets cross-sections, and draws inferences about physical and human landscape, including coastal and glacial features, from OS maps.

## Syllabus items taught here
- 3.4.1h - OS maps: gradient, contour and spot height: interpret relief
- 3.4.1i - OS maps: numerical and statistical information: count and calculate from maps
- 3.4.1j - OS maps: identifying landscape features and describing their characteristics: describe
- 3.4.1k - OS maps: major relief features and cross-sectional drawings: relate cross-sections to relief
- 3.4.1l - OS maps: drawing inferences about physical and human landscape: relief, drainage, settlement, communication, land use
- 3.4.1m - OS maps: cross sections and transects: construct and interpret
- 3.4.1n - OS maps: physical features of coastal and glacial landscapes on large-scale maps: describe features (coasts and glacial chosen)

## How to teach this
Ask: 'A map is flat, but the land is not. How does a flat piece of paper show you that a hill is steep in one place and gentle in another?'

#### 3.4.1h OS maps: gradient, contour and spot height
Contour lines join points of equal height, usually at 10 m intervals on a 1:50 000 map; where contours are close together, the land is steep, and where they are far apart, the land is gentle. A spot height is an exact height marked at a single point (often a triangulation pillar or hilltop) and gives a precise value rather than a range. Gradient can be estimated by comparing horizontal distance between two contours with the vertical height difference between them.

#### 3.4.1i OS maps: numerical and statistical information
Beyond relief, OS maps carry numerical information: grid square area (1 km2 per square at 1:50 000), scale for calculating distance, contour values for height, and figures in the map key (for example, road numbers). Learners should be able to extract and use these numbers, for example calculating the approximate area of a woodland by counting whole and part grid squares it covers.

#### 3.4.1j OS maps: identifying landscape features and describing their characteristics
Using the map key and contour pattern, landscape features can be identified: a valley (V-shaped contours pointing towards higher ground, often with a river), a spur (contours pointing away from higher ground), a plateau (a large flat high area with contours only at the edges), or a floodplain (flat, low-lying land next to a river, often marked with the risk of flooding). Describe each feature's shape, height range and any linked human use.

#### 3.4.1k OS maps: major relief features and cross-sectional drawings
Major relief features (a mountain range, an escarpment, a river valley) can be identified from the overall contour pattern across a map extract, and their shape shown clearly by drawing a cross-section — a side-on profile of the land surface along a chosen line. A cross-sectional drawing lets a reader see steepness and height changes that are harder to judge directly from contour lines alone.

#### 3.4.1l OS maps: drawing inferences about physical and human landscape
An inference goes beyond simply describing what is shown, using map evidence to reason about why the landscape looks as it does: for example, inferring that a settlement grew at a bridging point because the river narrows there, or that farming is arable rather than livestock because of the field pattern and lack of contour steepness. Always support an inference with specific map evidence (grid references, contour values, symbols).

#### 3.4.1m OS maps: cross sections and transects
To construct a cross-section, lay a strip of paper along the chosen line on the map, mark where each contour crosses the paper edge and note its height, then transfer these marks to graph paper with height on the vertical axis and distance along the horizontal axis, plotting and joining the points smoothly. A transect is a similar line drawn across a landscape (often in fieldwork) along which features or data are recorded at intervals.

#### 3.4.1n OS maps: physical features of coastal and glacial landscapes on large-scale maps
On a large-scale (1:25 000) OS map extract, coastal features can be identified from their shape and symbols (a headland as a protruding, often cliff-marked coastline; a bay as a curved indentation; a spit as a narrow finger of land extending from the coast). Glacial features can similarly be identified (a corrie as a steep, rounded hollow often holding a small lake or tarn; a U-shaped valley as a wide, flat-floored valley with steep sides).

## Explicitly not here
