# S25_Fieldwork_Enquiry_Questions_and_Data_Collection - Lesson: Fieldwork requirements, and enquiry strands 1-2: questions, theory, evidence and risk

## Goal
The learner explains the fieldwork requirements and how fieldwork is assessed, and applies enquiry strands 1 and 2: forming a question, linking to theory, choosing evidence and locations, managing risk, and selecting appropriate data.

## Syllabus items taught here
- 3.3.2.0a - Fieldwork requirements: two enquiries in contrasting environments: know what must be done
- 3.3.2.0b - Fieldwork assessment: unfamiliar contexts and own enquiries: two ways assessed
- 3.3.2.1a - Enquiry strand 1: suitable questions and hypotheses: select a testable question
- 3.3.2.1b - Enquiry strand 1: the geographical theory or concept underpinning the enquiry: link enquiry to theory
- 3.3.2.1c - Enquiry strand 1: primary and secondary evidence and fieldwork locations: choose sources and sites
- 3.3.2.1d - Enquiry strand 1: risks of fieldwork and how to reduce them: risk assessment

## How to teach this
Ask: 'This course uses two worked fieldwork enquiries, Cleveleys and Salford Quays. Before we look at their data, what makes a good geographical enquiry question, as opposed to a question that is too vague to actually investigate?'

#### 3.3.2.0a Fieldwork requirements: two enquiries in contrasting environments
AQA requires students to complete two fieldwork enquiries in contrasting environments: typically one investigating a physical geography topic (here, coastal management at Cleveleys) and one investigating a human geography topic (here, urban regeneration at Salford Quays). This gives experience of the full enquiry process (question, data collection, presentation, analysis, conclusion, evaluation) applied to genuinely different kinds of geographical question.

#### 3.3.2.0b Fieldwork assessment: unfamiliar contexts and own enquiries
Fieldwork is assessed on Paper 3 in two ways: through questions about the learner's own two enquiries (this course uses Cleveleys and Salford Quays as its worked examples), and through questions based on an unfamiliar fieldwork context provided in the exam, testing whether the learner can apply general fieldwork skills and reasoning to a new, unseen example rather than only recalling their own trips.

#### 3.3.2.1a Enquiry strand 1: suitable questions and hypotheses
A good enquiry question or hypothesis is specific, testable with real data, and focused on a genuine geographical issue, for example 'How effective is coastal management at Cleveleys?' rather than a vague question like 'What is Cleveleys like?'. A hypothesis is a testable statement predicting an expected outcome, such as 'coastal defences at Cleveleys have successfully reduced the rate of erosion', which the fieldwork data can then support or reject.

#### 3.3.2.1b Enquiry strand 1: the geographical theory or concept underpinning the enquiry
Every enquiry should be grounded in relevant geographical theory: the Cleveleys coastal enquiry draws on theory of coastal processes and management (erosion, longshore drift, hard and soft engineering); the Salford Quays urban enquiry draws on theory of urban regeneration and change (deindustrialisation, redevelopment, changing land use). Linking the enquiry to theory shows why the question matters geographically, not just locally.

#### 3.3.2.1c Enquiry strand 1: primary and secondary evidence and fieldwork locations
Primary evidence is data collected first-hand by the learner during fieldwork (for example, measuring beach profiles at Cleveleys, or conducting an environmental quality survey at Salford Quays). Secondary evidence is data collected by someone else and used by the learner (for example, historical maps, council reports or census data). Choosing suitable fieldwork locations means selecting sites that will actually let the enquiry question be tested, for example comparing a defended and an undefended stretch of coastline near Cleveleys.

#### 3.3.2.1d Enquiry strand 1: risks of fieldwork and how to reduce them
Fieldwork carries risks: at a coastal site like Cleveleys, risks include slippery rocks, tides and cold water; at an urban site like Salford Quays, risks include traffic, water hazards near the docks, and unfamiliar areas. Risk assessment identifies hazards in advance and plans ways to reduce them: appropriate footwear and clothing, checking tide times, staying in groups, following teacher instructions, and carrying a mobile phone and first aid kit.
