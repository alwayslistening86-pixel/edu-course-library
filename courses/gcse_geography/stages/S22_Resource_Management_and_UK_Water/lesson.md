# S22_Resource_Management_and_UK_Water - Lesson: Resource significance, global inequality, and managing UK water supply

## Goal
The learner explains why food, water and energy matter to wellbeing, describes global inequalities in resource supply and consumption, and explains UK water demand, quality management, supply-demand matching and transfer.

## Syllabus items taught here
- 3.2.3.1a - Significance of food, water and energy to well-being: explain significance
- 3.2.3.1b - Global inequalities in supply and consumption of resources: describe patterns
- 3.2.3.1c - Overview of resources in relation to the UK: changing demand and provision
- 3.2.3.1e - UK water: changing demand for water: explain trends
- 3.2.3.1f - UK water: quality and pollution management: explain and evaluate
- 3.2.3.1g - UK water: matching supply and demand, deficit and surplus: map pattern

## How to teach this
Ask: 'Most of the UK's rain falls in the north and west, but most of the demand for water is in the south and east, where most people live. What problem does that create?'

#### 3.2.3.1a Significance of food, water and energy to well-being
Food, water and energy are essential resources for human wellbeing and development: food provides the nutrition needed for health and productivity; water is needed for drinking, sanitation, agriculture and industry; and energy powers homes, transport, industry and services. A secure, reliable supply of all three underpins a country's economic development, public health and political stability; shortages in any one can cause serious hardship and conflict.

#### 3.2.3.1b Global inequalities in supply and consumption of resources
Supply and consumption of food, water and energy are very unevenly distributed globally. Higher-income countries generally consume far more per person than lower-income countries, and access to a reliable supply is also unequal, shaped by climate, geography, infrastructure and wealth; some regions face physical scarcity (not enough available), while others face economic scarcity (resources exist but people cannot afford or access them).

#### 3.2.3.1c Overview of resources in relation to the UK
The UK's demand for food, water and energy has grown due to a rising population and higher living standards and consumption per person, and provision has had to change in response: for example, more resources are now imported, supply infrastructure has had to expand and adapt, and there is growing emphasis on managing demand and sustainability rather than only increasing supply.

#### 3.2.3.1e UK water: changing demand for water
UK water demand has risen due to population growth, a rising number of households (partly from more people living alone), increased use of water-using appliances (washing machines, dishwashers), and higher expectations of water use (showering, gardens, leisure). Demand is not evenly spread: the densely populated south and east of England has much higher demand than the more sparsely populated north and west.

#### 3.2.3.1f UK water: quality and pollution management
Water quality in the UK is managed through regulation and treatment: water companies and the Environment Agency monitor rivers and treat both drinking water (to remove contaminants) and wastewater (before it is returned to rivers or the sea) to protect public health and aquatic ecosystems. Pollution risks include agricultural run-off (fertilisers and pesticides), industrial discharge, and sewage overflow during heavy rainfall, all of which must be managed and reduced.

#### 3.2.3.1g UK water: matching supply and demand, deficit and surplus
The UK has a clear geographical mismatch between water supply and demand: the north and west (such as Scotland, Wales, and the Lake District) receive high rainfall and have a water surplus, while the south and east of England, despite lower rainfall, has the highest population density and therefore the greatest demand, creating areas of water deficit, particularly during dry periods.

#### 3.2.3.1h UK water: the need for transfer to maintain supplies
Because supply and demand do not match geographically, water must sometimes be transferred from surplus to deficit areas via reservoirs, pipelines and aqueducts. Examples include Thirlmere and Haweswater in the Lake District, which supply water to Manchester and Merseyside via long-distance aqueducts, and Kielder Water in Northumberland, a large reservoir built to secure supply for industry and population in the North East of England, including the Tyne, Wear and Tees areas.
