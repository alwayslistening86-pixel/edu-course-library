# S19_Financial_Markets_Monetary_Policy - Lesson: Financial markets, banks, monetary policy and financial regulation

## Goal
The learner explains money and the money supply, the money, capital and foreign exchange markets, debt and equity, bond prices and yields, commercial and investment banks (balance sheets, objectives, credit creation), central banks and monetary policy (the MPC, bank rate, transmission mechanism, QE, Funding for Lending, forward guidance), and financial regulation (systemic risk, moral hazard, the FPC, PRA and FCA).

## Syllabus items taught here
- 4.2.4.1 - The structure of financial markets and financial assets
- 4.2.4.2 - Commercial banks and investment banks
- 4.2.4.3 - Central banks and monetary policy
- 4.2.4.4 - The regulation of the financial system

## How to teach this
Ask where the money in a bank account 'is'. Teach each topic as a chain of reasoning: define, explain with a diagram (described in words, then sketched by the learner), apply to a real context, then evaluate (it depends on...). Numerical examples were computed when the course was built. For current figures (Bank Rate, the National Living Wage, inflation, tax rates), check the latest official sources rather than relying on the course text.

#### 4.2.4.1 The structure of financial markets and financial assets
Money: characteristics (acceptable, portable, durable, divisible, scarce/limited supply, hard to counterfeit) and functions (medium of exchange, store of value, unit of account, standard of deferred payment). Narrow money (notes, coins and banks' reserves at the central bank) and broad money (includes bank deposits). The money market (short-term borrowing and lending), capital market (medium- and long-term finance: shares and bonds; primary and secondary markets) and foreign exchange market. Firms raise finance by issuing shares (equity), issuing corporate bonds (debt) and borrowing from banks. Bond prices and market interest rates are inversely related: a bond pays a fixed coupon; if market rates rise, the bond's price falls until its yield matches. Yield = annual coupon ÷ market price x 100. *Example:* a £100 bond with a 4% coupon (£4 a year) trading at £80 yields 5.0%.

#### 4.2.4.2 Commercial banks and investment banks
Commercial banks accept deposits, make loans, provide payment services; investment banks trade securities, advise on mergers and help firms raise capital (only an outline is needed; many banks do both, which may increase systemic risk). A bank's balance sheet: liabilities (deposits, borrowing, shareholders' equity/capital) and assets (cash and reserves, loans, securities). Objectives: liquidity, profitability and security (solvency), which conflict: the most profitable assets (long-term loans) are least liquid and riskiest. Banks create credit: when a bank makes a loan, it creates a deposit, increasing broad money; constrained by capital and liquidity requirements, demand for loans, and prudence (the credit multiplier isn't required).

#### 4.2.4.3 Central banks and monetary policy
A central bank (the Bank of England) is the government's and bankers' bank, lender of last resort, issues notes, and conducts monetary policy. Monetary policy influences interest rates, the money supply and credit, and the exchange rate. The government sets the objective (2% CPI inflation, while supporting growth and employment); the Monetary Policy Committee (MPC) sets Bank Rate, considering data such as wage growth, the output gap, house prices, the exchange rate, commodity prices and inflation expectations. Transmission mechanism: a rise in Bank Rate raises market interest rates, reducing borrowing and spending (consumption, investment), reducing asset prices and wealth, lowering confidence, and appreciating the exchange rate (reducing net exports and import prices), so AD and inflation fall. The exchange rate affects AD and the objectives. Other instruments: quantitative easing (the central bank buys assets, especially government bonds, to lower long-term interest rates and increase the money supply) and its reverse, quantitative tightening (selling or not replacing bonds, under way in the UK since 2022); the Funding for Lending Scheme (2012-2018, cheap funding for banks linked to their lending; now closed); and forward guidance (signalling future rates).

#### 4.2.4.4 The regulation of the financial system
Regulation of the financial system aims to prevent crises and protect consumers. Market failures in the financial sector: asymmetric information, externalities, moral hazard (risk-taking because a bailout is expected: 'too big to fail'), speculation and market bubbles, market rigging. Systemic risk: the failure of one institution spreads through the system. The Bank of England's Financial Policy Committee (FPC; macroprudential regulation of the system as a whole), the Prudential Regulation Authority (PRA; microprudential regulation of individual banks and insurers) and the Financial Conduct Authority (FCA; conduct and consumer protection). Capital ratios and liquidity ratios; the lender of last resort role. Why banks fail: they borrow short (deposits that can be withdrawn at any time) and lend long (loans and mortgages), so a loss of confidence can cause a run they can't meet (liquidity risk), and losses on loans can wipe out their capital (solvency risk).
