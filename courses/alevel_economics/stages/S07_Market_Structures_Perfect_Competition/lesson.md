# S07_Market_Structures_Perfect_Competition - Lesson: Market structures, objectives of firms and perfect competition

## Goal
The learner explains the spectrum of market structures and concentration ratios, the objectives of firms (profit maximisation and alternatives, the divorce of ownership from control), and the short- and long-run equilibrium of firms in perfect competition.

## Syllabus items taught here
- 4.1.5.1 - Market structures
- 4.1.5.2 - The objectives of firms
- 4.1.5.3 - Perfect competition

## How to teach this
Ask why a farmer selling wheat can't choose their own price. Teach each topic as a chain of reasoning: define, explain with a diagram (described in words, then sketched by the learner), apply to a real context, then evaluate (it depends on...). Numerical examples were computed when the course was built. For current figures (Bank Rate, the National Living Wage, inflation, tax rates), check the latest official sources rather than relying on the course text.

#### 4.1.5.1 Market structures
Market structures range from perfect competition through monopolistic competition and oligopoly to monopoly, differing in the number and size of firms, product differentiation, barriers to entry and information. The concentration ratio measures market share of the largest n firms. *Example:* the four largest firms have shares 28%, 22%, 15% and 10%: 4-firm concentration ratio = 75%, suggesting oligopoly.

#### 4.1.5.2 The objectives of firms
Traditional theory assumes firms maximise profit (MC = MR). Alternatives: revenue maximisation (MR = 0), sales maximisation (subject to normal profit: AC = AR), survival, and satisficing. The divorce of ownership from control in large firms leads to a principal-agent problem: managers (agents) may pursue their own objectives rather than shareholders' (principals'). Not-for-profit organisations have other objectives.

#### 4.1.5.3 Perfect competition
Perfect competition assumptions: many buyers and sellers, identical (homogeneous) products, perfect information, no barriers to entry or exit, firms are price takers. Short run: firms may earn supernormal profit (P > AC) or losses; long run: entry or exit drives price to minimum AC, so only normal profit is made. The firm's supply curve is its MC curve above AVC; a firm shuts down in the short run if P < AVC. Long-run equilibrium is allocatively efficient (P = MC) and productively efficient (minimum AC). Perfect competition is a model: few real markets meet all its assumptions, but it is a benchmark.
