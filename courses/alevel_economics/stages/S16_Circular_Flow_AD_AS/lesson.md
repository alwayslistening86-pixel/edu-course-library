# S16_Circular_Flow_AD_AS - Lesson: The circular flow of income and AD/AS analysis

## Goal
The learner explains the circular flow of income with injections and withdrawals, the multiplier, the AD/AS model, the determinants of aggregate demand (consumption, investment including the accelerator, government spending, net exports), the effects of AD changes on output and prices, and the determinants of short-run and long-run aggregate supply (including Keynesian and classical LRAS).

## How to teach this
Ask why a new factory can raise a town's income by more than the factory's own wage bill. Teach each topic as a chain of reasoning: define, explain with a diagram (described in words, then sketched by the learner), apply to a real context, then evaluate (it depends on...). Numerical examples were computed when the course was built. For current figures (Bank Rate, the National Living Wage, inflation, tax rates), check the latest official sources rather than relying on the course text.

#### 4.2.2.1 The circular flow of income
The circular flow: households supply factors to firms and receive income, spent on firms' output. Withdrawals (leakages): savings, taxation, imports; injections: investment, government spending, exports. National income = national output = national expenditure (three ways of measuring the same flow). Nominal (money) income vs real income (adjusted for inflation); full-employment national income is the output when resources are fully used. Equilibrium national income occurs where injections = withdrawals. The multiplier: an injection causes a larger final change in national income, because spending becomes someone else's income, with withdrawals at each round; multiplier k = 1 ÷ (1 - MPC), where MPC is the marginal propensity to consume domestic output. *Example:* MPC = 0.5: k = 2; a £10bn rise in investment raises national income by £20bn.

#### 4.2.2.2 Aggregate demand and aggregate supply analysis
The AD/AS model: aggregate demand (total planned spending on domestic output at each price level; downward sloping because of the wealth effect, the trade effect and the interest rate effect) and aggregate supply (total planned output). Equilibrium where AD = SRAS; the price level and real output are determined together. Shifts vs movements along.

#### 4.2.2.3 The determinants of aggregate demand
AD = C + I + G + (X - M). Consumption: disposable income, the propensity to consume, wealth, interest rates, confidence, credit availability. Saving: its determinants and the paradox of thrift. Investment: expected profitability, interest rates, business confidence, technology, government policies; the accelerator: investment depends on the rate of change of output (so investment is volatile). Government spending: policy decisions. Net exports: exchange rate, relative inflation, incomes abroad, competitiveness.

#### 4.2.2.4 Aggregate demand and the level of economic activity
An increase in AD raises real output and the price level; the split depends on the shape of SRAS and spare capacity. Demand-side shocks (e.g. a financial crisis reducing confidence) and the multiplier effects on national income.

#### 4.2.2.5 Determinants of short-run aggregate supply
Short-run aggregate supply shifts with costs of production: wages, raw material and energy prices, productivity, indirect taxes and subsidies, and the exchange rate (import prices). A supply shock (e.g. an oil price rise) shifts SRAS left, raising prices and lowering output (stagflation).

#### 4.2.2.6 Determinants of long-run aggregate supply
Long-run aggregate supply (the productive potential) depends on the quantity and quality of factors of production, technology, productivity, and institutions (supply-side policies). Classical view: LRAS vertical at full-employment output; in the long run, wages and prices adjust and AD affects only the price level. Keynesian view: LRAS is horizontal at low output (spare capacity), then upward sloping, then vertical at full capacity; the economy can remain below full employment. LRAS shifts outward with economic growth.
