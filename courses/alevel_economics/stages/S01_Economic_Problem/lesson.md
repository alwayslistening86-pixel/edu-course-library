# S01_Economic_Problem - Lesson: Economic methodology and the economic problem

## Goal
The learner explains economics as a social science, positive and normative statements and value judgements, the purpose of economic activity, factors of production, scarcity, choice and opportunity cost, and uses production possibility diagrams.

## Syllabus items taught here
- 4.1.1.1 - Economic methodology
- 4.1.1.2 - The nature and purpose of economic activity
- 4.1.1.3 - Economic resources
- 4.1.1.4 - Scarcity, choice and the allocation of resources
- 4.1.1.5 - Production possibility diagrams

## How to teach this
Ask what the learner gave up to be here now, and call it their opportunity cost. Teach each topic as a chain of reasoning: define, explain with a diagram (described in words, then sketched by the learner), apply to a real context, then evaluate (it depends on...). Numerical examples were computed when the course was built. For current figures (Bank Rate, the National Living Wage, inflation, tax rates), check the latest official sources rather than relying on the course text.

#### 4.1.1.1 Economic methodology
Economics is a social science: economists build models (simplifications with assumptions, such as ceteris paribus) and test them against evidence, but controlled experiments are rarely possible and people's behaviour changes. Positive statements can be tested against evidence ('a rise in the minimum wage reduced youth employment'); normative statements contain value judgements ('the minimum wage should be raised'). Value judgements influence economic decisions and policy; economists may agree on the positive analysis but disagree on the normative conclusions.

#### 4.1.1.2 The nature and purpose of economic activity
The central purpose of economic activity is the production of goods and services to satisfy needs and wants. Economic decisions weigh up the welfare (well-being) they create; economic agents include consumers, producers (firms) and the government.

#### 4.1.1.3 Economic resources
Factors of production: land (natural resources), labour (human effort), capital (man-made aids to production, e.g. machinery) and enterprise (organising the other factors and bearing risk). Renewable resources can be replenished; non-renewable ones cannot; environmental resources are part of the economy's stock and should be considered when evaluating economic activity.

#### 4.1.1.4 Scarcity, choice and the allocation of resources
The fundamental economic problem is scarcity: wants are unlimited but resources are finite, so choices must be made about what to produce, how and for whom. Opportunity cost is the next best alternative forgone. Economic systems (market, command, mixed) allocate resources differently; all economies face the same problem. Different decision makers have different objectives: consumers maximise utility, firms profits, governments social welfare, although in practice objectives are more varied.

#### 4.1.1.5 Production possibility diagrams
A production possibility diagram (frontier/curve, PPF) shows the maximum combinations of two goods an economy can produce with its resources and technology. Points on the curve are productively efficient (no more of one good can be made without less of another), but not all are allocatively efficient: only one point matches the combination society values most; inside, resources are unemployed or inefficiently used; outside, unattainable now. Movement along the curve shows opportunity cost; a concave (bowed-out) curve shows increasing opportunity cost as resources are not equally suited to both goods; a straight line shows constant opportunity cost. An outward shift shows economic growth (more resources, better technology, higher productivity); the distinction between capital and consumer goods matters for future growth. *Example:* moving from 100 to 80 units of capital goods to gain 30 units of consumer goods: the opportunity cost of each consumer good is 20/30 = 0.67 units of capital goods.
