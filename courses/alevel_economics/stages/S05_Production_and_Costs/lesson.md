# S05_Production_and_Costs - Lesson: Production, specialisation, returns and costs

## Goal
The learner explains production and productivity, specialisation and the division of labour, the law of diminishing returns and returns to scale, short- and long-run costs (fixed, variable, average and marginal), economies and diseconomies of scale, and calculates costs.

Ask why a restaurant kitchen doesn't get twice as productive by hiring twice as many chefs. Teach each topic as a chain of reasoning: define, explain with a diagram (described in words, then sketched by the learner), apply to a real context, then evaluate (it depends on...). #### 4.1.4.1 Production and productivity
Production converts inputs into outputs. Productivity is output per unit of input per period; labour productivity = output per worker (or per hour). Higher productivity lowers unit costs and improves competitiveness; it is influenced by capital, technology, skills and management.

#### 4.1.4.2 Specialisation, division of labour and exchange
Specialisation (by individuals, firms, regions and countries) and the division of labour (breaking production into tasks) raise productivity: workers develop skills, time isn't wasted switching tasks, machinery can be used. Drawbacks: boredom, over-dependence, risk. Specialisation requires exchange; money as a medium of exchange avoids the double coincidence of wants needed in barter.

#### 4.1.4.3 The law of diminishing returns and returns to scale
Short run: at least one factor is fixed; long run: all factors are variable. The law of diminishing returns (short run): as more of a variable factor is added to a fixed factor, the marginal product eventually falls, so marginal cost rises. Returns to scale (long run): increasing (output rises proportionately more than inputs), constant, or decreasing returns to scale.

#### 4.1.4.4 Costs of production
Costs: fixed costs (don't vary with output), variable costs (vary with output); total cost = TFC + TVC; average cost AC = TC/Q; marginal cost MC = change in TC from one more unit. Short-run AC is U-shaped because of diminishing returns (after falling AFC); MC cuts AC at its minimum. Long-run AC (LRAC) is the envelope of short-run curves, shaped by economies and diseconomies of scale; it may be U-shaped or L-shaped (costs fall, then stay constant over a wide range of output if diseconomies don't appear). Increasing returns to scale lead to economies of scale (falling LRAC); decreasing returns to diseconomies. Factor prices and productivity affect costs: a rise in wages raises costs unless productivity rises too, and relative factor prices influence the choice of inputs (e.g. substituting capital for labour when wages rise). *Example:* TC for 0-6 units = [50, 80, 100, 114, 136, 170, 220]: TFC = 50; MC = [30, 20, 14, 22, 34, 50]; AC at 4 units = 34.

#### 4.1.4.5 Economies and diseconomies of scale
Internal economies of scale: technical (larger machinery, the law of increased dimensions), managerial (specialist managers), financial (cheaper borrowing), marketing and purchasing (bulk buying), risk-bearing (diversification). External economies: from the growth of the industry (a skilled local labour force, specialist suppliers, infrastructure). Diseconomies of scale: coordination and communication problems, poor motivation, control problems. Minimum efficient scale: the lowest output at which LRAC is minimised. A firm's LRAC can shift with external economies or diseconomies.

#### QS6 Calculate cost, revenue and profit (marginal, average, totals)
Calculating costs, revenue and profit: totals (TC = TFC + TVC; TR = P x Q), averages (AC = TC/Q, AR = TR/Q) and marginals (MC and MR are changes in the totals for one more unit, or Δ totals ÷ Δ output for larger steps). Profit = TR - TC.
