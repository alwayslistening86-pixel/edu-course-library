# S20_Fiscal_and_Supply_Side_Policy - Lesson: Fiscal policy and supply-side policies

## Goal
The learner explains fiscal policy (government spending, taxation, budget balances, the national debt, automatic stabilisers, the multiplier), the principles and types of taxation, and supply-side policies (market-based and interventionist), evaluating their effects on AD, AS and the macroeconomic objectives.

## Syllabus items taught here
- 4.2.5.1 - Fiscal policy
- 4.2.5.2 - Supply-side policies

## How to teach this
Ask whether cutting income tax pays for itself. Teach each topic as a chain of reasoning: define, explain with a diagram (described in words, then sketched by the learner), apply to a real context, then evaluate (it depends on...). Numerical examples were computed when the course was built. For current figures (Bank Rate, the National Living Wage, inflation, tax rates), check the latest official sources rather than relying on the course text.

#### 4.2.5.1 Fiscal policy
Fiscal policy uses government spending and taxation to influence AD (demand management) and the supply side (e.g. through incentives and public investment). Budget deficit: spending exceeds revenue; surplus: revenue exceeds spending; the national debt is the accumulated stock of borrowing. Cyclical deficit (from the economic cycle) vs structural deficit (would remain at full employment). Automatic stabilisers (tax revenue and benefit spending that change with the cycle) vs discretionary policy. Taxation: direct (income tax, corporation tax) and indirect (VAT, excise duties); progressive, proportional and regressive taxes; principles of a good tax (Adam Smith's canons: equity, certainty, convenience, economy; plus efficiency and flexibility). The Laffer curve suggests very high tax rates may reduce revenue. The Office for Budget Responsibility (OBR) makes independent forecasts of the economy and public finances and judges whether the government is on course to meet its fiscal rules. Evaluate fiscal policy: time lags, crowding out, the size of the multiplier, debt sustainability. *Example:* a person earning £60,000 pays tax of £12,000: the average tax rate is 20%; if the rate on the last £1,000 is 40%, the marginal rate is 40%.

#### 4.2.5.2 Supply-side policies
Supply-side improvements (any increase in productive potential, which may come from firms and markets themselves, e.g. new technology) are distinct from supply-side policies (deliberate government measures). Supply-side policies aim to increase productive potential (shift LRAS right) and improve the efficiency of markets. Market-based: tax cuts (income and corporation tax) to improve incentives, reducing welfare benefits, labour market deregulation (reducing trade union power), privatisation and deregulation, promoting competition. Interventionist: government spending on education and training, infrastructure, subsidies for research and development, regional policy and industrial policy (support for particular sectors or technologies). Effects: lower unemployment (natural rate), lower inflationary pressure, improved competitiveness and current account. Evaluation: long time lags, uncertain effects, costs, equity (market-based policies may widen inequality).
