# S14_Government_Intervention - Lesson: Competition policy, ownership, regulation, intervention and government failure

## Goal
The learner explains competition policy (CMA, mergers, abuse of market power), public ownership, privatisation, regulation and deregulation, the tools of government intervention (indirect taxes, subsidies, price controls, state provision, tradable permits, regulation, information) and government failure.

## Syllabus items taught here
- 4.1.8.7 - Competition policy
- 4.1.8.8 - Public ownership, privatisation, regulation and deregulation of markets
- 4.1.8.9 - Government intervention in markets
- 4.1.8.10 - Government failure

## How to teach this
Ask whether a sugar tax on soft drinks is a good policy, and how you'd know. Teach each topic as a chain of reasoning: define, explain with a diagram (described in words, then sketched by the learner), apply to a real context, then evaluate (it depends on...). Numerical examples were computed when the course was built. For current figures (Bank Rate, the National Living Wage, inflation, tax rates), check the latest official sources rather than relying on the course text.

#### 4.1.8.7 Competition policy
Competition policy aims to promote competition and protect consumers: the Competition and Markets Authority (CMA) investigates mergers (which may reduce competition), anti-competitive practices (cartels, price fixing) and the abuse of a dominant position (predatory pricing, limit pricing, refusal to supply). Remedies include fines, blocking mergers, and forcing divestment. Regulation of prices (e.g. RPI - X price caps, introduced for privatised utilities: prices could rise by inflation minus an efficiency factor X; regulators now mostly index to CPI or CPIH instead, e.g. Ofwat's CPIH - X). EU competition policy (the European Commission) applies to firms operating in EU markets, including UK firms trading there, and cases can affect UK markets and performance targets (quality standards).

#### 4.1.8.8 Public ownership, privatisation, regulation and deregulation of markets
Public ownership (nationalisation): the state owns and runs industries (e.g. natural monopolies) to pursue social objectives, but may suffer from X-inefficiency and lack of profit incentive. Privatisation transfers ownership to the private sector: aims include efficiency, competition, raising revenue and reducing state involvement; problems: private monopolies, need for regulation, underinvestment. Regulation and deregulation (removing rules to increase competition, e.g. in bus services or airlines). Regulatory capture: regulators act in the interest of the firms they regulate.

#### 4.1.8.9 Government intervention in markets
Government intervention to correct market failure: indirect taxes (specific or ad valorem) on negative externalities and demerit goods, shifting supply up by the tax and internalising the externality (incidence depends on elasticities); subsidies for positive externalities and merit goods; minimum prices (above equilibrium; e.g. minimum unit pricing of alcohol, causing excess supply) and maximum prices (below equilibrium; e.g. rent controls, causing excess demand); state provision of public and merit goods; tradable pollution permits (a cap on total pollution, with trading to reduce it at least cost); regulation (bans, limits); extending property rights (so owners can charge for damage or protect resources, e.g. fishing quotas); provision of information. Each has strengths and weaknesses (e.g. difficulty valuing externalities, inelastic demand limits the effect of taxes).

#### 4.1.8.10 Government failure
Government failure: intervention leads to a net welfare loss compared with the free market outcome. Causes: distortion of price signals, unintended consequences (e.g. black markets, tax evasion), excessive administrative costs, information gaps (e.g. valuing externalities), conflicting objectives, regulatory capture, political short-termism.
