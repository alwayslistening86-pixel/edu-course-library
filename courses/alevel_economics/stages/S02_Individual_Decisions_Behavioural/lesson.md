# S02_Individual_Decisions_Behavioural - Lesson: Consumer behaviour, imperfect information and behavioural economics

## Goal
The learner explains rational decision making and utility (including diminishing marginal utility), imperfect and asymmetric information, behavioural economics (bounded rationality, bounded self-control, biases, social norms, altruism, fairness) and how behavioural insights inform policy (choice architecture, framing, nudges, default, restricted and mandated choices).

## Syllabus items taught here
- 4.1.2.1 - Consumer behaviour
- 4.1.2.2 - Imperfect information
- 4.1.2.3 - Aspects of behavioural economic theory
- 4.1.2.4 - Behavioural economics and economic policy

## How to teach this
Ask why gyms sell annual memberships that most people barely use. Teach each topic as a chain of reasoning: define, explain with a diagram (described in words, then sketched by the learner), apply to a real context, then evaluate (it depends on...). Numerical examples were computed when the course was built. For current figures (Bank Rate, the National Living Wage, inflation, tax rates), check the latest official sources rather than relying on the course text.

#### 4.1.2.1 Consumer behaviour
Traditional economic theory assumes consumers act rationally to maximise their utility (satisfaction). Diminishing marginal utility: each additional unit consumed gives less extra satisfaction than the previous one, which helps explain the downward-sloping demand curve. The margin matters: decisions compare marginal benefits and marginal costs.

#### 4.1.2.2 Imperfect information
Imperfect information: buyers and sellers lack full information, affecting decisions. Asymmetric information: one party knows more than the other (e.g. second-hand car sellers, insurance buyers, doctors and patients), which can lead to market failure (under- or over-consumption, adverse selection, moral hazard).

#### 4.1.2.3 Aspects of behavioural economic theory
Behavioural economics questions full rationality. Bounded rationality: decisions are limited by information, cognitive ability and time, so people satisfice. Bounded self-control: people know what's best but lack the willpower (e.g. over-eating, under-saving). Biases in decision making: rules of thumb (heuristics), anchoring (relying on an initial piece of information), availability bias (judging likelihood by how easily examples come to mind). Social norms influence behaviour; altruism means people care about others' welfare; perceptions of fairness matter (e.g. the ultimatum game shows people reject 'unfair' offers even at a cost to themselves).

#### 4.1.2.4 Behavioural economics and economic policy
Behavioural insights inform policy. Choice architecture: the design of the way choices are presented. Framing: the way information is presented changes decisions (e.g. '90% fat-free' vs '10% fat'). Nudges: changes in choice architecture that steer behaviour without restricting options or changing incentives much (e.g. healthy food at eye level). Default choices: the option that applies if no active choice is made (e.g. automatic enrolment in workplace pensions raised participation). Restricted choice: limiting options to make decisions easier. Mandated choice: people must make a decision (e.g. whether to be an organ donor). Evaluation: nudges are cheap and preserve freedom, but effects may be small, temporary, or paternalistic.
