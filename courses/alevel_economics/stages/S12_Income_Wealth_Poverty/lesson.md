# S12_Income_Wealth_Poverty - Lesson: The distribution of income and wealth, poverty and inequality

## Goal
The learner distinguishes income and wealth, explains the causes of inequality and the Lorenz curve and Gini coefficient, distinguishes absolute and relative poverty and their causes and effects, evaluates government policies to reduce poverty and inequality, and uses means, medians and quantiles.

## Syllabus items taught here
- 4.1.7.1 - The distribution of income and wealth
- 4.1.7.2 - The problem of poverty
- 4.1.7.3 - Government policies to alleviate poverty and to influence the distribution of income and wealth
- QS3 - Understand and use the terms mean, median and relevant quantiles

## How to teach this
Ask whether a country where everyone's income doubles but the richest's triples has become fairer. Teach each topic as a chain of reasoning: define, explain with a diagram (described in words, then sketched by the learner), apply to a real context, then evaluate (it depends on...). Numerical examples were computed when the course was built. For current figures (Bank Rate, the National Living Wage, inflation, tax rates), check the latest official sources rather than relying on the course text.

#### 4.1.7.1 The distribution of income and wealth
Income is a flow (earnings, rent, interest, dividends, benefits); wealth is a stock of assets (property, pensions, savings, shares). Equality means equal shares; equity means fairness (a normative idea). Causes of inequality: differences in skills and education, wage differentials, inheritance and wealth, household composition, discrimination, tax and benefit systems, globalisation and technological change. The Lorenz curve plots the cumulative share of income against the cumulative share of households; the further from the line of equality, the greater the inequality. Gini coefficient = area between the line of equality and the Lorenz curve ÷ total area under the line of equality (0 = perfect equality, 1 = perfect inequality). Inequality can provide incentives but may harm growth, social cohesion and health.

#### 4.1.7.2 The problem of poverty
Absolute poverty: income too low to meet basic needs; relative poverty: income below a proportion of the median (in the UK, below 60% of median household income). Causes: unemployment, low pay, old age, disability, lone parenthood, and the benefit system. Effects: poorer health and education outcomes, reduced opportunities, social problems and a cycle of poverty passed between generations.

#### 4.1.7.3 Government policies to alleviate poverty and to influence the distribution of income and wealth
Policies: progressive taxation (a rising proportion of income paid as income rises), means-tested benefits and universal benefits, the National Minimum Wage, state provision of education and healthcare, and policies to increase employment and skills. Evaluation: disincentive effects (on work and investment), the poverty trap (withdrawal of means-tested benefits as income rises), cost, and the difficulty of targeting.

#### QS3 Understand and use the terms mean, median and relevant quantiles
Mean, median and quantiles. *Example:* ten household incomes (£000) [12, 15, 18, 22, 25, 29, 34, 41, 52, 90]: mean £33.8k, median £27k; the top decile (the richest 10%) earns £90k. The mean exceeds the median because the distribution is skewed by high incomes. Quintiles divide households into fifths; the ratio of the top quintile's income to the bottom's measures inequality: here (52 + 90)/(12 + 15) = 5.3. Relative poverty line = 60% of median = £16.2k.
