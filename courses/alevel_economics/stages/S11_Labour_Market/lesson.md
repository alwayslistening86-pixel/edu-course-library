# S11_Labour_Market - Lesson: The labour market

## Goal
The learner explains the demand for labour (marginal productivity theory) and its elasticity, the supply of labour, wage determination in perfectly and imperfectly competitive labour markets (monopsony), the influence of trade unions, the National Minimum Wage, and labour-market discrimination.

## Syllabus items taught here
- 4.1.6.1 - The demand for labour, marginal productivity theory
- 4.1.6.2 - Influences upon the supply of labour to different markets
- 4.1.6.3 - The determination of relative wage rates and levels of employment in perfectly competitive labour markets
- 4.1.6.4 - The determination of relative wage rates and levels of employment in imperfectly competitive labour markets
- 4.1.6.5 - The Influence of trade unions in determining wages and levels of employment
- 4.1.6.6 - The National Minimum Wage
- 4.1.6.7 - Discrimination in the labour market

## How to teach this
Ask why a premier-league footballer earns more than a nurse. Teach each topic as a chain of reasoning: define, explain with a diagram (described in words, then sketched by the learner), apply to a real context, then evaluate (it depends on...). Numerical examples were computed when the course was built. For current figures (Bank Rate, the National Living Wage, inflation, tax rates), check the latest official sources rather than relying on the course text.

#### 4.1.6.1 The demand for labour, marginal productivity theory
Demand for labour is a derived demand. Marginal productivity theory: firms employ workers up to where the marginal revenue product (MRP = marginal physical product x marginal revenue) equals the wage (marginal cost of labour); the MRP curve is the firm's demand curve for labour. Shifts: productivity, the price of the product, demand for the product, the prices of other factors. Elasticity of demand for labour depends on the proportion of total costs, the ease of substitution with capital, PED for the product, and time.

#### 4.1.6.2 Influences upon the supply of labour to different markets
Supply of labour to a market depends on the wage and non-wage factors (working conditions, job satisfaction, training, qualifications required, status, the wage in alternative jobs), the size of the working population, migration and taxes/benefits.

#### 4.1.6.3 The determination of relative wage rates and levels of employment in perfectly competitive labour markets
In a perfectly competitive labour market: many employers and workers, identical workers, perfect information and mobility; the wage is set by market demand and supply; each firm is a wage taker (horizontal supply of labour at the market wage) and employs where MRP = wage. Wage differentials arise from differences in demand (productivity, product demand) and supply (skills, training, unpleasantness), and from market imperfections.

#### 4.1.6.4 The determination of relative wage rates and levels of employment in imperfectly competitive labour markets
Imperfectly competitive labour markets. Monopsony: a single (or dominant) employer faces an upward-sloping supply of labour, so the marginal cost of labour (MCL) exceeds the wage; it employs where MRP = MCL, paying a wage below MRP and employing fewer workers than a competitive market. Other imperfections: immobility of labour (occupational and geographical), imperfect information, discrimination, and trade unions.

#### 4.1.6.5 The Influence of trade unions in determining wages and levels of employment
Trade unions bargain collectively for higher wages and better conditions. In a competitive labour market, a wage above equilibrium reduces employment; in a monopsony, a union (bilateral monopoly) can raise both wages and employment up to a point, by removing the monopsonist's incentive to restrict hiring. Unions may also raise productivity (voice) or cause disruption (strikes).

#### 4.1.6.6 The National Minimum Wage
The National Minimum Wage (and the National Living Wage, for workers aged 21 and over) sets a legal minimum. Effects in a competitive labour market: if above equilibrium, it may cause unemployment (excess supply of labour); in a monopsony, it can raise wages and employment. Other effects: incentives to work, productivity, poverty reduction, business costs, prices. Evidence in the UK suggests limited job losses so far. (Check current rates from official sources.)

#### 4.1.6.7 Discrimination in the labour market
Discrimination by employers, employees or customers against groups (by gender, ethnicity, age, disability) leads to lower wages and employment for those groups, a misallocation of labour, and lower output. Discrimination can be shown by an MRP curve perceived lower than actual. Wage discrimination (an employer paying different wages to different groups of workers, like price discrimination in reverse) can occur when an employer has monopsony power and groups have different elasticities of labour supply; it may benefit the employer (lower costs, higher profits) and can raise employment compared with a single monopsony wage, but it is unfair to the lower-paid group and can misallocate labour. Anti-discrimination legislation (Equality Act 2010) aims to reduce discrimination.
