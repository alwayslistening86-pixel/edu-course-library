# S22_Balance_of_Payments_Exchange_Rates_Development - Lesson: The balance of payments, exchange rates, growth and development

## Goal
The learner explains the structure of the balance of payments, the causes and consequences of current account deficits and surpluses and policies to correct them (expenditure-switching and expenditure-reducing), exchange rate systems (floating, fixed, managed), the causes and effects of exchange rate changes, and economic growth and development (indicators, barriers and policies).

## How to teach this
Ask whether a falling pound is good or bad news for the UK. Teach each topic as a chain of reasoning: define, explain with a diagram (described in words, then sketched by the learner), apply to a real context, then evaluate (it depends on...). Numerical examples were computed when the course was built. For current figures (Bank Rate, the National Living Wage, inflation, tax rates), check the latest official sources rather than relying on the course text.

#### 4.2.6.3 The balance of payments
The balance of payments records a country's transactions with the rest of the world: the current account (trade in goods, trade in services, primary income: investment income and compensation of employees, and secondary income: transfers), the capital account, and the financial account (direct investment, portfolio investment, reserves); the accounts balance overall (with net errors and omissions). Causes of a current account deficit: low competitiveness (productivity, costs, exchange rate), high domestic demand, structural factors. Consequences: depends on financing (financial account inflows), may put downward pressure on the exchange rate, may reflect a strong economy. Policies: expenditure-switching (devaluation, protectionism, supply-side improvements in competitiveness) and expenditure-reducing (contractionary fiscal or monetary policy). The Marshall-Lerner condition: a depreciation improves the current account if the sum of the absolute values of the PEDs for exports and imports exceeds 1; the J-curve: the current account may worsen first because demand is inelastic in the short run.

#### 4.2.6.4 Exchange rate systems
Exchange rate systems: floating (set by market demand and supply), fixed (maintained by the central bank through intervention and interest rates), managed (floating with intervention). Determinants of a floating rate: demand for exports, imports, interest rate differentials (hot money flows), FDI, speculation, relative inflation, confidence. Effects of a depreciation: exports cheaper, imports dearer, improving the current account (subject to Marshall-Lerner and the J-curve), boosting AD and growth, but raising inflation. Advantages and disadvantages of fixed and floating systems. Monetary union (e.g. the euro): costs (loss of independent monetary policy and exchange rate) and benefits (lower transaction costs, certainty, price transparency).

#### 4.2.6.5 Economic growth and development
Economic growth is not the same as economic development: development involves improvements in living standards and welfare (health, education, freedoms). Characteristics of less-developed economies: low incomes, high dependence on agriculture and primary products, large informal sectors, high population growth, low life expectancy and literacy. Indicators: GDP per capita (PPP), the Human Development Index (life expectancy, education, GNI per capita), and others (poverty rates, access to clean water). Barriers to growth and development: corruption, poor governance, lack of property rights (discouraging investment and borrowing against assets), lack of infrastructure, low savings and investment, primary product dependency and volatile prices, debt, conflict, poor health and education, capital flight. Strategies can be market-based (trade liberalisation, privatisation, FDI, microfinance) or interventionist (aid, infrastructure, human capital, protection of infant industries). Global imbalances: when a major economy corrects a large deficit or surplus (e.g. by cutting demand), others are affected through trade and financial flows. Policies: aid, trade liberalisation, investment in human capital and infrastructure, microfinance, FDI, debt relief, fair trade, and the role of the World Bank and IMF.
