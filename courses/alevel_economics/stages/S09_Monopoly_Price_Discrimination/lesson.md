# S09_Monopoly_Price_Discrimination - Lesson: Monopoly, price discrimination and consumer and producer surplus

## Goal
The learner explains pure monopoly and monopoly power, profit-maximising equilibrium, natural monopoly, the costs and benefits of monopoly, third-degree price discrimination and its conditions, and consumer and producer surplus with the welfare effects of monopoly and price discrimination.

## Syllabus items taught here
- 4.1.5.6 - Monopoly and monopoly power
- 4.1.5.7 - Price discrimination
- 4.1.5.11 - Consumer and producer surplus

## How to teach this
Ask why train tickets for the same journey can cost ten times more at 8 a.m. than at 11 a.m. Teach each topic as a chain of reasoning: define, explain with a diagram (described in words, then sketched by the learner), apply to a real context, then evaluate (it depends on...). Numerical examples were computed when the course was built. For current figures (Bank Rate, the National Living Wage, inflation, tax rates), check the latest official sources rather than relying on the course text.

#### 4.1.5.6 Monopoly and monopoly power
A pure monopoly is a single seller; monopoly power exists when a firm can influence price and restrict competition; UK competition authorities usually treat a share of 40% or more as possible dominance, and 25% is a threshold for investigating mergers. Sources: barriers to entry (economies of scale, legal barriers such as patents and licences, control of resources, brand loyalty, sunk costs), product differentiation, and the number of competitors. The monopolist is a price maker: profit maximised where MC = MR, with price read from AR: P > MC (allocative inefficiency), usually not at minimum AC, and supernormal profit protected by barriers. Natural monopoly: economies of scale so large that one firm can supply the market at lower cost than several (water, rail track). Benefits: economies of scale, dynamic efficiency (profits funding R&D), and ability to compete internationally; costs: higher prices, lower output, less choice, X-inefficiency. *Example:* P = 100 - Q and MC = AC = 20: MR = 100 - 2Q = 20 gives Q = 40, P = 60, profit = (60 - 20) x 40 = 1,600.

#### 4.1.5.7 Price discrimination
Price discrimination: charging different prices to different consumers for the same product for reasons not related to costs. Conditions: the firm has price-making power; it can identify groups with different PEDs; it can separate the markets (prevent resale, 'seepage'). Third-degree price discrimination: higher price in the market with less elastic demand. Effects: higher profits; some consumers pay less (and some are served who otherwise wouldn't be); consumer surplus is transferred to producers. Examples: peak and off-peak travel, student discounts.

#### 4.1.5.11 Consumer and producer surplus
Consumer surplus: the difference between what consumers are willing to pay and the price they pay (area under demand above price). Producer surplus: the difference between the price received and the minimum price sellers would accept (area above supply below price). Monopoly compared with competition reduces consumer surplus, transfers some to producers and creates a deadweight welfare loss. Price discrimination converts consumer surplus into producer surplus. *Example:* for demand P = 100 - Q at P = 60 (Q = 40), consumer surplus = 0.5 x 40 x 40 = 800; with MC = 20, competitive output would be 80, so the deadweight loss is 0.5 x (60 - 20) x (80 - 40) = 800.
