# S04_Market_Equilibrium - Lesson: Equilibrium prices and interrelated markets

## Goal
The learner explains how equilibrium price and quantity are determined, the effects of shifts in demand and supply (using diagrams), the rationing, incentive and signalling functions of prices, and the interrelationships between markets (joint demand, composite demand, derived demand and joint supply).

## Syllabus items taught here
- 4.1.3.5 - The determination of equilibrium market prices
- 4.1.3.6 - The interrelationship between markets

## How to teach this
Ask why the price of houses in a city rises when a big employer opens an office there. Teach each topic as a chain of reasoning: define, explain with a diagram (described in words, then sketched by the learner), apply to a real context, then evaluate (it depends on...). Numerical examples were computed when the course was built. For current figures (Bank Rate, the National Living Wage, inflation, tax rates), check the latest official sources rather than relying on the course text.

#### 4.1.3.5 The determination of equilibrium market prices
Equilibrium is where quantity demanded equals quantity supplied: no tendency to change. Above it there is excess supply, which pushes the price down; below it excess demand pushes it up. Shifts: an increase in demand raises price and quantity; an increase in supply lowers price and raises quantity; the size of the price and quantity changes depends on the elasticities. *Example:* Qd = 200 - 4P and Qs = 20 + 5P: equilibrium where 200 - 4P = 20 + 5P, P = 20, Q = 120. Price has rationing, incentive and signalling functions: it rations scarce goods, signals changes in demand and supply, and gives incentives for resources to move.

#### 4.1.3.6 The interrelationship between markets
Markets are interrelated. Joint demand (complements: cars and petrol); competitive demand (substitutes); composite demand (a good with several uses: milk for cheese and yoghurt: more demand for one use reduces supply for others); derived demand (demand for a factor comes from demand for the product: steel for cars, labour); joint supply (producing one good gives another: beef and leather). Use diagrams of two markets to show knock-on effects.
