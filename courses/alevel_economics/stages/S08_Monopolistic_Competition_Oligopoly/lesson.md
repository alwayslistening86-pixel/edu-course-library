# S08_Monopolistic_Competition_Oligopoly - Lesson: Monopolistic competition and oligopoly

## Goal
The learner explains monopolistic competition (short- and long-run equilibrium), the characteristics of oligopoly, interdependence, collusion (overt, tacit and cartels) and non-collusion, price wars and non-price competition, barriers to entry, and uses simple game theory to analyse oligopoly.

## Syllabus items taught here
- 4.1.5.4 - Monopolistic competition
- 4.1.5.5 - Oligopoly

## How to teach this
Ask why supermarket petrol prices tend to move together. Teach each topic as a chain of reasoning: define, explain with a diagram (described in words, then sketched by the learner), apply to a real context, then evaluate (it depends on...). Numerical examples were computed when the course was built. For current figures (Bank Rate, the National Living Wage, inflation, tax rates), check the latest official sources rather than relying on the course text.

#### 4.1.5.4 Monopolistic competition
Monopolistic competition: many firms, differentiated products (branding), low barriers to entry, some price-making power (downward-sloping demand). Short run: supernormal profit possible; long run: entry shifts each firm's demand left until AR is tangential to AC, so only normal profit; firms produce with excess capacity (not at minimum AC) and P > MC. Examples: hairdressers, restaurants.

#### 4.1.5.5 Oligopoly
Oligopoly: a few firms dominate (high concentration ratio), high barriers to entry, differentiated or homogeneous products, and interdependence: each firm's decisions depend on its rivals' reactions. Oligopoly can be defined by market structure (a high concentration ratio, barriers) or by conduct (interdependent behaviour). Collusion: overt (formal agreements, cartels, which are illegal in the UK), tacit (informal, e.g. price leadership); cooperation between firms (e.g. joint ventures, shared research or common standards) is different from collusion and can be legal and beneficial. Reasons for collusion (higher joint profits, less uncertainty) and for it breaking down (incentive to cheat, many firms, differing costs, detection by authorities). Competitive oligopoly: price wars, non-price competition (advertising, branding, loyalty schemes, quality). Price stability is illustrated by the kinked demand curve model: a firm assumes rivals will match a price cut (demand inelastic below the current price) but not a price rise (demand elastic above it), so the demand curve is kinked and MR has a vertical gap; costs can change within the gap without changing the profit-maximising price. The model is one illustration of interdependence (it doesn't explain how the original price was set). Game theory: a two-firm payoff matrix shows the prisoners' dilemma: each firm's dominant strategy (e.g. a low price) leads to an outcome worse for both than cooperation (a Nash equilibrium in which neither gains from changing strategy alone). Concentrated markets are not necessarily uncompetitive.
