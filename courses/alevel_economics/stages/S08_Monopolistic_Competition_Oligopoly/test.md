## Test items
1. Two firms, A and B, each choose a high or low price. Profits (A, B) in £m: both high (10, 10); A high, B low (4, 13); A low, B high (13, 4); both low (6, 6). Identify each firm's dominant strategy, the likely outcome, and why collusion might occur. [5 marks]
2. Explain why firms in oligopoly often use non-price competition. [3 marks]
3. Explain the long-run equilibrium of a firm in monopolistic competition and one inefficiency. [4 marks]
4. Explain two reasons why cartels may break down. [4 marks]
5. Distinguish between overt and tacit collusion. [2 marks]

## Answer key (for the tutor only)
1. [5] B1 if B is high, A earns more by going low (13 > 10); if B is low, A earns more by going low (6 > 4); B1 so low price is dominant for A (and, by symmetry, for B); B1 outcome: both low (6, 6), a Nash equilibrium; B1 both would be better off at (10, 10); B1 so they have an incentive to collude (but also to cheat on any agreement).
2. [3] B1 interdependence: a price cut may be matched by rivals; B1 leading to a price war and lower profits for all; B1 non-price competition (advertising, branding, loyalty cards) builds market share without triggering retaliation.
3. [4] B1 supernormal profits attract entry (low barriers); B1 each firm's demand curve shifts left until AR is tangential to AC; B1 normal profit only; B1 excess capacity: output below minimum AC, so productively inefficient (or P > MC).
4. [4] B1 incentive to cheat; B1 a firm can raise its own profit by secretly cutting price/raising output; B1 legal risk: competition authorities can impose large fines; B1 whistle-blowers are offered leniency (or differing costs, new entrants, falling demand).
5. [2] B1 overt: a formal, explicit agreement between firms (e.g. a cartel); B1 tacit: informal coordination without communication (e.g. price leadership).
