# S18_Inflation_and_Policy_Conflicts - Lesson: Inflation, deflation and conflicts between objectives

## Goal
The learner explains inflation, deflation and disinflation, demand-pull and cost-push causes (and the role of money supply growth and expectations), the consequences of inflation and deflation, and conflicts and trade-offs between macroeconomic objectives, including the Phillips curve.

## Syllabus items taught here
- 4.2.3.3 - Inflation and deflation
- 4.2.3.4 - Possible conflicts between macroeconomic policy objectives

## How to teach this
Ask why prices falling might be bad news for an economy. Teach each topic as a chain of reasoning: define, explain with a diagram (described in words, then sketched by the learner), apply to a real context, then evaluate (it depends on...). Numerical examples were computed when the course was built. For current figures (Bank Rate, the National Living Wage, inflation, tax rates), check the latest official sources rather than relying on the course text.

#### 4.2.3.3 Inflation and deflation
Inflation: a sustained rise in the general price level; deflation: a sustained fall; disinflation: a fall in the rate of inflation. Causes: demand-pull (AD growing faster than productive capacity), cost-push (rising costs: wages, raw materials, import prices, indirect taxes), growth of the money supply (the quantity theory of money, MV = PQ (or MV = PT): if V and Q are stable, faster money growth raises prices), and expectations (wage-price spirals). Consequences: loss of purchasing power, redistribution (from savers and lenders to borrowers), menu and shoe-leather costs, uncertainty discouraging investment, loss of international competitiveness, fiscal drag. Deflation may be benign (from productivity gains) or malign (falling AD: consumers delay spending, debt burdens rise, a deflationary spiral). Effects on real incomes and interest rates.

#### 4.2.3.4 Possible conflicts between macroeconomic policy objectives
Conflicts between objectives: growth vs inflation (rapid demand growth raises inflation), unemployment vs inflation (the short-run Phillips curve trade-off), growth vs the current account (rising incomes suck in imports), growth vs the environment, and fiscal consolidation vs growth. The short-run Phillips curve shows an inverse relationship between unemployment and inflation; the long-run Phillips curve is vertical (drawn as an L-shape near the axis in some texts, showing no long-run trade-off) at the natural rate of unemployment (the NAIRU), so in the long run there's no trade-off (expectations adjust). Policies can reduce conflicts (e.g. supply-side policies raise potential output).
