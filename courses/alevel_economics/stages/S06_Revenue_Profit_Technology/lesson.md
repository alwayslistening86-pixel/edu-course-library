# S06_Revenue_Profit_Technology - Lesson: Revenue, profit and technological change

## Goal
The learner calculates and relates total, average and marginal revenue (including for price-taking and price-making firms), explains normal, supernormal and sub-normal profit and their roles, and analyses the effects of technological change on markets, costs and invention and innovation (creative destruction).

## Syllabus items taught here
- 4.1.4.6 - Marginal, average and total revenue
- 4.1.4.7 - Profit
- 4.1.4.8 - Technological change

## How to teach this
Ask why a music streaming company can cut its prices when a record shop couldn't. Teach each topic as a chain of reasoning: define, explain with a diagram (described in words, then sketched by the learner), apply to a real context, then evaluate (it depends on...). Numerical examples were computed when the course was built. For current figures (Bank Rate, the National Living Wage, inflation, tax rates), check the latest official sources rather than relying on the course text.

#### 4.1.4.6 Marginal, average and total revenue
Total revenue TR = P x Q; average revenue AR = TR/Q = price; marginal revenue MR = change in TR from one more unit. For a price taker (perfectly elastic demand), AR = MR = price (horizontal). For a price maker facing a downward-sloping demand curve, MR < AR, falls twice as fast for a straight-line demand curve, and TR is maximised where MR = 0 (unit elasticity). *Example:* demand P = 50 - Q: TR = 50Q - Q^2, MR = 50 - 2Q; TR is maximised at Q = 25, P = 25.

#### 4.1.4.7 Profit
Profit = TR - TC. Normal profit: the minimum profit needed to keep a firm in the industry in the long run (treated as a cost: it covers opportunity cost). Supernormal (abnormal) profit: profit above normal profit, signalling entry (if barriers allow). Sub-normal profit: below normal, leading to exit in the long run. Profit is an incentive, a source of finance for investment, and a signal for resource allocation. Profit is maximised where MC = MR.

#### 4.1.4.8 Technological change
Technological change: invention (a new idea) and innovation (bringing it to market). It lowers costs, creates new products and markets, and can change market structure (e.g. online retail, streaming); it can destroy existing markets (creative destruction, Schumpeter): new firms and technologies replace old ones. Effects on productivity, costs (LRAC shifts down) and on the structure of markets.
