# S21_Globalisation_and_Trade - Lesson: Globalisation and trade

## Goal
The learner explains globalisation (causes, characteristics, costs and benefits, the role of multinationals), the gains from trade by comparative advantage (with calculations), the terms of trade, patterns of trade, trading blocs and the WTO, and the effects of protectionism (tariffs, quotas, subsidies and other barriers).

## Syllabus items taught here
- 4.2.6.1 - Globalisation
- 4.2.6.2 - Trade

## How to teach this
Ask why the UK imports bananas but also imports cars, when it makes cars itself. Teach each topic as a chain of reasoning: define, explain with a diagram (described in words, then sketched by the learner), apply to a real context, then evaluate (it depends on...). Numerical examples were computed when the course was built. For current figures (Bank Rate, the National Living Wage, inflation, tax rates), check the latest official sources rather than relying on the course text.

#### 4.2.6.1 Globalisation
Globalisation: the growing integration of the world's economies through trade, capital flows, migration and the spread of technology and culture. Causes: lower transport and communication costs, trade liberalisation, the growth of multinational corporations (MNCs), and capital market liberalisation. Consequences for developed and developing economies: cheaper goods, access to markets, growth, and technology transfer, against structural unemployment, inequality, environmental damage, and loss of economic sovereignty. The role of MNCs and transfer pricing.

#### 4.2.6.2 Trade
Absolute advantage: producing more of a good with the same resources. Comparative advantage: producing a good at a lower opportunity cost. Countries gain from specialising according to comparative advantage and trading, provided the terms of trade lie between their opportunity-cost ratios. *Example:* with the same resources, country A can make 4 cars or 2 tonnes of wheat; B can make 3 cars or 1 tonne of wheat. Opportunity cost of 1 tonne of wheat: A 2 cars, B 3 cars; so A has the comparative advantage in wheat and B in cars (1 car costs B 0.33 t of wheat vs A 0.50 t). Limitations: transport costs, assumptions of constant costs and factor mobility. Terms of trade = index of export prices ÷ index of import prices x 100; an improvement means more imports per unit of exports. Patterns of trade are changing (e.g. the rise of China and emerging economies). Trading blocs (free trade areas, customs unions, common markets, monetary unions); the Single European Market: free movement of goods, services, capital and labour among EU members, common rules and standards (the UK left in 2021) and the World Trade Organization (WTO), which promotes free trade and settles disputes. Protectionism: tariffs, quotas, export subsidies, administrative barriers and regulations. Arguments for (infant industries, strategic industries, dumping, jobs) and against (higher prices, retaliation, inefficiency, loss of gains from trade); diagrams showing a tariff's effect on consumer surplus, government revenue and deadweight losses.
