# S13_Market_Failure - Lesson: The market mechanism and market failure

## Goal
The learner explains how markets allocate resources, the meaning of market failure, public, private and quasi-public goods, externalities (using marginal private and social costs and benefits), merit and demerit goods, and market imperfections (imperfect information, monopoly, immobility).

## Syllabus items taught here
- 4.1.8.1 - How markets and prices allocate resources
- 4.1.8.2 - The meaning of market failure
- 4.1.8.3 - Public goods, private goods and quasi-public goods
- 4.1.8.4 - Positive and negative externalities in consumption and production
- 4.1.8.5 - Merit and demerit goods
- 4.1.8.6 - Market imperfections

## How to teach this
Ask why nobody builds a lighthouse to make a profit. Teach each topic as a chain of reasoning: define, explain with a diagram (described in words, then sketched by the learner), apply to a real context, then evaluate (it depends on...). Numerical examples were computed when the course was built. For current figures (Bank Rate, the National Living Wage, inflation, tax rates), check the latest official sources rather than relying on the course text.

#### 4.1.8.1 How markets and prices allocate resources
The price mechanism allocates resources through its rationing, incentive and signalling functions (S04); Adam Smith's 'invisible hand': individuals pursuing self-interest can produce outcomes that benefit society, if markets work well. Advantages of the price mechanism: decentralised decisions, incentives, efficiency, choice; disadvantages: inequality, market failures, instability. Markets can be extended into new areas (e.g. payments for blood donation, or emissions trading), raising both efficiency arguments and ethical concerns (e.g. whether paying for blood reduces altruistic giving).

#### 4.1.8.2 The meaning of market failure
Market failure occurs when the market allocates resources inefficiently (not allocatively efficient), so social welfare isn't maximised. Complete market failure: a missing market (public goods); partial: the market exists but produces the wrong quantity or price (externalities, merit/demerit goods). Also inequity (a normative view).

#### 4.1.8.3 Public goods, private goods and quasi-public goods
Private goods are rival (one person's consumption reduces availability to others) and excludable (non-payers can be excluded). Public goods are non-rival and non-excludable (street lighting, national defence): the free-rider problem means the market fails to provide them (a missing market). Quasi-public goods have some characteristics (roads: non-excludable but can become rival when congested; can be tolled). Technology can change a good's status. The tragedy of the commons: shared resources that are rival but non-excludable (fishing grounds, common grazing) are over-used, because no one owns them.

#### 4.1.8.4 Positive and negative externalities in consumption and production
Externalities are costs or benefits to third parties not reflected in the price; a key cause is the absence of well-defined property rights (no one owns clean air, so no one can charge for polluting it). Social cost = private cost + external cost; social benefit = private benefit + external benefit. Negative production externality (pollution): marginal social cost exceeds marginal private cost, so the market overproduces (at MPC = MPB rather than MSC = MSB), creating a welfare loss. Positive consumption externality (vaccination, education): MSB > MPB, so underconsumption. Negative consumption (smoking passive effects) and positive production (training by firms) externalities also occur. Diagrams show the welfare loss triangle.

#### 4.1.8.5 Merit and demerit goods
Merit goods (education, healthcare) are underconsumed if left to the market, because of positive externalities and/or imperfect information (people underestimate private benefits); demerit goods (tobacco, alcohol) are overconsumed because of negative externalities and imperfect information (underestimating private costs). Value judgements are involved in labelling goods as merit or demerit.

#### 4.1.8.6 Market imperfections
Market imperfections: imperfect and asymmetric information (S02), monopoly and monopoly power (S09), and the immobility of factors of production (occupational and geographical). They lead to a misallocation of resources.
