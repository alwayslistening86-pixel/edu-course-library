# S15_Measuring_Macro_Performance - Lesson: Macroeconomic objectives, indicators, index numbers and national income

## Goal
The learner explains the objectives of government macroeconomic policy, the main indicators (real GDP, CPI and RPI, unemployment measures, the balance of payments), constructs and interprets index numbers, converts money values to real terms, and evaluates the use of national income data to compare living standards.

## Syllabus items taught here
- 4.2.1.1 - The objectives of government economic policy
- 4.2.1.2 - Macroeconomic indicators
- 4.2.1.3 - Uses of index numbers
- 4.2.1.4 - Uses of national income data
- QS5 - Calculate and interpret index numbers
- QS7 - Make calculations to convert from money to real terms

## How to teach this
Ask whether a country with a higher GDP than the UK must have a higher standard of living. Teach each topic as a chain of reasoning: define, explain with a diagram (described in words, then sketched by the learner), apply to a real context, then evaluate (it depends on...). Numerical examples were computed when the course was built. For current figures (Bank Rate, the National Living Wage, inflation, tax rates), check the latest official sources rather than relying on the course text.

#### 4.2.1.1 The objectives of government economic policy
Objectives: economic growth (sustainable, rising real GDP), low unemployment (full employment), low and stable inflation (the UK government's target for the Bank of England is 2% CPI inflation), a satisfactory balance of payments on current account, and others: balanced government budget, a more equal distribution of income, environmental protection. Their relative importance has changed over time (e.g. from full employment in the 1950s-60s to inflation control later).

#### 4.2.1.2 Macroeconomic indicators
Indicators: real GDP and real GDP per capita (output adjusted for inflation and population); the Consumer Prices Index (CPI) and Retail Prices Index (RPI; includes mortgage interest and council tax; uses a different formula); measures of unemployment (the claimant count and the Labour Force Survey/ILO measure); the balance of payments on current account; and productivity (output per worker or per hour). Limitations of each (e.g. the claimant count excludes people not claiming benefits).

#### 4.2.1.3 Uses of index numbers
Index numbers express values relative to a base period (= 100): index = value ÷ base value x 100. The percentage change between index numbers = (new - old) ÷ old x 100 (not simply the difference in points, unless the starting index is 100). A weighted price index (such as CPI) weights price changes by their share of spending. *Example:* a basket costing £240 in the base year and £258 now: index = 107.5. An index rising from 112 to 118 is a rise of 5.4%, although the difference is 6 points.

#### 4.2.1.4 Uses of national income data
National income data (GDP, GNI) are used to assess economic performance and compare living standards over time and between countries. Limitations: inflation (use real values), population (use per capita), purchasing power parity (PPP) adjusts for different price levels, the hidden (informal) economy, non-marketed output (household work), income distribution, externalities and environmental damage, leisure, quality of goods, and the composition of output. Other measures of well-being: the Human Development Index (HDI) and happiness surveys.

#### QS5 Calculate and interpret index numbers
Calculate and interpret index numbers. *Example:* nominal wages index rises from 100 to 108 while the price index rises from 100 to 105: real wages rise by about 2.9%.

#### QS7 Make calculations to convert from money to real terms
Convert money (nominal) values to real terms: real value = nominal value ÷ price index x 100. *Example:* nominal GDP rises from £2,000bn to £2,200bn while the price index rises from 100 to 106: real GDP in base-year prices = 2,200/1.06 = £2,075bn, real growth = 3.8%.
