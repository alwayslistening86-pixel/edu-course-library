# S30_Nazi_Racial_Policy_and_Persecution - Lesson: Weimar and Nazi Germany: racial ideology and the persecution of minorities and Jewish people

## Goal
The learner can describe Nazi racial beliefs and treatment of Slavs, Roma and Sinti, homosexuals and disabled people, narrate escalating persecution of Jewish people from 1933 to Kristallnacht, analyse interpretations of Nazi control and life in Nazi Germany, and answer the Paper 3 inference question.

## How to teach this
Open with: 'Nazi racial ideology did not target only Jewish people, though they were its central target. Who else was persecuted, and how did persecution of Jewish people escalate step by step across the 1930s?'

#### 31.4.4a Life in Nazi Germany: racial beliefs and treatment of Slavs, Roma and Sinti, homosexuals, disabled people
Nazi ideology held a pseudo-scientific racial hierarchy with 'Aryans' (idealised as Germanic, blond, blue-eyed) at the top, and treated other groups as inferior or dangerous to racial purity. Slavic peoples (Poles, Russians and others) were regarded as racially inferior, intended for subjugation and exploitation, especially once war began. Roma and Sinti people (sometimes referred to historically as 'Gypsies') faced escalating discrimination, registration, forced sterilisation and, from the war years, mass deportation and murder in the same camp system used against Jewish people. Homosexual men were persecuted under a strengthened version of an existing law criminalising homosexuality, facing arrest, imprisonment and, for many, incarceration in concentration camps. Disabled people, deemed a genetic and financial burden inconsistent with racial 'purity', suffered forced sterilisation from 1933 and, from 1939, mass murder through the secret 'T4' euthanasia programme.

#### 31.4.4b Life in Nazi Germany: persecution of Jewish people - 1933 boycott, Nuremberg Laws, Kristallnacht
Persecution of Jewish people escalated steadily. A one-day national boycott of Jewish-owned businesses took place in April 1933, followed by exclusion of Jews from the civil service and many professions that same year. The Nuremberg Laws (1935) stripped Jews of German citizenship and banned marriage or sexual relations between Jews and other Germans, giving racial persecution a formal legal basis and defining who counted as Jewish by ancestry. Kristallnacht ('Night of Broken Glass', 9-10 November 1938), triggered by the assassination of a German diplomat by a young Jewish man in Paris, saw coordinated Nazi-organised attacks across Germany: synagogues burned, Jewish shops and homes smashed and looted, around 30,000 Jewish men arrested and sent to concentration camps, and dozens killed, marking a decisive shift from legal and economic discrimination towards open, organised violence.

#### 31.INT2 Historians' interpretations: Nazi control, consent and coercion, life in Nazi Germany (Gellately, Kershaw, Mommsen, Overy, Stargardt)
Historians differ on how the Nazi regime actually functioned and secured obedience. Robert Gellately has argued that widespread ordinary German participation, including denunciations to the Gestapo by neighbours and colleagues, meant the regime relied heavily on active popular cooperation, not just top-down terror. Ian Kershaw's influential concept of 'working towards the Führer' suggests officials at all levels anticipated and enacted what they believed Hitler wanted, radicalising policy (including racial persecution) without always needing direct orders, reflecting a more chaotic, competitive structure than a perfectly organised dictatorship. Hans Mommsen similarly emphasised structural rivalry and improvisation within the Nazi state ('polycracy') rather than a single all-controlling will. Richard Overy and Nicholas Stargardt have explored how genuine belief, fear and self-interest combined differently across German society and over time, cautioning against any single simple explanation of Nazi control.

#### Q-P3-1 Paper 3 Q1: what can you infer from Source A
Q1 on Paper 3 is a 4-mark AO3 inference question, giving one contemporary source (often connected to Weimar or Nazi Germany, such as a poster, photograph, or extract) and asking what can be inferred from it about a stated topic, for example 'What can you infer from Source A about Nazi propaganda aimed at young people?' Technique: make an inference (a reasonable conclusion beyond the literal content), then support it directly with specific detail quoted or closely paraphrased from the source, doing this twice for full marks. A common error is simply describing or summarising the source's content without stating what it implies or suggests, which does not earn inference marks; another is inferring something the source does not actually support.
