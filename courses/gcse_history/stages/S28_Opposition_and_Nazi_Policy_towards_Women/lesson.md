# S28_Opposition_and_Nazi_Policy_towards_Women - Lesson: Weimar and Nazi Germany: opposition to the regime and Nazi policy towards women

## Goal
The learner can describe Church opposition and Pastor Niemoeller, describe youth opposition movements, and explain Nazi ideology and policy towards women, marriage, family and employment.

## Syllabus items taught here
- 31.3.4b - Nazi control: opposition from the Churches, Pastor Niemoeller: Describe Church opposition
- 31.3.4c - Nazi control: opposition from the young - Swing Youth and Edelweiss Pirates: Describe youth opposition
- 31.4.1a - Life in Nazi Germany: Nazi views on women and the family: Explain Nazi ideology on women
- 31.4.1b - Life in Nazi Germany: policies towards women - marriage, family, employment, appearance: Describe policies and their effects

## How to teach this
Open with: 'Even in a dictatorship that used terror and propaganda together, some people still refused to conform. Who resisted, and how much could they actually achieve?'

#### 31.3.4b Nazi control: opposition from the Churches, Pastor Niemoeller
Despite the Concordat and the Reich Church, some Christians resisted Nazi interference. The Confessing Church, formed in 1934 by Protestant clergy opposed to state control and the 'German Christian' movement's blending of Nazism with faith, tried to maintain independent Christian teaching. Pastor Martin Niemöller, an early prominent member, publicly criticised Nazi religious policy and was arrested in 1937, spending years in concentration camps until liberation in 1945; he is remembered partly for a post-war reflection on the danger of failing to speak out against persecution of others before it reached oneself. Catholic opposition included occasional individual clergy criticism, notably Bishop von Galen's 1941 sermons condemning the euthanasia programme, though the Church hierarchy generally avoided open, sustained confrontation with the regime as a whole, limiting the overall scale of Church-based opposition.

#### 31.3.4c Nazi control: opposition from the young - Swing Youth and Edelweiss Pirates
Not all young Germans conformed to the compulsory Hitler Youth movement. The Swing Youth, mostly middle-class urban teenagers from the later 1930s, rejected Nazi conformity through fashion, socialising and enthusiasm for banned American and British swing jazz music and dancing, expressing cultural rather than overtly political dissent, though the regime still viewed and punished it as subversive. The Edelweiss Pirates, working-class youth groups active especially in western Germany from the late 1930s, more directly rejected Hitler Youth discipline, roamed and camped independently, sometimes clashed physically with Hitler Youth patrols, and in some cases moved towards more active resistance, including helping deserters, during the war. Both movements show that Nazi control of youth, though extensive through the Hitler Youth, was never total.

#### 31.4.1a Life in Nazi Germany: Nazi views on women and the family
Nazi ideology held rigid, traditional views on women's role, summarised in the phrase 'Kinder, Küche, Kirche' (children, kitchen, church), rejecting the Weimar-era 'new woman' image of independence, career and changing fashion as a symptom of national decline. Women were idealised primarily as wives and mothers, responsible for the home and, crucially, for producing racially 'pure' children to strengthen the nation, reflecting the regime's broader racial and population goals. This ideology directly reversed several Weimar-era trends towards greater female participation in public and professional life, treating women's domestic role as a patriotic duty essential to national and racial renewal rather than as a matter of individual choice.

#### 31.4.1b Life in Nazi Germany: policies towards women - marriage, family, employment, appearance
Policy reinforced this ideology concretely. The Law for the Encouragement of Marriage (1933) offered loans to newly married couples, part of which was cancelled for each child born, directly incentivising larger families. The Mother's Cross award (from 1939) honoured women with large numbers of children. Women were discouraged or barred from many professions and higher education places, particularly in law and medicine, and married women were often pushed out of paid employment, especially in the earlier 1930s, to free jobs for men amid continuing economic concern, though labour shortages from rearmament later in the decade complicated this and drew some women back into work. Approved appearance meant plain, traditional dress and natural hairstyles, rejecting makeup, trousers and the fashionable, cosmopolitan Weimar 'new woman' look as un-German.
