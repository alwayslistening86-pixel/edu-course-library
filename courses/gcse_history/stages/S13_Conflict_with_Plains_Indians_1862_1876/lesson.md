# S13_Conflict_with_Plains_Indians_1862_1876 - Lesson: The American West: government policy and conflict with Plains Indians, c1862-c1876

## Goal
The learner can explain the impact of railroads, cattle and mining on Plains Indians, describe government reservation policy and the second Fort Laramie Treaty, and narrate and assess the significance of Little Crow's War, Sand Creek and Red Cloud's War, and answer a Paper 2 consequence question.

## Syllabus items taught here
- P3.2.3a - American West: impact of railroads, cattle industry and gold prospecting on Plains Indians: Explain the impact on Plains peoples
- P3.2.3b - American West: government policy, reservations, second Fort Laramie Treaty 1868: Explain reservation policy and 1868
- P3.2.3c - American West: conflict - Little Crow's War, Sand Creek, Red Cloud's War: Narrate the wars and assess their significance
- Q-P2P-1 - Paper 2 period study Q1: explain two consequences: Answer the consequence question

## How to teach this
Open with: 'The railroad, the rancher and the miner never fired a shot at each other, yet all three helped destroy the Plains Indian way of life. How can that be true?' Build the answer through the stage.

#### P3.2.3a American West: impact of railroads, cattle industry and gold prospecting on Plains Indians
Railroads split hunting grounds, brought armed hunters who shot buffalo from train windows for sport and to feed railway workers, and carried ever more settlers directly through tribal land. The expanding cattle industry took over grazing land that buffalo and tribes depended on, and cattle competed with, and sometimes displaced, the buffalo herds. Gold prospecting, especially after discoveries in Colorado (1858-59) and later in the Black Hills of the Dakotas (technically Sioux treaty land), brought waves of miners onto or across land promised to tribes, provoking anger and violent clashes, and the US Army often struggled or declined to keep prospectors out. All three developments, driven by white economic interest rather than Indian consent, steadily narrowed the land and resources available for the Plains way of life, deepening tension that had been building since the 1850s.

#### P3.2.3b American West: government policy, reservations, second Fort Laramie Treaty 1868
As earlier treaties failed to prevent conflict, government policy shifted more firmly towards concentrating tribes on defined reservations, intending to free the rest of the land for settlement, railroads and ranching. The second Fort Laramie Treaty (1868), following the Sioux's successful resistance in Red Cloud's War, established the Great Sioux Reservation (including the Black Hills) as Sioux land, and the government agreed to abandon forts along the Bozeman Trail. However, the treaty was undermined within years, especially once gold was found in the Black Hills in the 1870s, showing that government promises depended on white economic interests rather than firm commitment, and reservation policy generally aimed at ending, not protecting, the traditional Plains way of life.

#### P3.2.3c American West: conflict - Little Crow's War, Sand Creek, Red Cloud's War
Little Crow's War (the Dakota War, 1862) broke out in Minnesota after broken treaty payments, corrupt traders and hunger drove the Dakota Sioux, led by Little Crow, to attack settlers; the US Army crushed the uprising and 38 Dakota men were hanged in the largest mass execution in US history. The Sand Creek Massacre (1864) saw Colorado militia under Colonel John Chivington attack a peaceful, flag-flying Cheyenne and Arapaho camp under Black Kettle, killing around 150, mostly women and children, provoking national outrage and further conflict once news spread. Red Cloud's War (1866-68), fought by the Sioux under Red Cloud to defend hunting grounds along the Bozeman Trail, including the destruction of Captain Fetterman's entire command in 1866, was the rare case of a Plains tribe forcing the US government to abandon forts and negotiate favourable terms in the 1868 treaty.

#### Q-P2P-1 Paper 2 period study Q1: explain two consequences
Q1 on the American West period study is a 4-mark AO2 question asking the learner to explain two consequences of a named event or development (for example 'Explain two consequences of the discovery of gold in the Black Hills in the 1870s'). Technique: state each consequence clearly and explain, with 'so' or 'as a result', what followed from it and why that matters, rather than simply describing the original event again. Two fully explained consequences, each with a clear causal link stated, score more highly than several vague or merely descriptive statements; specific detail (a date, a named individual, a treaty) strengthens each point.
