# S05_Early_Modern_Enforcement_Punishment_and_Case_Studies - Lesson: Early modern enforcement and punishment: the Gunpowder Plotters and Matthew Hopkins

## Goal
The learner can describe town watchmen, the continuing use of corporal and capital punishment, transportation and the Bloody Code, and can narrate the Gunpowder Plot and the Hopkins witch-hunts with reasons and punishments.

## Syllabus items taught here
- 10.2.2a - Law enforcement in early modern England, including town watchmen: Describe local enforcement and its limits
- 10.2.2b - Corporal and capital punishment; transportation begins; the start of the Bloody Code: Explain punishment changes and the Bloody Code's origins
- 10.2.3a - Case study: the Gunpowder Plotters, 1605 - crimes and punishment: Narrate the plot, arrests and executions
- 10.2.3b - Case study: Matthew Hopkins and the witch-hunts of 1645-47: Explain the intensity of the hunts and the punishment of the convicted

## How to teach this
Open with: 'What do the Gunpowder Plot and the Hopkins witch-hunts tell us about how the authorities in 1600s England saw crime?' Then ask the learner to name one thing that had not changed since 1200 (community policing, hanging) and one that had.

#### 10.2.2a Law enforcement in early modern England, including town watchmen
Enforcement remained local. In villages, parish constables (unpaid, annually chosen) still raised the hue and cry and held suspects for Justices of the Peace, who increased in power under the Tudors. In towns and cities, watchmen patrolled at night (they carried lanterns and staves, called the hours and locked up drunks) and were chosen by householders or paid small sums. The watch was often elderly or unwilling and could be ignored. There were no detectives; victims did much of the work, and thief-takers who were paid rewards appeared in London later. The Church continued to be involved through churchwardens and church courts. Continuity from the medieval period is strong, while towns needed more organised night watches as they grew, but enforcement was inefficient.

#### 10.2.2b Corporal and capital punishment; the introduction of transportation and the start of the Bloody Code
Punishments were still public, corporal and capital. Whipping (vagrants were whipped from town to town), branding, stocks, the pillory and ducking stool were used; hanging remained the punishment for serious crime, burning for heresy and for women convicted of treason, and hanging, drawing and quartering for male traitors. Houses of correction (Bridewell, London, 1553) set the idle poor to work, an early sign of a new idea, but they were few. Transportation was introduced in the early seventeenth century (banishment to the colonies is allowed by the 1597 Vagrancy Act, with convicts sent to Virginia from the 1610s). The Bloody Code was beginning: as the propertied classes feared crime, more offences became capital, from about 50 in 1688 rising to over 200 by 1800.

#### 10.2.3a Case study: the Gunpowder Plotters, 1605 - crimes and punishment
A group of Catholic gentry led by Robert Catesby, angered when James I did not end fines and persecution of Catholics, planned to blow up the House of Lords at the State Opening of Parliament on 5 November 1605, killing the king and his ministers. Thomas Percy rented a cellar under the Lords; Guy Fawkes, an experienced soldier, guarded about 36 barrels of gunpowder. Lord Monteagle received an anonymous warning letter on 26 October (Francis Tresham is often suspected). Fawkes was arrested on the night of 4-5 November, tortured on the king's orders and confessed. Catesby, Percy and others were killed at Holbeach House on 8 November. Eight surviving plotters were tried for treason in January 1606 and hanged, drawn and quartered; Fawkes fell from the scaffold and broke his neck. Anti-Catholic laws tightened.

#### 10.2.3b Case study: Matthew Hopkins and the witch-hunts of 1645-47
Matthew Hopkins, from Manningtree in Essex, styled himself 'Witchfinder General' and with John Stearne hunted witches in East Anglia in 1645-47. In two years about 250 people were accused and around 100 hanged, more than in the previous century. He used swimming (bound suspects thrown into water; floating meant guilt), pricking for the witch's mark, sleep deprivation ('watching') and questioning about familiars, which produced confessions. Reasons for the intensity: the Civil War (1642-51) disrupted courts and local government; East Anglia was strongly Puritan and afraid of the devil; Hopkins charged fees; and torture was officially illegal but the methods evaded limits. Convicted witches were hanged in England (not burned). The trials ended after 1647 as complaints grew and Hopkins died.
