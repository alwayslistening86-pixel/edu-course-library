# S04_Medieval_Enforcement_Church_and_Early_Modern_Crime - Lesson: Medieval law enforcement, the Church and early modern definitions of crime

## Goal
The learner can explain medieval community policing and punishments, the Church's role (Sanctuary, Benefit of Clergy, trial by ordeal), and how crime and its definitions changed in early modern England.

## Syllabus items taught here
- 10.1.2a - Law enforcement in Anglo-Saxon, Norman and later medieval England: tithings, hue and cry, parish constable: Describe community-based policing and the role of authorities
- 10.1.2b - Deterrence and retribution: fines, corporal and capital punishment; the Saxon Wergild: Explain medieval punishments and the use and end of Wergild
- 10.1.3 - Case study: the Church, Sanctuary, Benefit of Clergy and trial by ordeal (early thirteenth century): Explain the Church's influence and why the ordeal ended
- 10.2.1a - Continuity and change in crimes against the person, property and authority, including heresy and treason: Show continuity with medieval crime and new significance of heresy and treason
- 10.2.1b - New definitions of crime in the sixteenth century: vagabondage and witchcraft: Explain why vagabondage and witchcraft became crimes

## How to teach this
Open with: 'You are a peasant in 1150 and your neighbour steals your pig. There is no police force. What do you do?' Build the answer from the hue and cry and tithing, then ask what the punishment would be, then ask how different it would be if the thief were a priest.

#### 10.1.2a Law enforcement in Anglo-Saxon, Norman and later medieval England: tithings, hue and cry, parish constable
There was no police force. Communities policed themselves. In Anglo-Saxon and Norman England every man over twelve belonged to a tithing (a group of ten households) responsible for each other's behaviour; if a member committed a crime, the others had to produce him for trial or be fined. If a crime was witnessed, the hue and cry was raised: everyone who heard it had to chase the suspect and could be fined for refusing. The shire reeve (sheriff) represented the king in each county and held suspects; from 1285 the Statute of Winchester regularised watchmen in towns and the parish constable, an unpaid part-time local man appointed annually. Justices of the Peace, appointed from the fourteenth century, tried lesser offences. Enforcement was communal, cheap and depended on cooperation.

#### 10.1.2b Deterrence and retribution: fines, corporal and capital punishment; the Saxon Wergild
Medieval punishment emphasised deterrence (frightening others) and retribution (making the criminal suffer), and most punishments were public. Fines paid to the crown or lord were the most common. Corporal (bodily) punishments included whipping, the stocks and pillory, branding and mutilation such as cutting off a hand for theft or forgery. Capital punishment, usually hanging (nobles were beheaded; traitors hanged, drawn and quartered; women who committed treason burned) was used for serious crimes such as murder and major theft. Before 1066 the Saxon Wergild (man-price) required compensation paid by a killer's family to the victim's family, its size depending on the status of the victim. The Normans replaced it with fines to the king and harsher physical punishments, so Wergild ended.

#### 10.1.3 Case study: the Church, Sanctuary, Benefit of Clergy and trial by ordeal (early thirteenth century)
The Church shaped medieval law. Sanctuary: a criminal who reached a church could claim safety for forty days, after which he could stand trial or confess and abjure the realm, leaving England by a fixed route to a port. Benefit of Clergy: clergy could be tried in Church courts, which were more lenient and could not impose death; later anyone who could read (often by reciting Psalm 51, the 'neck verse') could claim it. Henry II's 1164 Constitutions of Clarendon and the murder of Archbishop Becket in 1170 showed the conflict between Church and Crown. Trial by ordeal was used when there was no clear evidence: hot iron or hot water (a healed wound after three days meant innocence) and cold water (sinking meant innocence). It ended after 1215 when Pope Innocent III forbade priests to take part; juries replaced it.

#### 10.2.1a Continuity and change in crimes against the person, property and authority, including heresy and treason
Most crime in 1500-1700 continued from the medieval period: theft, murder, assault and poaching. But the Reformation changed the crimes against authority. Henry VIII's break with Rome (Act of Supremacy 1534) made denying royal supremacy treason: Thomas More was executed in 1535. Heresy meant the religion of the ruler could change overnight: under Mary I (1553-58) about 280 Protestants were burned, including Cranmer, Latimer and Ridley, and under Elizabeth I Catholic priests were executed as traitors. Treason plots continued, culminating in the Gunpowder Plot of 1605. Theft rose in a time of population growth, inflation and poverty. Compare: religious crimes were about authority and fear of rebellion, not simply belief.

#### 10.2.1b New definitions of crime in the sixteenth century: vagabondage and witchcraft
The specification limits new definitions to two examples. Vagabondage: rising population, inflation, enclosure of common land, unemployment and the end of monastery charity (Dissolution 1536-40) created many wandering poor. Statutes hardened: 1547 allowed vagrants to be enslaved and branded; 1572 whipping and burning through the ear; 1597 allowed banishment. It was punished because authorities feared disorder and disease. Witchcraft: Acts of 1542 (repealed 1547), 1563 (Elizabeth) and 1604 (James I) made harmful witchcraft a crime; James I wrote Daemonologie (1597). The Reformation, fear of the devil, disease, bad harvests and disputes with neighbours produced accusations, often against poor older women. Both crimes show attitudes and government shaping what counted as crime.
