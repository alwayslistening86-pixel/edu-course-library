# S20_Elizabethan_Society_Education_and_Poverty - Lesson: Early Elizabethan England: education, leisure and the problem of poverty

## Goal
The learner can describe Elizabethan education in the home and schools, describe sport, pastimes and the theatre, explain the causes of rising poverty and vagabondage, and trace the change in policy from punishment towards provision for the poor.

## Syllabus items taught here
- B4.3.1a - Elizabeth: education in the home and schools: Describe Elizabethan education
- B4.3.1b - Elizabeth: sport, pastimes and the theatre: Describe leisure
- B4.3.2a - Elizabeth: reasons for the increase in poverty and vagabondage: Explain the causes of poverty
- B4.3.2b - Elizabeth: changing attitudes and policies towards the poor: Trace policy from punishment to provision

## How to teach this
Open with: 'If you were born poor in Elizabethan England, your chances in life depended enormously on whether you were seen as "deserving" or "undeserving". What do you think decided which you were?'

#### B4.3.1a Elizabeth: education in the home and schools
Education reflected and reinforced social hierarchy and gender roles. Most girls, especially poorer girls, were educated at home in domestic skills, religion and basic literacy if any, while wealthier girls might receive tutoring in languages, music and needlework, aimed at preparing them for marriage and household management rather than public life. Boys from wealthier families attended petty schools for basic reading, then grammar schools (many founded or re-endowed in this period) for Latin, classical texts, religious instruction and rhetoric, preparing some for university (Oxford or Cambridge) or the professions. Poor children usually received little or no formal schooling, instead beginning work early, so literacy and educational opportunity closely tracked social class, and education overall aimed to produce obedient, godly and socially fitting adults rather than to challenge the existing order.

#### B4.3.1b Elizabeth: sport, pastimes and the theatre
Leisure varied sharply by class. The wealthy enjoyed hunting, hawking, real tennis, dancing and elaborate entertainments; ordinary people enjoyed football (a rough, often violent village game), archery (still encouraged for practical defence reasons), animal-baiting (bear and bull baiting) and drinking in alehouses. The theatre grew rapidly in popularity across all classes during Elizabeth's reign: London's first purpose-built public playhouses (such as The Theatre, 1576, and later the Globe) drew large, mixed audiences, and playwrights, most famously William Shakespeare, flourished with royal and noble patronage. Puritans strongly criticised theatre and many popular pastimes as immoral, idle or disorderly, creating ongoing tension between a lively popular culture and calls for stricter moral and religious discipline.

#### B4.3.2a Elizabeth: reasons for the increase in poverty and vagabondage
Several factors combined to increase poverty across Elizabeth's reign. Population growth (rising from around three million) increased competition for land, work and food, pushing up prices faster than wages (price inflation, partly caused also by imported American silver). Enclosure of common land by landowners for more profitable sheep farming reduced land available to small farmers and labourers, and harvest failures in some years caused sharp local food shortages. The Dissolution of the Monasteries under Henry VIII had already removed a major source of charitable relief for the poor. Rising rents, periodic unemployment (including from a struggling cloth trade at times) and the general instability of casual and seasonal work left many with no reliable income, some of whom became 'vagabonds', travelling in search of work or, in a minority of cases, resorting to begging or crime.

#### B4.3.2b Elizabeth: changing attitudes and policies towards the poor
Early Tudor policy largely punished vagrancy harshly, treating the poor as a public order problem rather than a social one (as covered in the crime and punishment option, the 1547 and later Vagrancy Acts allowed severe punishment). Across Elizabeth's reign, attitudes and policy gradually shifted towards distinguishing the 'deserving poor' (the old, sick, or genuinely unable to work, deserving support) from the 'undeserving poor' (those seen as able but unwilling to work, still liable to punishment). Local parishes were increasingly required to levy a compulsory poor rate to fund relief, formalised nationally by the Poor Law Act of 1601, which established parish-based relief, workhouses for some, and continued punishment for able-bodied vagrants, marking a significant, though incomplete, move from pure deterrence towards organised, if minimal, provision.
