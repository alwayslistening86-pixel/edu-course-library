# S27_Nazi_Propaganda_Culture_and_the_Churches - Lesson: Weimar and Nazi Germany: propaganda, culture and control of the Churches

## Goal
The learner can explain Goebbels' propaganda methods, describe Nazi control of culture and the arts, explain Nazi policy towards the Churches, and assess the extent of popular support for the regime.

## Syllabus items taught here
- 31.3.3a - Nazi control: Goebbels and the Ministry of Propaganda - censorship, media, rallies, sport, Berlin Olympics: Explain propaganda methods
- 31.3.3b - Nazi control: culture and the arts - art, architecture, literature, film: Describe Nazi cultural control
- 31.3.3c - Nazi control: the Churches - Concordat and Reich Church: Explain Nazi policy towards the Churches
- 31.3.4a - Nazi control: the extent of support for the regime: Assess consent and coercion

## How to teach this
Open with: 'A regime can control people by force, or by making them want to obey. Which do you think the Nazis relied on more: fear, or persuasion?' Keep this balance in view across the stage.

#### 31.3.3a Nazi control: Goebbels and the Ministry of Propaganda - censorship, media, rallies, sport, Berlin Olympics
Joseph Goebbels headed the Reich Ministry of Public Enlightenment and Propaganda from 1933, controlling newspapers, radio and film to promote Nazi ideology and the leader cult around Hitler while suppressing dissenting views through strict censorship; cheap, mass-produced 'People's Receiver' radios spread Nazi broadcasts widely. Huge, carefully staged rallies, especially the annual Nuremberg Rallies, used mass choreography, lighting and symbolism to project unity, strength and Hitler's personal authority. Sport was used similarly: the 1936 Berlin Olympics, though briefly complicated by African American athlete Jesse Owens' multiple gold medals undermining Nazi racial claims, was staged as a major propaganda showcase of a modern, disciplined, welcoming Germany, temporarily toning down open antisemitic signage for the benefit of foreign visitors.

#### 31.3.3b Nazi control: culture and the arts - art, architecture, literature, film
The Nazis enforced strict ideological conformity in culture, promoting an approved style, broadly traditional, heroic and nationalist, glorifying Aryan racial ideals, rural life, family and the state, while condemning modernist, abstract or Jewish-associated work as 'degenerate art' (Entartete Kunst), notoriously displayed in a 1937 exhibition designed to mock and discredit it. Architecture, overseen partly by Albert Speer, favoured grand, monumental neoclassical styles intended to convey permanence and power. Books judged un-German, Jewish-authored or politically hostile were publicly burned in organised events from May 1933, and literature and film were closely censored and directed towards approved propaganda themes, including overt antisemitic material such as the film 'Jud Süss' (1940). Culture became a tool of ideological control rather than independent creative expression.

#### 31.3.3c Nazi control: the Churches - Concordat and Reich Church
Hitler initially sought accommodation with the powerful Christian Churches rather than open confrontation. The Concordat (1933) with the Catholic Church guaranteed the Church's institutional and religious freedoms in Germany in return for the Church staying out of political opposition; in practice the Nazis frequently violated its terms once signed, closing Catholic schools and organisations over time. For Protestants, the regime encouraged a unified, Nazi-sympathetic 'Reich Church' (from 1933) under 'German Christians' who blended Nazi ideology with Protestant worship, aiming to bring the many separate Protestant regional churches under centralised, pro-Nazi control and reduce the Churches' independent moral authority as a potential source of opposition.

#### 31.3.4a Nazi control: the extent of support for the regime
Historians debate how far Nazi rule rested on genuine popular support (consent) versus fear and coercion. Economic recovery, national pride restored after Versailles, and effective propaganda won real, substantial support from many ordinary Germans, especially once unemployment fell sharply after 1933. Yet coercion was also constant and severe: the Gestapo, informers, concentration camps and a controlled legal system punished dissent harshly, and public conformity may have masked private doubt that people felt unsafe expressing. A balanced view, associated with historians who studied both propaganda's reach and its limits, holds that most Germans neither actively resisted nor were purely terrorised, but combined genuine approval of some policies (economic recovery, national revival) with fear-driven silence about others (persecution, loss of freedom), producing widespread outward conformity from mixed motives.
