# S09_Whitechapel_Sources_and_Enquiry_Skills - Lesson: Whitechapel enquiries: using historical sources

## Goal
The learner can name local and national sources useful for a Whitechapel enquiry, evaluate the strengths and weaknesses of different source types, frame focused enquiry questions, and select appropriate sources for a given historical enquiry.

## Syllabus items taught here
- 10.W2a - Whitechapel enquiries: knowledge of local sources: Name and use local sources for Whitechapel enquiries
- 10.W2b - Whitechapel enquiries: knowledge of national sources: Name and use national sources for Whitechapel enquiries
- 10.W2c - Whitechapel enquiries: strengths and weaknesses of different types of source: Evaluate source types for a specific enquiry
- 10.W2d - Whitechapel enquiries: framing questions for a specific enquiry: Frame enquiry questions
- 10.W2e - Whitechapel enquiries: selecting appropriate sources: Select the right sources for an investigation

## How to teach this
Open with: 'If you wanted to find out how the police in Whitechapel actually worked day to day, would you trust a newspaper report, a police notebook, or a photograph most? Why?' Use the answer to launch a discussion of source types.

#### 10.W2a Whitechapel enquiries: knowledge of local sources
Local sources specific to Whitechapel include: the Metropolitan Police's own files and case papers on the Ripper murders (now held at the National Archives), local newspaper reports (for example the East London Observer), photographs of streets, courts and buildings (some taken by social reformers), and maps such as those made by Charles Booth's poverty survey, which colour-coded streets by wealth and criminality in the 1880s-90s. Workhouse and hospital records, coroners' inquest reports into the murder victims, and the records or memoirs of organisations such as the Salvation Army, active in the East End, also count as local sources. Learners should be able to name several of these and say what each could show about crime, poverty or policing in Whitechapel specifically, rather than Victorian Britain generally.

#### 10.W2b Whitechapel enquiries: knowledge of national sources
National sources place Whitechapel in a wider context: national newspapers (The Times, Punch) covered the case and reveal national opinion and press sensationalism; Home Office correspondence and Metropolitan Police annual reports reveal official policy and resources; parliamentary papers and debates show political responses; and government statistics on crime, poverty and population (including the national census) allow comparison between Whitechapel and the rest of London or the country. National Vigilance Committee records and the writings of social investigators such as Charles Booth (whose 'Life and Labour of the People in London' surveyed poverty nationally as well as locally) also count. Learners should distinguish a national source, useful for context and comparison, from a local one, useful for detail specific to Whitechapel.

#### 10.W2c Whitechapel enquiries: strengths and weaknesses of different types of source
Each source type has different value. Newspapers are widely available and show contemporary opinion and sensational detail, but were often exaggerated, inaccurate or driven by a need to sell copies, so are weak for establishing plain fact. Police and official records are usually more reliable for fact (arrests, statements, procedure) but can be incomplete, selective or written to protect the police's own reputation. Photographs show physical reality (streets, buildings) directly but rarely show process or explain causes, and were sometimes staged. Personal accounts and memoirs give vivid detail and viewpoint but rely on memory and personal bias. Statistics (census, crime figures) allow scale and comparison but can undercount hidden crime such as domestic violence or unreported theft. Good enquiry work always weighs a source's nature, origin and purpose against what is being asked.

#### 10.W2d Whitechapel enquiries: framing questions for a specific enquiry
A good historical enquiry question is specific, answerable from available evidence, and clearly connected to a stated purpose, not a vague topic label. Weak: 'What was Whitechapel like?' Better: 'How effectively did H Division police the streets of Whitechapel in 1888?' or 'What can newspaper coverage of the Ripper murders tell us about Victorian attitudes to the poor?' Framing a question involves stating the focus (policing, poverty, press reaction), the period, and, ideally, the type of detail wanted (an aspect of daily life, an attitude, a process). In the Paper 1 follow-up question, the learner must also name a further detail they would like to find out and suggest what type of source might supply it, and explain why that source type would help answer that specific question.

#### 10.W2e Whitechapel enquiries: selecting appropriate sources
Once a question is framed, the historian selects sources fit for purpose. An enquiry into daily policing methods should prioritise police records, beat logs or officers' own accounts over sensational press reports. An enquiry into public opinion or fear should prioritise newspapers, letters to the press, and Vigilance Committee records. An enquiry into living conditions should prioritise Booth's poverty maps, housing surveys and photographs. Learners should practise justifying a choice by naming the source type, saying what it would likely show, and explaining why it suits that particular question better than an alternative, always tying the choice back to nature, origin and purpose rather than simply 'it is useful'. This selection skill underpins Paper 1 Q2(b), which specifically credits sensible, justified choices of further source and detail.
