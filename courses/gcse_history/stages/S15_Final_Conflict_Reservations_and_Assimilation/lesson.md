# S15_Final_Conflict_Reservations_and_Assimilation - Lesson: The American West: the buffalo, Little Big Horn, Wounded Knee and reservation policy, c1876-c1895

## Goal
The learner can explain the destruction of the buffalo, narrate Little Big Horn and Wounded Knee, describe reservation life, explain the Dawes Act's assimilation policy, and answer Paper 2 narrative and importance questions.

## How to teach this
Open with: 'In 1830 there were tens of millions of buffalo. By 1895 there were a few hundred. What does that number alone tell you about what happened to the Plains Indians?'

#### P3.3.2c American West: Little Big Horn 1876 and Wounded Knee 1890
At the Battle of the Little Bighorn (25 June 1876), Sioux and Cheyenne warriors led by Sitting Bull and Crazy Horse, resisting government demands to move onto reservations after gold was found in the Black Hills, destroyed Lieutenant Colonel George Custer's detachment of the US 7th Cavalry, killing Custer and around 260 men; it was the Plains Indians' greatest single victory but provoked a massive US Army response that crushed remaining resistance within a year. The Wounded Knee Massacre (29 December 1890), the effective end of Plains Indian armed resistance, occurred when the 7th Cavalry, disarming a group of Sioux (including followers of the Ghost Dance movement, which promised the return of the old way of life) at Wounded Knee Creek, opened fire after a confused struggle, killing around 150-300 Sioux men, women and children.

#### P3.3.3a American West: the hunting and extermination of the buffalo
Buffalo numbers collapsed from tens of millions in the 1830s to only a few hundred by the mid-1890s. Causes included commercial hunting for hides (used for industrial leather belts) and later for sport, encouraged by railroad companies that let passengers shoot from train windows and by the army, which recognised that destroying the buffalo would starve resisting tribes into submission on reservations. Competition from expanding cattle herds for grazing land and disease from domestic cattle further reduced numbers. The buffalo's destruction was catastrophic for Plains Indian life, removing the central source of food, shelter materials and clothing, and was understood by both settlers and government at the time as a deliberate, effective way of breaking Plains Indian independence.

#### P3.3.3b American West: Plains Indians' lives on the reservations
Reservation life required a dramatic change from the mobile, buffalo-based way of life to fixed settlement, often on poor-quality land unsuited to the farming the government expected tribes to adopt. Government rations, frequently inadequate, irregular or reduced through corruption among Indian agents, left many reservations facing hunger and poverty. Traditional social and spiritual structures were undermined as chiefs lost real authority to government-appointed agents, and children were often sent to boarding schools intended to suppress tribal language and culture. Movements such as the Ghost Dance (from 1889-90), promising spiritual renewal and the return of the buffalo and the old life, reflected both despair at these conditions and continued resistance, and alarmed authorities, contributing directly to the tension that led to Wounded Knee.

#### P3.3.3c American West: changing government attitudes and the Dawes Act 1887
By the 1880s, government policy shifted from treating tribes as separate nations to be confined on reservations towards 'assimilation': the belief, held by some reformers as relatively benevolent and by others as a further land grab, that Indians should become individual farmers and citizens like white Americans. The Dawes Act (General Allotment Act) of 1887 broke up reservations into individual allotments (typically 160 acres) for families, with the aim of encouraging farming and private property; land left over after allotment ('surplus land') was sold to white settlers. In practice, allotted land was often poor for farming, many allotments were later sold or lost, and total Indian-held land fell drastically by the early twentieth century, so the Act badly undermined, rather than helped, Plains Indian economic independence and traditional communal culture.

#### Q-P2P-2 Paper 2 period study Q2: write a narrative account
Q2 is an 8-mark AO2 question asking the learner to write a narrative account analysing the key events of a named development or conflict over a stated period (for example 'Write a narrative account analysing the key events of the destruction of the Plains Indians' way of life, 1876-90'), usually with two stimulus points to use alongside the learner's own knowledge. Technique: organise events strictly in chronological order, use linking words that show consequence and connection between events ('this led to...', 'as a result...', 'this meant that...') rather than a bare list, and keep the account analytical, showing why one event led to the next, not just what happened. A strong answer uses both stimulus points and adds further own material to build a connected chain of cause and effect across the whole period asked about.

#### Q-P2P-3 Paper 2 period study Q3: explain the importance of...
Q3 offers a choice of three named topics, from which the learner answers two, each worth 8 marks (16 marks total), explaining the importance of each named topic for a stated outcome (for example 'Explain the importance of the Homestead Act of 1862 for the development of the Plains'). Technique: explain directly why the named topic mattered for the stated outcome, using specific supporting detail, and, for the strongest answers, briefly weigh its importance against other factors to show judgement, rather than only describing the topic itself. Choose the two topics the learner has the most specific, accurate detail for, since importance marks reward developed explanation, not just accurate recall.
