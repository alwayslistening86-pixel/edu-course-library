# S17_Puritan_and_Catholic_Challenges_and_Mary_Queen_of_Scots - Lesson: Early Elizabethan England: Puritan and Catholic challenges, and Mary, Queen of Scots

## Goal
The learner can assess the nature and extent of the Puritan challenge to the settlement, assess the Catholic challenge from nobility, Papacy and foreign powers, explain Mary Queen of Scots' claim to the throne, and explain relations between Elizabeth and Mary in 1568-69.

## Syllabus items taught here
- B4.1.3a - Elizabeth: the Puritan challenge - nature and extent: Assess the Puritan challenge
- B4.1.3b - Elizabeth: the Catholic challenge - nobility, Papacy, foreign powers: Assess the Catholic challenge
- B4.1.4a - Elizabeth: Mary, Queen of Scots - her claim to the English throne: Explain the claim and its threat
- B4.1.4b - Elizabeth: relations between Elizabeth and Mary, 1568-69: Explain the arrival and detention of Mary

## How to teach this
Open with: 'Elizabeth's religious settlement pleased almost no one completely. Which side, Puritan or Catholic, do you think was the more dangerous threat to her in the 1560s, and why?'

#### B4.1.3a Elizabeth: the Puritan challenge - nature and extent
Puritans were committed Protestants who believed the 1559 settlement had not gone far enough in removing Catholic ceremony and hierarchy (vestments, kneeling for communion, the role of bishops) and wanted a simpler, more Calvinist-style Church closer to continental reformed practice. Their challenge in this early period was mostly non-violent: pressure within Parliament and the Church for further reform, refusal by some clergy to wear prescribed vestments (the 'Vestments Controversy' of the 1560s), and the growth of unlicensed preaching and 'prophesyings' (informal Bible study and discussion meetings). Elizabeth resisted further reform, valuing uniformity and her own authority as Supreme Governor, and Archbishop Parker enforced conformity on vestments. While troublesome and persistent, Puritanism in this period stopped short of outright rebellion and remained a manageable, if irritating, pressure for change from within the Church.

#### B4.1.3b Elizabeth: the Catholic challenge - nobility, Papacy, foreign powers
Catholic opposition came from several directions. Some northern noble families, notably in Yorkshire and Durham, remained strongly Catholic and resented the religious settlement and their declining local power, providing a domestic focus for potential rebellion. The Papacy posed an ideological and international threat: Pope Pius V would later excommunicate Elizabeth (1570), which, in Catholic teaching, released English Catholics from any duty of obedience to her, raising fears of Catholic subjects becoming a fifth column at home. Foreign Catholic powers, chiefly Spain and France, had the military strength to support a Catholic rising or invasion, and their support for Mary, Queen of Scots' claim was especially threatening because it offered a plausible, legitimate Catholic alternative monarch already resident, from 1568, on English soil.

#### B4.1.4a Elizabeth: Mary, Queen of Scots - her claim to the English throne
Mary, Queen of Scots was Elizabeth's first cousin once removed, granddaughter of Henry VIII's elder sister Margaret Tudor, giving her a strong hereditary claim to the English throne, arguably stronger than Elizabeth's in the eyes of Catholics who regarded Henry VIII's marriage to Anne Boleyn (Elizabeth's mother) as invalid. Mary was raised a Catholic in France, married briefly to the French king, and later became Queen of Scotland in her own right, making her both a legitimate dynastic claimant and a committed Catholic, a combination that made her the natural figurehead for any Catholic plot to remove Elizabeth. Her claim became far more dangerous in practice once she was physically present in England from 1568, giving plotters a real candidate to act around rather than a distant possibility.

#### B4.1.4b Elizabeth: relations between Elizabeth and Mary, 1568-69
Mary was forced to abdicate the Scottish throne in 1567 after scandal (including suspicion over the murder of her husband Lord Darnley and her marriage to the Earl of Bothwell, widely believed complicit) and a rebellion of Scottish nobles. She fled to England in 1568 seeking Elizabeth's protection and support to regain her throne. Elizabeth faced a dilemma: restoring a Catholic queen to Scotland by force was risky and expensive, but freeing Mary to leave England (for instance to France) would let her build foreign support against Elizabeth, and keeping her in England unguarded left her free to plot. Elizabeth therefore had Mary effectively detained under guard in England from 1568-69, a decision that, while cautious, kept a rival claimant permanently close at hand and became the seed of the plots covered in the next stage.
