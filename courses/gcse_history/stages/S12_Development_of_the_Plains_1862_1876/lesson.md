# S12_Development_of_the_Plains_1862_1876 - Lesson: The American West: railroads, homesteading and cattle, c1862-c1876

## Goal
The learner can explain the significance of the transcontinental railroad, the Homestead Act and homesteaders' problems and solutions, early law and order in settlements, and the growth of the cattle industry and the changing role of the cowboy.

- P3.2.1b - American West: Homestead Act 1862, homesteaders' problems and solutions, Timber Culture Act 1873: Explain homesteading and its problems

## How to teach this
Open with: 'Three things arrived on the Plains in the 1860s that changed everything: a law giving away land, a railway, and cattle drives. Which do you think mattered most, and why?' Return to this after the stage.

#### P3.2.1a American West: railroads - Pacific Railroad Act 1862, First Transcontinental 1869, network spread
The Pacific Railroad Act of 1862 authorised and funded (through land grants and loans) two companies, the Union Pacific building west from Omaha and the Central Pacific building east from Sacramento, to build a transcontinental railroad. Central Pacific relied heavily on Chinese immigrant labour, and Union Pacific on Irish immigrants and war veterans, working in dangerous conditions across plains, deserts and the Sierra Nevada. The two lines met at Promontory Point, Utah, on 10 May 1869, marked by the ceremonial 'golden spike'. The railroad transformed the West: it moved settlers, cattle, mail and goods far faster than wagons, connected eastern markets to western produce, and further branch lines spread rapidly through the 1870s-80s, accelerating settlement and undermining Plains Indian life by fragmenting hunting grounds and buffalo herds.

#### P3.2.1b American West: Homestead Act 1862, homesteaders' problems and solutions, Timber Culture Act 1873
The Homestead Act of 1862 offered 160 acres of public land free to any settler (including women and freed slaves) who lived on and improved it for five years, aiming to encourage rapid settlement of the Plains. Homesteaders faced serious problems: the treeless Plains gave little timber for building or fencing, so many built sod houses ('soddies'); tough prairie grass roots made ploughing difficult until John Deere's steel plough (developed earlier but widely adopted in this period) helped; and water, fuel and isolation remained constant challenges, alongside drought, prairie fires, and pests such as grasshopper plagues. Solutions included wells and later windmill pumps, barbed wire (patented 1874, widely used from the later 1870s) for cheap fencing, and dry-farming techniques. The Timber Culture Act of 1873 offered further land to settlers who planted trees on part of their claim, addressing the lack of timber.

#### P3.2.1c American West: law and order in settlements - law officers, federal influence
As towns grew along the railroads and homesteading spread, formal law enforcement slowly developed: elected sheriffs (county level) and town marshals (appointed by town councils) took on policing roles, though they were often few in number, sometimes only loosely trained, and relied heavily on the cooperation of local citizens. Federal marshals had wider, cross-territory authority and could pursue offenders across county lines, giving some federal reach into remote areas. Enforcement remained patchy and often reactive rather than preventive, and boom towns linked to the railroad or cattle trade (with itinerant, often armed populations) were harder to control than settled farming communities. This period saw the beginnings of more organised law and order, building on but still far short of the structures found in eastern cities.

#### P3.2.2a American West: cattle industry - Iliff, McCoy, Goodnight, Abilene, railroads
The cattle industry boomed once railroads reached the Plains, because cattle raised cheaply on the open range in Texas could now be driven north to railheads and shipped profitably to eastern markets. Joseph McCoy built stockyards and shipping facilities at Abilene, Kansas, from 1867, turning it into the first major 'cow town' at the end of trails such as the Chisholm Trail. Charles Goodnight (with Oliver Loving) pioneered long-distance cattle trails, including the Goodnight-Loving Trail, opening new routes to markets and forts. John Wesley Iliff became one of the first major Plains cattle barons, supplying beef to railroad workers and later building a large ranching operation in Colorado. Together, railroads, trail drives and entrepreneurial ranchers created a hugely profitable 'beef bonanza' through the 1870s and into the 1880s.

#### P3.2.2b American West: the changing role of the cowboy; ranchers and homesteaders
Cowboys drove huge herds, sometimes several thousand head, on trail drives lasting weeks or months, facing stampedes, river crossings, extreme weather, and the constant risk of Indian or rustler attack; the work was skilled, dangerous and poorly paid, and about a quarter of cowboys were Mexican (vaqueros, from whom much cowboy equipment and technique derived) or African American. As railroads extended further into Texas and ranching became more settled and fenced, the era of the long trail drive declined and cowboys increasingly worked as ranch hands on fixed, fenced ranges rather than driving cattle vast distances. Conflict grew between open-range ranchers, who needed unfenced land for grazing, and homesteaders, who fenced smaller farms with the new barbed wire, each blaming the other for blocking their way of life on the same land.
