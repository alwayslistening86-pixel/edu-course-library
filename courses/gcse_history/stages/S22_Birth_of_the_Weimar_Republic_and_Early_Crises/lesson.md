# S22_Birth_of_the_Weimar_Republic_and_Early_Crises - Lesson: Weimar and Nazi Germany: the birth of the Republic and the crises of 1919-23

## Goal
The learner can narrate the end of the war and abdication of the Kaiser, evaluate the strengths and weaknesses of the Weimar Constitution, explain the 'stab in the back' myth and reaction to Versailles, compare the Spartacist and Kapp challenges, and explain the causes and effects of the 1923 hyperinflation crisis.

## Syllabus items taught here
- 31.1.1a - Weimar: end of the war - unrest, abdication of the Kaiser, armistice, new republic: Narrate the birth of the Republic
- 31.1.1b - Weimar: strengths and weaknesses of the Weimar Constitution: Evaluate the Constitution
- 31.1.2a - Weimar: early unpopularity - stab in the back and the Treaty of Versailles: Explain why the Republic was resented
- 31.1.2b - Weimar: challenges from Left and Right - Spartacists, Freikorps, Kapp Putsch: Compare the challenges and the response
- 31.1.2c - Weimar: 1923 - hyperinflation and the French occupation of the Ruhr: Explain causes and effects of the 1923 crisis

## How to teach this
Open with: 'Germany lost the First World War, got a new democratic constitution, and within five years its money was worthless. Was democracy itself the problem, or just terrible luck?' Return to this across the whole Weimar unit.

#### 31.1.1a Weimar: end of the war - unrest, abdication of the Kaiser, armistice, new republic
By autumn 1918, Germany's military position was collapsing, food shortages caused by the British naval blockade had created severe hardship, and unrest spread: sailors mutinied at Kiel in late October 1918, and revolutionary councils of workers and soldiers formed in several cities, echoing the Russian Revolution. Kaiser Wilhelm II abdicated on 9 November 1918 amid this unrest, and Germany was declared a republic that same day. An armistice ending the fighting was signed on 11 November 1918. Friedrich Ebert, leader of the moderate Social Democratic Party (SPD), became the republic's first leader, tasked with establishing stable government amid military defeat, economic hardship and open revolutionary threat from further left, a highly unstable birth for the new state.

#### 31.1.1b Weimar: strengths and weaknesses of the Weimar Constitution
Agreed in 1919 and named after the city where the new National Assembly met, the constitution had real democratic strengths: all citizens over 20 (including, for the first time, women) could vote, proportional representation ensured every party's vote share translated fairly into Reichstag seats, and a Bill of Rights guaranteed civil liberties. However, structural weaknesses proved dangerous: proportional representation encouraged many small parties and made stable single-party majorities almost impossible, leading to frequent coalition governments and instability; and Article 48 let the president rule by emergency decree, bypassing the Reichstag, intended for genuine crises but later heavily exploited to weaken parliamentary government, including by Hitler's opponents and eventually by Hitler himself in 1933.

#### 31.1.2a Weimar: early unpopularity - stab in the back and the Treaty of Versailles
The new republic was unpopular from birth partly because it, not the former military and imperial leadership, had to sign the armistice and later the harsh Treaty of Versailles (1919), which imposed heavy reparations, territorial losses, military restrictions and the 'War Guilt Clause' blaming Germany for the war. Many Germans, especially nationalists and former soldiers, believed the 'stab-in-the-back myth' (Dolchstosslegende): the false claim that Germany's army had not really been defeated in the field but had been betrayed by civilian politicians, socialists and Jews at home, who agreed to the humiliating peace. Those who signed and accepted Versailles, the republic's own leaders, became known contemptuously as the 'November Criminals', permanently associating the new democracy with national humiliation in the eyes of many.

#### 31.1.2b Weimar: challenges from Left and Right - Spartacists, Freikorps, Kapp Putsch
The Republic faced violent challenges from both extremes early on. The Spartacist uprising (January 1919), led by communists Karl Liebknecht and Rosa Luxemburg seeking a Soviet-style revolution, was crushed by the government using the Freikorps, unofficial right-wing paramilitary groups of demobilised soldiers; both leaders were murdered. The Kapp Putsch (March 1920), led by right-wing nationalist Wolfgang Kapp with Freikorps support, seized Berlin briefly, protesting the government's plan to disband Freikorps units under Versailles' military restrictions; it collapsed within days when Berlin's workers launched a general strike, paralysing the city and denying Kapp's government any functioning administration. Together they show the Republic under attack from both revolutionary left and nationalist right, with the army's loyalty often doubtful in each crisis.

#### 31.1.2c Weimar: 1923 - hyperinflation and the French occupation of the Ruhr
In January 1923, France and Belgium occupied the Ruhr, Germany's key industrial region, after Germany fell behind on reparations payments. The government's response, ordering workers to passively resist (a general strike while still paying wages) while continuing to print money to cover both the strike's wage costs and other spending, caused catastrophic hyperinflation: prices doubled every few days by autumn 1923, and the mark became almost worthless (famously, banknotes were used as wallpaper or to light fires, and wages had to be paid, and spent, the same day before they lost further value). Savings were wiped out, hitting the middle class especially hard, and the crisis deeply damaged confidence in the Republic's ability to govern, even though it was eventually brought under control.
