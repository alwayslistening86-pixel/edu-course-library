# S16_Elizabeths_Accession_and_Religious_Settlement - Lesson: Early Elizabethan England: accession, the Virgin Queen and the religious settlement

## Goal
The learner can describe society and government in 1558, explain Elizabeth's problems as the 'Virgin Queen' and the challenges she faced on accession, and describe and evaluate the 1559 religious settlement and the Church of England's role.

## Syllabus items taught here
- B4.1.1a - Elizabeth: England in 1558 - society and government: Describe society and government at the accession
- B4.1.1b - Elizabeth: the Virgin Queen - legitimacy, gender, marriage, character and strengths: Explain Elizabeth's problems and strengths
- B4.1.1c - Elizabeth: challenges at home and abroad in 1558 - finances, religious divisions, the French threat: Explain the challenges on accession
- B4.1.2a - Elizabeth: the religious settlement of 1559 - features and impact: Describe and evaluate the settlement
- B4.1.2b - Elizabeth: the Church of England and its role in society: Describe the Church's role

## How to teach this
Open with: 'It is November 1558. You are 25, unmarried, a woman, and you have just become Queen of a country divided by religion and short of money. What is your first problem?' Build the stage from the answers.

#### B4.1.1a Elizabeth: England in 1558 - society and government
In 1558, England's population was around three million, mostly rural, with society organised in a clear hierarchy: the monarch, nobility (a small number of powerful landowning families), gentry, merchants and yeomen, and, at the base, the large majority of labourers and the poor. Government centred on the monarch, advised by the Privy Council (senior nobles and officials who met regularly to advise and administer), with Parliament (Lords and Commons) called mainly to grant taxation and pass laws, not sitting permanently. Local government relied on Justices of the Peace and Lord Lieutenants, unpaid local gentry who enforced law and order and organised the county militia. England was a minor European power, without a standing army, and its economy depended heavily on the wool and cloth trade.

#### B4.1.1b Elizabeth: the Virgin Queen - legitimacy, gender, marriage, character and strengths
Elizabeth's legitimacy was contested by some Catholics, who regarded Henry VIII's marriage to her mother Anne Boleyn as invalid, making Mary, Queen of Scots the legitimate Catholic claimant in their eyes. As an unmarried woman, Elizabeth faced deep doubts about whether a woman could rule effectively; many expected and pressed her to marry quickly to secure the succession and provide male guidance, yet marriage to a foreign prince risked dragging England into foreign wars, and marriage to an English noble risked internal factional resentment. Elizabeth used her unmarried status strategically, encouraging foreign suitors for diplomatic advantage while committing to none ('the Virgin Queen'). Her strengths included a sharp intellect, education in several languages, political skill, and calculated, image-conscious public presentation of authority.

#### B4.1.1c Elizabeth: challenges at home and abroad in 1558 - finances, religious divisions, the French threat
Elizabeth inherited a nearly bankrupt crown, debts from Mary I's and earlier wars, and a debased currency that Elizabeth later restored. Religious division was severe: Mary I's restoration of Catholicism and persecution of Protestants (the 'Marian persecutions') had left the country split, with Elizabeth herself a Protestant needing to settle the Church's future without provoking rebellion from either side. Abroad, France was a major threat: Mary, Queen of Scots (also Queen of France by marriage until 1560) had a strong claim to the English throne and French troops were present in Scotland, raising fears of a Catholic, French-backed invasion or succession challenge from England's northern neighbour. England also lacked strong alliances and a powerful military, leaving it vulnerable.

#### B4.1.2a Elizabeth: the religious settlement of 1559 - features and impact
The religious settlement, passed through Parliament in 1559, aimed at a compromise ('via media', middle way) between Protestantism and Catholicism to reduce conflict. The Act of Supremacy made Elizabeth 'Supreme Governor' (not 'Supreme Head', a softer title acceptable to more Catholics) of the Church of England, requiring an oath from clergy and officials. The Act of Uniformity set a single Protestant Book of Common Prayer for services, with some ambiguous wording on the nature of communion that could be read in more traditional ways, and fined those who did not attend church (recusancy fines) but kept them modest. The settlement broadly succeeded in reducing serious religious conflict for over a decade, though it satisfied committed Puritans and committed Catholics only partially, leaving both potential sources of future challenge.

#### B4.1.2b Elizabeth: the Church of England and its role in society
The Church of England, headed by Elizabeth as Supreme Governor, structured much of everyday life: attendance at parish church was expected (and after 1559 legally required, with fines for absence), sermons reinforced obedience to the monarch and social order, and parish registers recorded births, marriages and deaths. Bishops, appointed by the crown, administered dioceses and enforced conformity, while local clergy (many retained from before 1559, adapting to the new settlement) delivered services and moral instruction to their communities. The Church also acted as a mechanism of social control and information, since sermons could be used to announce royal proclamations. Its settled, moderate Protestant character (avoiding extremes of continental Calvinism or continued Catholicism) aimed to bind together a religiously divided population under royal authority.
