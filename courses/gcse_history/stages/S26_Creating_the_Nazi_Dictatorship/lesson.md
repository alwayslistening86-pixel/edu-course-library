# S26_Creating_the_Nazi_Dictatorship - Lesson: Weimar and Nazi Germany: creating and consolidating the dictatorship, 1933-34

## Goal
The learner can explain how the Reichstag Fire and Enabling Act were used to destroy opposition, explain the Night of the Long Knives and the army oath, describe the Gestapo, SS and concentration camps, and explain Nazi control of the legal system.

## Syllabus items taught here
- 31.3.1a - Nazi control: Reichstag Fire, Enabling Act, banning of parties and trade unions: Explain how the dictatorship was created
- 31.3.1b - Nazi control: Roehm and the SA, Night of the Long Knives, Hindenburg's death, Fuehrer, army oath: Explain how Hitler consolidated power
- 31.3.2a - Nazi control: the Gestapo, SS and concentration camps: Describe the terror apparatus
- 31.3.2b - Nazi control: control of the legal system: Explain the Nazification of the law

## How to teach this
Open with: 'Hitler became Chancellor legally in January 1933. By August 1934 he was an unchallenged dictator. That took just eighteen months. How?' Build the timeline across the stage.

#### 31.3.1a Nazi control: Reichstag Fire, Enabling Act, banning of parties and trade unions
On 27 February 1933, the Reichstag building burned down; a Dutch communist, Marinus van der Lubbe, was arrested at the scene, though historians still debate how far the Nazis themselves may have been involved or simply exploited the event. Hitler used the fire to persuade Hindenburg to sign the Reichstag Fire Decree, suspending civil liberties (freedom of speech, assembly and the press) and allowing mass arrest of communist opponents just before the March 1933 election. The Enabling Act (March 1933), passed with support secured partly through intimidation of opponents and a promise to the Catholic Centre Party, let Hitler's government pass laws without Reichstag approval for four years, effectively ending parliamentary democracy legally. Trade unions were banned and replaced by the Nazi German Labour Front (May 1933), and all political parties except the Nazi Party were banned by July 1933, creating a one-party state.

#### 31.3.1b Nazi control: Roehm and the SA, Night of the Long Knives, Hindenburg's death, Fuehrer, army oath
By 1934, Ernst Röhm, leader of the large SA, pushed for the SA to absorb or replace the regular army and for further 'second revolution' social change, threatening both the army's institutional position and conservative elites' interests, and worrying Hitler that Röhm's ambitions could destabilise his own power. On the Night of the Long Knives (30 June - 2 July 1934), Hitler had Röhm and dozens of other SA leaders and political enemies (including some old rivals) arrested and murdered by the SS, eliminating the SA as a threat and winning crucial army approval by removing a rival paramilitary force. When President Hindenburg died in August 1934, Hitler merged the presidency and chancellorship into a single position, 'Führer', and had every member of the armed forces swear a personal oath of loyalty to him rather than to the constitution or the state, completing his personal, unchallenged control.

#### 31.3.2a Nazi control: the Gestapo, SS and concentration camps
The Gestapo (secret state police), under Heinrich Himmler from 1934, used surveillance, informers and terror to identify and suppress political and other opponents, operating largely outside normal legal restraint, with wide powers of arrest and interrogation. The SS (Schutzstaffel), originally Hitler's personal bodyguard, grew under Himmler into a vast organisation controlling the Gestapo, concentration camps, and later an entire parallel military and police empire, seen as an elite, ideologically committed Nazi vanguard. Concentration camps, the first (Dachau) opened in March 1933, initially held political opponents (communists, socialists, trade unionists) without trial for 're-education' or punishment, using forced labour and often brutal, sometimes fatal, conditions; their population and function later expanded to include other targeted groups, discussed in the stages on Nazi racial policy.

#### 31.3.2b Nazi control: control of the legal system
The Nazis systematically undermined judicial independence to serve the regime's interests. Judges were required to be Nazi Party members or sympathisers and were pressured, including through 'guidance' from the Ministry of Justice, to interpret and apply law in line with Nazi ideology rather than impartial precedent. Special courts (People's Courts, from 1934) were created to try political crimes swiftly, with limited defence rights and, often, predetermined harsh outcomes, bypassing normal legal protections entirely for the regime's opponents. Lawyers deemed unreliable, including all Jewish lawyers, were expelled from the profession from 1933. The rule of law, in the sense of impartial, predictable legal process protecting individual rights against the state, was effectively replaced by law as an instrument of Nazi political control and terror.
