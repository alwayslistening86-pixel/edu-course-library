# S14_Farming_Ranching_and_Law_in_the_Final_Phase - Lesson: The American West: farming, ranching and law and order, c1876-c1895

## Goal
The learner can explain changes in farming technology, the decline of the open-range cattle industry, describe the final phase of settlement, and assess law and order including named lawmen and the OK Corral.

## Syllabus items taught here
- P3.3.1a - American West: changes in farming - new technology and methods: Explain changes in farming
- P3.3.1b - American West: changes in the cattle industry - winter 1886-87, end of the open range: Explain the decline of open-range ranching
- P3.3.1c - American West: continued settlement - Exodusters, Oklahoma Land Rush 1893, closure of the Indian Frontier: Describe the last phase of settlement
- P3.3.2a - American West: law and order - sheriffs and marshals, Billy the Kid, Wyatt Earp, OK Corral 1881: Assess law enforcement and its famous figures
- P3.3.2b - American West: range wars and the Johnson County War 1892: Explain range wars

## How to teach this
Open with: 'By 1895 the "Wild West" of open range and cattle drives was almost over. What ended it: nature, technology, or people?' Keep returning to all three across the stage.

#### P3.3.1a American West: changes in farming - new technology and methods
Farming on the Plains improved through new technology and method. Dry-farming techniques (deep ploughing and frequent shallow cultivation to trap moisture) let farmers cope with low rainfall further west. Windmill-powered pumps drew water from deep wells, easing the constant water problem. Mechanisation, including improved steel ploughs, seed drills, and later mechanical reapers and threshers, let fewer farmers work more land faster, though machinery was expensive and often required credit or cooperation. New, hardier wheat strains (including 'Turkey Red' wheat brought by Mennonite immigrants) suited the drier climate. Combined, these changes let farming expand successfully further onto the dry, previously marginal, high Plains through the late 1870s and 1880s, though drought years still brought hardship and failed claims.

#### P3.3.1b American West: changes in the cattle industry - winter 1886-87, end of the open range
Open-range ranching, based on free grazing of unfenced public land, was already under strain from overstocking (too many cattle competing for the same grass), falling beef prices from overproduction, and spreading barbed-wire fencing by homesteaders and rival ranchers. The catastrophic winter of 1886-87 ('the Great Die-Up'), an unusually severe blizzard season following a hot, dry summer that had left little grass, killed huge numbers of cattle, in places over half a herd, financially ruining many ranchers. Afterwards, survivors switched to smaller, fenced ranches with managed herds, winter feed and selective breeding rather than relying on the open range, ending the classic era of vast unfenced cattle kingdoms and long trail drives by the start of the 1890s.

#### P3.3.1c American West: continued settlement - Exodusters, Oklahoma Land Rush 1893, closure of the Indian Frontier
'Exodusters' were African Americans who migrated, especially from the South, to Kansas and other Plains states from the late 1870s (the term from 'exodus'), seeking land, political rights and freedom from the discrimination and violence of the post-Reconstruction South, though many still faced hardship and prejudice on arrival. The Oklahoma Land Rush of 1893 (one of several such rushes, following the opening of former Indian Territory land) saw tens of thousands of settlers race to stake claims on newly opened land in a single day. By the 1890s, remaining unassigned or 'surplus' Indian land was steadily opened to white settlement, and the US Census Bureau declared the frontier line effectively closed in 1890, symbolising the end of the era of open western land available for settlement.

#### P3.3.2a American West: law and order - sheriffs and marshals, Billy the Kid, Wyatt Earp, OK Corral 1881
By this period, law enforcement, though still thin in remote areas, was more established: elected sheriffs and appointed town marshals, sometimes assisted by federal marshals, policed growing towns, though personal reputation and gunfighting skill still mattered as much as formal authority. Billy the Kid, a notorious young outlaw and gunman in New Mexico, became involved in the Lincoln County War (a violent feud over local trade and land) before being killed by Sheriff Pat Garrett in 1881. Wyatt Earp, a lawman in several frontier towns including Tombstone, Arizona, led the famous Gunfight at the OK Corral on 26 October 1881 alongside his brothers and Doc Holliday against the rival Clanton-McLaury faction, in a dispute over cattle rustling and control of the town, killing three men in under a minute; it became emblematic of frontier law and disorder blurring together.

#### P3.3.2b American West: range wars and the Johnson County War 1892
Range wars broke out as competition for land and grazing intensified between large, established cattle barons and smaller, newer ranchers and homesteaders, often centring on accusations of cattle rustling used to justify violence against rivals. The Johnson County War (1892) in Wyoming saw large cattle barons, frustrated by falling profits after the 1886-87 winter and suspicious of small ranchers, hire gunmen to attack alleged rustlers; the resulting siege and violence required federal troops to intervene and restore order. It exposed how far private interests, rather than formal law, still shaped 'law and order' on the range even late in the period, and how government (federal) authority was still sometimes the only force able to end serious local conflict.
