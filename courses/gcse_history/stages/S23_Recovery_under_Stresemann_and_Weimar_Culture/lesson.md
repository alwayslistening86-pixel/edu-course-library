# S23_Recovery_under_Stresemann_and_Weimar_Culture - Lesson: Weimar and Nazi Germany: recovery under Stresemann and cultural change, 1924-29

## Goal
The learner can explain economic recovery under Stresemann, explain diplomatic recovery through Locarno and the League of Nations, assess changes in living standards, assess the changing position of women, and describe cultural change in the Weimar 'golden years'.

## Syllabus items taught here
- 31.1.3a - Weimar: recovery - Stresemann, Rentenmark, Dawes and Young Plans, American loans: Explain economic recovery
- 31.1.3b - Weimar: Stresemann and international acceptance - Locarno Pact, League of Nations: Explain diplomatic recovery
- 31.1.4a - Weimar: changes in the standard of living 1924-29: Assess living standards
- 31.1.4b - Weimar: changes in the position of women - work, politics, leisure: Assess the 'new woman'
- 31.1.4c - Weimar: cultural changes - architecture, art, cinema: Describe Weimar culture

## How to teach this
Open with: 'Historians call 1924-29 the "golden years" of Weimar. Was Germany's recovery as solid as it looked, or was it built on foundations that could collapse again?' Keep this judgement open until the next stage.

#### 31.1.3a Weimar: recovery - Stresemann, Rentenmark, Dawes and Young Plans, American loans
Gustav Stresemann, Chancellor briefly in 1923 then Foreign Minister until 1929, led Germany's recovery. He called off passive resistance in the Ruhr and introduced the Rentenmark (November 1923), a new, stable currency backed by land and industrial assets rather than gold, ending hyperinflation almost immediately. The Dawes Plan (1924), negotiated with the Allies, reduced annual reparations payments to more manageable levels and arranged substantial American loans to rebuild German industry. The Young Plan (1929) further reduced total reparations and extended the repayment period. American loans fuelled a genuine economic recovery through the mid-1920s, with rising industrial production and employment, though this recovery depended heavily on continued American lending, a serious underlying vulnerability that became clear after the Wall Street Crash of 1929.

#### 31.1.3b Weimar: Stresemann and international acceptance - Locarno Pact, League of Nations
Stresemann also pursued diplomatic rehabilitation. The Locarno Pact (1925), agreed with France, Belgium and Britain, saw Germany voluntarily accept its western borders with France and Belgium, easing fears of German revisionism there (though eastern borders with Poland were left more ambiguous) and significantly improving relations with former wartime enemies. Germany joined the League of Nations in 1926, gaining a permanent seat on its Council, a major symbolic step back into the community of respected nations after the Treaty of Versailles' pariah status. The Kellogg-Briand Pact (1928), which Germany also signed, renounced war as a tool of policy. Together these agreements restored a measure of international trust and cooperation, though nationalist critics within Germany condemned Stresemann for accepting Versailles' territorial settlement rather than seeking to overturn it.

#### 31.1.4a Weimar: changes in the standard of living 1924-29
Living standards genuinely improved for many in this period: wages rose in real terms for many workers, unemployment fell from the crisis levels of 1923, and social welfare provisions, including unemployment insurance introduced in 1927, extended a safety net further than before. Housing improved through public building programmes in cities, and consumer goods and popular entertainment (radio, cinema) became more widely accessible. However, the recovery was uneven and fragile: agricultural workers and farmers often did not share fully in the recovery, as farm prices remained low, and industrial workers' gains depended on the continued flow of American loans. A balanced answer stresses that although recovery was real and welcomed by many, it was neither universal nor secure against future shocks.

#### 31.1.4b Weimar: changes in the position of women - work, politics, leisure
The Weimar Constitution gave women the vote and formal legal equality, and women entered the Reichstag and some professions (law, medicine, the civil service) in growing, though still limited, numbers. More women worked outside the home, especially in new white-collar roles such as clerical and department-store work, and the image of the independent, fashionable, short-haired 'new woman', symbolised by figures in Berlin's nightlife and cinema, became a notable, if controversial, feature of urban culture. Change should not be overstated: most women still faced lower pay than men for similar work, traditional expectations around marriage and motherhood remained strong especially outside big cities, and conservative and religious opinion, and later the Nazis, strongly criticised the 'new woman' as a symptom of moral and social decline.

#### 31.1.4c Weimar: cultural changes - architecture, art, cinema
Weimar Germany, especially Berlin, became a major centre of cultural experimentation. The Bauhaus school (founded by Walter Gropius in 1919) pioneered modernist architecture and design, emphasising function and simplicity over ornament, and influenced design worldwide. German Expressionist cinema (such as Robert Wiene's 'The Cabinet of Dr Caligari', 1920, and Fritz Lang's 'Metropolis', 1927) achieved international acclaim for innovative, often unsettling visual style. Art, theatre and cabaret embraced bold, sometimes provocative new styles reflecting the era's mixture of freedom, anxiety and social change after the trauma of war and inflation. This cultural flourishing coexisted with, and partly provoked, conservative and nationalist unease at what many traditionalists saw as moral decadence, a tension the Nazis would later exploit fiercely.
