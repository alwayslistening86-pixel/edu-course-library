# S24_Hitlers_Early_Career_and_the_Munich_Putsch - Lesson: Weimar and Nazi Germany: Hitler's early career and the Munich Putsch

## Goal
The learner can explain Hitler's early rise in Munich, describe the Twenty-Five Point Programme and the SA, narrate and evaluate the Munich Putsch and Mein Kampf, and explain the Nazi Party's limited support and reorganisation, 1924-28.

## Syllabus items taught here
- 31.2.1a - Hitler: early career - German Workers' Party and the Nazi Party 1919-20: Explain Hitler's early rise in Munich
- 31.2.1b - Hitler: the Twenty-Five Point Programme and the role of the SA: Describe Nazi aims and the SA
- 31.2.2a - Hitler: the Munich Putsch - reasons, events, consequences; Mein Kampf: Narrate and evaluate the Putsch
- 31.2.2b - Hitler: limited support 1924-28; reorganisation and the Bamberg Conference 1926: Explain limited support and party reform

## How to teach this
Open with: 'In 1923, Hitler tried to seize power by force and failed, going to prison. Ten years later he became Chancellor legally. What do you think he learned from the failure?' Carry this forward to the next stage.

#### 31.2.1a Hitler: early career - German Workers' Party and the Nazi Party 1919-20
Adolf Hitler, an Austrian-born First World War veteran embittered by Germany's defeat, joined the small German Workers' Party (DAP) in Munich in September 1919, initially as an army informant sent to observe it, but quickly became its most effective public speaker, exploiting resentment over Versailles, economic hardship and fear of communism. His forceful oratory and organisational energy let him dominate the tiny party rapidly; it was renamed the National Socialist German Workers' Party (NSDAP, 'Nazi' Party) in 1920. Munich, in Bavaria, provided a favourable environment: a conservative, nationalist regional government and population resentful of the Berlin-based Republic and open to extreme right-wing ideas, giving the fledgling party room to organise and grow with relatively little official interference in its early years.

#### 31.2.1b Hitler: the Twenty-Five Point Programme and the role of the SA
The Twenty-Five Point Programme (1920) set out the party's core aims: extreme nationalism (union of all Germans, rejection of Versailles), virulent antisemitism (excluding Jews from citizenship), some anti-capitalist and welfare-sounding measures aimed at winning working-class support, and strong state control, blending nationalist and populist appeals to different discontented groups simultaneously. The SA (Sturmabteilung, 'stormtroopers' or 'brownshirts'), founded around 1921 under early leadership including Ernst Röhm, provided the party with a paramilitary force: they protected party meetings, intimidated and physically attacked opponents (especially communists and socialists), and gave the Nazis a visible, disciplined, uniformed presence on the streets that both attracted supporters drawn to strength and order and alarmed many others as openly violent and lawless.

#### 31.2.2a Hitler: the Munich Putsch - reasons, events, consequences; Mein Kampf
By November 1923, hyperinflation and the Ruhr crisis had badly weakened the Weimar government's authority, and Hitler judged the moment ripe to seize power, beginning in Bavaria and marching on Berlin in imitation of Mussolini's successful 1922 March on Rome. On 8-9 November 1923, Hitler and around 2,000 Nazi supporters, joined briefly by war hero General Ludendorff, attempted to seize control of Munich, but the planned march was met by armed police, who opened fire; sixteen Nazis and four police were killed, and the putsch collapsed within a day. Hitler was arrested, tried for treason, and sentenced to five years (serving only about nine months) in relatively comfortable conditions at Landsberg prison, where he wrote 'Mein Kampf' ('My Struggle'), setting out his ideology, including extreme antisemitism and the idea of German 'living space' (Lebensraum) in the east.

#### 31.2.2b Hitler: limited support 1924-28; reorganisation and the Bamberg Conference 1926
The failed putsch taught Hitler that violent seizure of power could not succeed against the state's armed forces; on his release he committed the party to gaining power through legal, electoral means instead, while still building an authoritarian movement underneath. Through 1924-28, however, Nazi support remained small: Stresemann's economic and diplomatic recovery removed much of the crisis atmosphere that had earlier fuelled extremist appeal, and the Nazis won only a small share of Reichstag seats in this period. Hitler used these quieter years to reorganise and unify the party, resolving disputes between its more urban, nationalist wing and a more radical, socialist-leaning northern wing (associated with Gregor Strasser) at the Bamberg Conference (1926), reasserting his own personal authority ('Führer principle') as the party's undisputed leader ahead of the crisis to come.
