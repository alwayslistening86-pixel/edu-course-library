# S03_Crime_Process_of_Change_and_Medieval_Crime - Lesson: Crime and punishment: the process of change and medieval crime, c1000-c1500

## Goal
The learner can explain how change is judged across c1000-present and describe medieval crimes, poaching as social crime and the effect of the Norman Conquest and Forest Laws.

## Syllabus items taught here
- 10.0a - Crime and punishment: the nature and process of change: Understand patterns of change, trends and turning points across c1000-present
- 10.0b - Key factors: attitudes in society, individuals and institutions, science and technology: Use the three key factors to explain change and continuity
- 10.0c - How factors worked together; using strand 3 case studies: Explain how factors combined at particular times and use case studies as evidence
- 10.1.1a - Medieval crimes against the person, property and authority; poaching as social crime: Describe medieval crime types and why poaching was a social crime
- 10.1.1b - Changing definitions of crime after the Norman Conquest; William I's Forest Laws: Explain how the Conquest redefined crime

## How to teach this
Open with: 'Draw a line from 1000 to today. Where do you think crime and punishment changed most, and why?' Let the learner mark guesses (Peel? abolition of hanging? the Norman Conquest?). Keep the line on screen and add each turning point as the course proceeds. Insist on 'because' for every mark.

#### 10.0a Crime and punishment: the nature and process of change
The thematic study asks the learner to understand how features of crime and punishment linked with features of society in each period, and to understand patterns of change, trends and turning points. Change is not steady: long periods of similarity (hanging and fines continued for centuries) can be broken by turning points, such as the 1215 end of trial by ordeal, the 1829 creation of the Metropolitan Police, or the abolition of the death penalty for murder in 1965. Teach the learner to judge each development by asking: how much changed, for how many people, how fast, and did it last? The four sections run c1000-c1500, c1500-c1700, c1700-c1900 and c1900-present. Paper 1 questions will normally span at least a century and sometimes the whole theme.

#### 10.0b Key factors: attitudes in society, individuals and institutions, science and technology
The specification names three factors that encourage or inhibit change. Attitudes in society: what people believe is a crime and what punishments they accept (for example, belief in the devil helped drive witch trials; Enlightenment thinking ended them). Individuals and institutions, meaning Church and government: William I and his Forest Laws, the Church's sanctuary and benefit of clergy, John Howard, Elizabeth Fry, Robert Peel, Parliament passing the Bloody Code. Science and technology: fingerprinting, forensics, DNA profiling, CCTV, telegraph and railways, which alter both crime and detection. A strong answer chooses the factor best fitting the change, gives evidence, and explains why other factors mattered less.

#### 10.0c How factors worked together; using strand 3 case studies
Factors rarely act alone. The end of trial by ordeal combined institutions (Pope Innocent III at the Fourth Lateran Council of 1215) with attitudes (doubts about God's judgement) and the rise of juries. The Metropolitan Police combined individuals (Peel), attitudes (fear of disorder after Peterloo and Swing) and technology (population growth in cities). Each period has strand 3 case studies (for example Benefit of Clergy and the ordeal, Hopkins, Pentonville, the Bentley case) that the learner should be able to use as detailed evidence in change-and-continuity answers, not just as stand-alone stories. Practise turning a case study into an argument: 'Pentonville shows attitudes moving from retribution toward reform.'

#### 10.1.1a Medieval crimes against the person, property and authority; poaching as social crime
Medieval crimes fell into three categories. Crimes against the person: murder, manslaughter, assault, rape. Crimes against property: theft (larceny), arson, burglary, handling stolen goods. Crimes against authority: treason, rebellion, coining (making false money), and evading laws such as refusing to join the hue and cry or breaking the Assize of Bread and Ale (selling short measure). Heresy was also a crime against the Church and authority. Most crime was theft, and most theft was of food, animals and tools. Poaching, illegally hunting game (especially deer) on royal or lords' land, is the specified example of 'social crime': an act treated as a crime by the law but not regarded as morally wrong by many ordinary people, who often needed the food and resented the restrictions.

#### 10.1.1b Changing definitions of crime after the Norman Conquest; William I's Forest Laws
William I won the throne at Hastings in 1066 and made law a tool of control over a hostile population. He declared large areas of England royal forests (Forest Law applied to land where the king kept deer, not simply woodland) and Forest Laws forbade hunting deer and wild boar, and cutting timber or clearing land, without permission; penalties could include fines, blinding, mutilation or death. Special forest courts and officials enforced them, and by the thirteenth century forests covered roughly a fifth to a third of England. The Normans also introduced the murdrum fine: if a Norman was found murdered and the killer was not caught, the local hundred paid a heavy fine. Crimes became offences against the king's peace, so fines went to the crown rather than to victims' families. The Conquest therefore changed definitions of crime, and poaching became a serious offence.
