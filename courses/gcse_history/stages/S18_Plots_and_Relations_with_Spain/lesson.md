# S18_Plots_and_Relations_with_Spain - Lesson: Early Elizabethan England: plots against Elizabeth and rivalry with Spain

## Goal
The learner can explain the Revolt of the Northern Earls, the Ridolfi, Throckmorton and Babington plots and Walsingham's spy network, the execution of Mary Queen of Scots and its significance, and explain political, religious and commercial rivalry with Spain.

## Syllabus items taught here
- B4.2.1a - Elizabeth: Revolt of the Northern Earls 1569-70 - reasons and significance: Explain the revolt
- B4.2.1b - Elizabeth: Ridolfi, Throckmorton and Babington plots; Walsingham and spies: Describe the plots and the spy network
- B4.2.1c - Elizabeth: the execution of Mary, Queen of Scots, 1587 - reasons and significance: Explain why Mary was executed

## How to teach this
Open with: 'Between 1569 and 1587, at least four serious plots tried to replace Elizabeth with Mary, Queen of Scots. Why do you think it took until 1587 for Elizabeth to finally have Mary executed?'

#### B4.2.1a Elizabeth: Revolt of the Northern Earls 1569-70 - reasons and significance
The Revolt of the Northern Earls (1569-70), led by the Earls of Northumberland and Westmorland, aimed to restore Catholicism, free and possibly marry Mary, Queen of Scots to the Duke of Norfolk, and reduce the growing power of lower-born royal advisers (notably William Cecil) at the expense of traditional northern nobility. The rebels briefly celebrated Catholic Mass in Durham Cathedral but failed to win wide support, retreated as royal forces approached, and were crushed; several hundred rebels were executed as a deterrent, and the northern earls' power was permanently broken. Its significance lies in showing that domestic Catholic rebellion, without foreign military backing, could not succeed, but it also confirmed Mary's danger as a rallying point, prompting closer government surveillance thereafter.

#### B4.2.1b Elizabeth: Ridolfi, Throckmorton and Babington plots; Walsingham and spies
The Ridolfi Plot (1571), organised by Italian banker Roberto Ridolfi, aimed to have Spain invade England, marry Mary to Norfolk and place her on the throne; it was uncovered through intercepted letters, leading to Norfolk's execution in 1572. The Throckmorton Plot (1583), led by Francis Throckmorton, planned a French-backed invasion to free Mary; it was uncovered by Sir Francis Walsingham's intelligence network. The Babington Plot (1586), the most decisive, involved coded letters between Mary and conspirator Anthony Babington discussing Elizabeth's assassination; Walsingham's agents intercepted and decoded the letters, proving Mary's direct knowledge and approval. Walsingham, Elizabeth's spymaster, built an extensive network of informers, codebreakers and intercepted correspondence across Europe, making each successive plot harder to hide.

#### B4.2.1c Elizabeth: the execution of Mary, Queen of Scots, 1587 - reasons and significance
The Babington Plot's clear evidence of Mary's approval for Elizabeth's murder finally persuaded Elizabeth's council that Mary could not safely be kept alive; Mary was tried, convicted of treason, and, after Elizabeth's long and reluctant hesitation over executing an anointed fellow queen, executed at Fotheringhay Castle on 8 February 1587. Its significance was major: it removed the Catholic claimant who had inspired every major plot since 1568, but also removed Philip II of Spain's main reason for restraint, since Mary had earlier, in some accounts, bequeathed her claim to the English throne to him; many historians see the execution as a direct contributing cause to Spain's decision to launch the Armada the following year, 1588.

#### B4.2.2a Elizabeth: relations with Spain - political, religious and commercial rivalry
Relations with Spain, the dominant European power under Philip II, worsened steadily across Elizabeth's reign for several intertwined reasons. Politically, Philip had briefly been King of England through his marriage to Mary I and retained ambitions of influence; religiously, Spain championed Catholicism against Protestant England and resented English support for Protestant rebels in the Spanish Netherlands. Commercially, England's growing trade and, more provocatively, English privateers raiding Spanish treasure ships and colonies competed directly with Spain's valuable American empire and its silver shipments. Each strand reinforced the others: religious rivalry justified political hostility, and commercial raiding gave Spain concrete grievances, so that by the mid-1580s outright war, though undeclared for some years, was effectively already under way at sea and in the Netherlands.
