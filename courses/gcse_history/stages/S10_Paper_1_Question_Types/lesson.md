# S10_Paper_1_Question_Types - Lesson: Paper 1 question types: thematic study and historic environment

## Goal
The learner can answer every Paper 1 question type on Crime and Punishment and Whitechapel: describe two features, source usefulness, follow-up enquiry, similarity or difference, explain why, and the extended how-far-do-you-agree judgement with SPaG.

## Syllabus items taught here
- Q-P1-1 - Paper 1 Q1: describe two features (historic environment): Answer the 4-mark AO1 question on features of the period
- Q-P1-2a - Paper 1 Q2(a): how useful are Sources A and B for an enquiry into...: Answer the 8-mark AO3 usefulness question
- Q-P1-2b - Paper 1 Q2(b): follow-up enquiry: Suggest a follow-up question, detail and source type
- Q-P1-3 - Paper 1 Q3: similarity or difference across time: Answer the 4-mark 'explain two similarities/differences' question
- Q-P1-4 - Paper 1 Q4: explain why (process of change): Answer the 12-mark AO1/AO2 change question
- Q-P1-56 - Paper 1 Q5/6: how far do you agree (extended judgement, 16 plus 4 SPaG): Answer the 20-mark judgement question with SPaG

## How to teach this
Open with: 'Paper 1 is worth 52 marks in 1 hour 20 minutes, split across the thematic study and the Whitechapel environment. Today we learn exactly what each question wants, not new content.' Work through each question type with its command word and mark allocation.

#### Q-P1-1 Paper 1 Q1: describe two features (historic environment)
Q1 is a 4-mark AO1 question testing knowledge of the historic environment (Whitechapel), asking the learner to describe two features of a named aspect, for example 'Describe two features of policing in Whitechapel in the 1880s.' The best technique is: state a feature in one clear sentence, then add specific supporting detail (a fact, date, name or example) in a second sentence, and repeat for the second feature. No explanation of causes or consequences is needed or rewarded; marks come from accurate, specific factual detail, not from length. A common error is writing two vague statements instead of one detailed point twice; another is straying into 'why', which wastes time on an AO1 question.

#### Q-P1-2a Paper 1 Q2(a): how useful are Sources A and B for an enquiry into...
Q2(a) is an 8-mark AO3 question giving two contemporary sources connected to Whitechapel and asking how useful they are for a stated enquiry. Technique: for each source, comment on its content (what it shows relevant to the enquiry), its provenance (who made it, when, why - nature, origin, purpose), and reach a judgement on usefulness for that specific enquiry, using own contextual knowledge to test or support what the source shows. A strong answer cross-references the two sources against each other and against the learner's own knowledge, and avoids simply describing content without judging usefulness. Avoid generic claims like 'it is useful because it is a primary source': usefulness always depends on the specific enquiry named in the question.

#### Q-P1-2b Paper 1 Q2(b): follow-up enquiry
Q2(b) is a short question (around 4 marks) that follows from Q2(a): the learner must suggest one further question they would ask as part of the same historical enquiry, name one detail they would want to find out, and suggest a type of source that might supply it, with a brief reason why that source type would help. Technique: keep the further question tightly linked to the original enquiry focus (not a new topic), and make the suggested source and detail concrete rather than generic (for example 'Metropolitan Police case files, to find out how many officers patrolled H Division at night' rather than just 'more information'). This tests the enquiry-planning skill taught in the previous stage.

#### Q-P1-3 Paper 1 Q3: similarity or difference across time
Q3 is a 4-mark AO2 question asking the learner to explain two similarities or two differences between crime, punishment or enforcement in two specified, often widely separated, periods (for example 'Explain one way in which punishment in c1700 was similar to punishment in c1900'). Technique: for each point, name the specific feature in each period and make the comparison explicit ('In both periods... whereas in...' or 'Both... because...'), rather than simply describing each period separately and leaving the comparison implicit. Accurate dates and named examples (Bloody Code versus non-custodial sentencing; hue and cry versus Neighbourhood Watch) earn the higher marks; vague comparisons without specific detail from both periods do not.

#### Q-P1-4 Paper 1 Q4: explain why (process of change)
Q4 is a 12-mark AO1/AO2 causation question, usually with stimulus bullet points, asking the learner to explain why a change occurred (for example 'Explain why there was a change in methods of law enforcement in the period c1700-c1900'). Technique: address each stimulus point with explanation, not just description ('because...' after every claim), add at least one further own point beyond the stimulus, and, where possible, show how factors connect (attitudes, individuals/institutions, science and technology). Organise by cause rather than by narrating events chronologically, and finish points fully rather than listing many undeveloped ones; two or three well-explained causes outscore five bare assertions.

#### Q-P1-56 Paper 1 Q5/6: how far do you agree (extended judgement, 16 plus 4 SPaG)
Q5 or Q6 (the learner answers whichever is offered, sometimes with a choice) is the 20-mark question (16 content marks plus 4 for spelling, punctuation, grammar and specialist terminology), presenting a statement (for example 'The most significant factor in the development of the police was the Metropolitan Police Act of 1829. How far do you agree?') with stimulus points, and asking the learner to reach a supported judgement. Technique: plan first; address each stimulus point and at least one own point; explain how each supports or challenges the statement; weigh points against each other explicitly; and write a final paragraph giving a clear, justified overall judgement ('most significant because...', not just 'yes' or 'no'). Use full sentences, paragraphs and specialist terms (deterrence, retribution, social crime) accurately for the SPaG marks.
