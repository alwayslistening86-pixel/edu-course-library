# S02_Second_Order_Concepts_Sources_Interpretations - Lesson: Second-order concepts, sources and interpretations

## Goal
The learner can use causation, consequence, change, continuity, similarity, difference and significance precisely, and can analyse a source and an interpretation for a specific enquiry.

## Syllabus items taught here
- H-SO1 - Second-order concepts: causation and consequence: Explain why events happened and what results followed
- H-SO2 - Second-order concepts: change and continuity: Judge the nature, pace, extent and turning points of change
- H-SO3 - Second-order concepts: similarity and difference: Compare periods, people and situations
- H-SO4 - Second-order concepts: significance: Judge why and to whom something mattered, and how much
- H-SRC - Working with contemporary sources: inference, content, provenance, context: Analyse a source's content, nature, origin, purpose and context
- H-INT - Working with interpretations: how and why they differ: Analyse an interpretation's argument, purpose, selection of evidence and viewpoint

## How to teach this
Ask: 'Was the Norman Conquest a turning point in the history of crime?' Then ask what 'turning point' actually means and how they would decide. Guide the learner to see that history questions ask for judgement using second-order concepts: the DfE's list is causation, consequence, similarity, difference, change, continuity and significance. Use the same short example, the introduction of the Forest Laws, to apply each concept.

#### H-SO1 Second-order concepts: causation and consequence
Causation asks why something happened; consequence asks what resulted. Both need an argument about relative importance. Distinguish long-term causes (the belief that the Church should guide law), short-term causes (a specific event) and triggers. In the Elizabethan Armada question, long-term causes were religious and commercial rivalry with Spain, and the trigger was Mary Queen of Scots' execution in 1587. Consequences can be short or long term, intended or unintended (the Forest Laws' harshness made poaching a social crime). Teach connectives: 'this led to', 'as a result', 'which meant that', 'a further reason', and the ranking phrase 'the most important reason was... because without it...'. Paper 2 Q1 and Paper 3 Q2 target this concept.

#### H-SO2 Second-order concepts: change and continuity
Change and continuity concern what altered and what stayed the same over a period, and how fast, how much and why. Paper 1 asks about the nature, extent and pace of change, and about turning points. Judge change by comparing before and after ('the number of crimes carrying the death penalty rose from about 50 in 1688 to about 220 by 1815') and continuity by naming what persisted (poaching and smuggling in every period). A turning point is where the direction or speed of change shifts. Use the three key factors in the crime unit (attitudes, individuals and institutions, science and technology) to explain change or lack of it. Avoid the trap that change is always progress: in the Whitechapel period policing improved and problems persisted.

#### H-SO3 Second-order concepts: similarity and difference
Similarity and difference compare two periods, people, groups or situations, and Paper 1 Q3 asks for this across at least a century. Good answers name a specific similarity or difference in each period rather than describing one only. 'Both in c1100 and c1800 communities were expected to catch criminals themselves' is a similarity, and it is stronger when both halves are evidenced (the hue and cry; victims hiring thief-takers or paying for a parish constable). Also compare within one period: Sand Creek versus Red Cloud's War, or the Spartacist Uprising versus the Kapp Putsch. Always finish with the point of comparison in words, not just two lists.

#### H-SO4 Second-order concepts: significance
Significance asks why something mattered, to whom, and for how long. Criteria include how many people were affected, how deeply, for how long, whether it shaped later events, and whether it is remembered. Paper 1's turning point questions and Paper 2's 'Explain the importance of...' questions test it. Example: the Pacific Railroad Act of 1862 mattered because it enabled the 1869 transcontinental link, which speeded settlement, made cattle drives to railheads viable and undermined the buffalo herds. Also see that significance is judged by different people differently (for Plains peoples the railroad was devastating). Always finish with a judgement rather than a list.

#### H-SRC Working with contemporary sources: inference, content, provenance, context
A contemporary source dates from the period studied. Teach three routes: content (what it says, what can be inferred beyond the surface), provenance (nature, origin and purpose: who made it, when, for whom, why, what kind of source) and context (what the learner knows from the period that supports, extends or challenges it). Inference means 'what does this suggest?', supported with a quotation or detail: 'Source A suggests policing was hard because the writer says the alleys were unlit'. Usefulness is always for a specific enquiry: a Punch cartoon is excellent evidence of public attitudes to the police but weak evidence about detective methods. Purpose can raise or lower value: a biased source is still useful evidence of the writer's views. Do not write generic 'it is biased so it is unreliable'.

#### H-INT Working with interpretations: how and why they differ
An interpretation is a later account by a historian (or others) that argues a view about the past. Analyse it for its main argument (what does it claim overall?), the evidence and emphasis it selects (what does it stress or omit?), its tone and viewpoint, and its possible purpose or context (a book for general readers, or written after the Cold War, or from a particular school of thought). To say how two interpretations differ, quote their different overall judgements. To suggest why, refer to different emphasis, different evidence, different viewpoint or purpose, and different context or time of writing. To evaluate, use own knowledge to test each claim and decide which is more convincing, and say why. Interpretations can differ legitimately, and both can have merit.
