# S21_Exploration_Colonisation_and_Paper_2_British_Depth_Study_Questions - Lesson: Early Elizabethan England: exploration, Virginia, and Paper 2 British depth study question types

## Goal
The learner can explain why exploration grew, explain Drake's circumnavigation, explain reasons for and the failure of the attempted colonisation of Virginia, and answer every Paper 2 British depth study question type.

## Syllabus items taught here
- B4.3.3a - Elizabeth: factors prompting exploration - technology and trade: Explain why exploration grew
- B4.3.3b - Elizabeth: Drake's circumnavigation of the globe - reasons and significance: Explain the voyage and its significance
- B4.3.4a - Elizabeth: attempted colonisation of Virginia and Raleigh's significance: Explain reasons for colonisation
- B4.3.4b - Elizabeth: reasons for the failure of the first Virginia settlement: Explain the failure of Roanoke
- Q-P2B-1 - Paper 2 British depth study Q1: describe two features: Answer the AO1 features question
- Q-P2B-2 - Paper 2 British depth study Q2: explain why: Answer the 12-mark causation question

## How to teach this
Open with: 'Elizabeth's England went from a country too poor and weak to challenge Spain in 1558, to a country founding colonies in America by the 1580s. What made that ambition possible?'

#### B4.3.3a Elizabeth: factors prompting exploration - technology and trade
Exploration grew from a combination of new opportunity and old motive. Improved shipbuilding and navigation technology (better maps, the compass, the astrolabe, and stronger, more seaworthy ship designs) made longer ocean voyages more feasible and survivable than in earlier centuries. The desire for trade and profit was central: English merchants sought new markets and, above all, access to the riches (gold, silver, spices) that Spain and Portugal already drew from their own overseas empires, driving envy as well as ambition. National and religious rivalry with Catholic Spain added motive: successful English voyages, especially those that damaged Spanish interests, brought prestige, wealth and a form of proxy warfare against a hostile rival, all encouraged, informally, by the crown.

#### B4.3.3b Elizabeth: Drake's circumnavigation of the globe - reasons and significance
Francis Drake set out in 1577, officially on a trading voyage but in practice authorised to raid Spanish territory and shipping in the Pacific, where Spanish ships and ports, unused to attack from that direction, were poorly defended. Sailing via South America (through the Strait of Magellan), up the Pacific coast raiding Spanish settlements and treasure ships, across the Pacific and Indian Oceans and around Africa, Drake returned to England in 1580 having circumnavigated the globe, only the second expedition ever to do so and the first led by its captain throughout. He returned with vast plundered treasure, and Elizabeth knighted him aboard his ship the Golden Hind in 1581, a deliberately provocative public reward that further angered Spain and boosted England's confidence and reputation as a maritime power.

#### B4.3.4a Elizabeth: attempted colonisation of Virginia and Raleigh's significance
Sir Walter Raleigh, a prominent courtier and favourite of Elizabeth, organised and funded (though he did not personally sail on) expeditions to establish an English colony in North America, named Virginia in Elizabeth's honour ('the Virgin Queen'). The aims were to establish a base for further exploration and privateering against Spanish shipping, to search for resources and potential wealth, to extend English and Protestant influence in the New World against Catholic Spanish and French claims, and to demonstrate England's growing maritime and colonial ambition. Raleigh's role, as an organiser, promoter and royal favourite rather than a settler himself, reflects how Elizabethan colonisation depended on wealthy courtiers seeking royal favour and financial gain as much as on the actual settlers who risked the voyage.

#### B4.3.4b Elizabeth: reasons for the failure of the first Virginia settlement
The first Roanoke colony (1585) was abandoned within a year after settlers, poorly prepared for self-sufficiency, ran short of supplies, suffered damaged relations and conflict with local Indigenous peoples, and were relieved by Drake's fleet, which happened to call at the colony. A second attempt (1587), including women and children under John White, fared worse: relations with Indigenous peoples remained difficult, supplies again ran low, and when White returned from a resupply trip to England (delayed several years partly by the Armada crisis diverting English ships) in 1590, the colony had vanished entirely, leaving only the cryptic word 'CROATOAN' carved on a post, with the settlers' fate never definitively established. Causes of failure included poor planning, inadequate supplies, conflict with Indigenous peoples, and unreliable, delayed support from England.

#### Q-P2B-1 Paper 2 British depth study Q1: describe two features
Q1 on the British depth study is a 4-mark AO1 question asking the learner to describe two features of a named aspect of Early Elizabethan England (for example 'Describe two features of the Elizabethan Poor Law'). As with the Paper 1 equivalent, the technique is one clear statement plus specific supporting detail per feature (a date, name, figure or example), with no explanation of cause or consequence required; two well-detailed features outscore several vague ones.

#### Q-P2B-2 Paper 2 British depth study Q2: explain why
Q2 is a 12-mark AO1/AO2 causation question with stimulus points, asking the learner to explain why something happened (for example 'Explain why Mary, Queen of Scots was executed in 1587'). Technique: explain each stimulus point fully with 'because' reasoning, add at least one own point, and, where relevant, show how causes connect or build on each other over time, organising by cause rather than narrating events in order; depth on fewer points scores better than a long, thin list.

#### Q-P2B-34 Paper 2 British depth study Q3/4: how far do you agree (judgement)
Q3 or Q4 is the 16-mark extended judgement question, presenting a statement with stimulus points (for example 'The most important reason for the failure of the Virginia colony was poor planning. How far do you agree?') and asking for a supported judgement; unlike Paper 1's equivalent, this question carries no separate SPaG allocation, but clear, accurate written expression still matters for AO1/AO2 marks. Technique: address each stimulus point and at least one own point, explain how each supports or challenges the statement, weigh the points against each other, and end with a clear, justified overall judgement rather than a simple restatement of both sides.
