# S11_Plains_Life_and_Early_Migration - Lesson: The American West: Plains Indian life and early migration, c1835-c1862

## Goal
The learner can describe Plains Indian social and tribal structures, their beliefs about land and property, the factors that encouraged migration west, and compare the Donner Party and Mormon migrations and early settlement's problems, including early government policy and tension and lawlessness.

- P3.1.1b - American West: Plains Indians - beliefs about land and nature, attitudes to war and property: Explain Plains beliefs and values
- P3.1.2a - American West: factors encouraging migration - Oregon Trail, Manifest Destiny, Gold Rush: Explain push and pull factors of migration
- P3.1.2b - American West: early migration to c1850 - the Donner Party and the Mormon migration: Narrate and compare two early migrations
- P3.1.2c - American West: development and problems of early settlement: Describe early settlement and its difficulties

- P3.1.3b - American West: lawlessness in early towns and settlements: Describe lawlessness and attempts to tackle it

## How to teach this
Open with: 'In 1835 the Great Plains belonged, in practice, to the peoples who lived there. By 1895 they did not. What began that change?' Keep this question live across the whole American West unit.

#### P3.1.1a American West: Plains Indians - social and tribal structures, ways of life, survival
The Plains Indians, including the Sioux (Lakota), Cheyenne, Pawnee and other nations, were organised into tribes divided into bands led by chiefs chosen for wisdom, bravery and generosity rather than inherited right, with decisions often made by councils rather than one ruler. Most Plains peoples were nomadic or semi-nomadic, following the vast buffalo herds that supplied food (meat, often dried as pemmican), clothing and tipi covers from hide, tools from bone, and fuel from dried dung. Horses, obtained from Spanish settlers from the seventeenth century, transformed hunting and mobility. Survival depended on cooperation within the band, seasonal movement, and detailed knowledge of the land, weather and buffalo migration patterns; warfare between rival tribes over hunting grounds was common but usually limited in scale.

#### P3.1.1b American West: Plains Indians - beliefs about land and nature, attitudes to war and property
Most Plains peoples believed land could not be owned by individuals; it was held and used communally, given by the Great Spirit for all to share, which put them in direct conflict with white settlers' idea of fenced, privately owned property. Nature was often viewed as sacred and interconnected with spiritual life; the buffalo in particular was treated with near-religious respect, with every part used and little waste. Warfare, especially among young men, often served to gain honour, horses or revenge rather than to conquer and hold territory permanently, and was governed by codes such as 'counting coup' (touching an enemy without necessarily killing). These beliefs made later US government demands to settle on fixed reservations and to fence and farm individual plots deeply alien to Plains culture.

#### P3.1.2a American West: factors encouraging migration - Oregon Trail, Manifest Destiny, Gold Rush
Several 'pull' factors drew settlers west. The Oregon Trail, a roughly 2,000-mile route opened from the early 1840s, offered fertile land in the Willamette Valley, and government land grants encouraged families to make the journey by wagon train. 'Manifest Destiny', the widely held belief that white Americans had a God-given right and duty to settle the whole continent, gave migration ideological justification and popular support. The discovery of gold in California in 1848 triggered the 1849 Gold Rush, drawing around 300,000 people ('forty-niners') west in a single burst seeking sudden wealth. Economic hardship, unemployment and land shortage in the east acted as 'push' factors, while cheap or free western land and the promise of a fresh start pulled families onward despite the journey's dangers.

#### P3.1.2b American West: early migration to c1850 - the Donner Party and the Mormon migration
The Donner Party (1846-47), around 90 emigrants heading for California, took an unproven shortcut, fell dangerously behind schedule, and became trapped by heavy snow in the Sierra Nevada mountains; roughly half died of cold and starvation, and some survivors resorted to cannibalism, making it a famous warning about the dangers of poor planning and unfamiliar terrain. The Mormons (Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints), led by Brigham Young after the murder of founder Joseph Smith, fled persecution in Illinois in 1846-47 and migrated in disciplined, well-organised wagon companies to the Great Salt Lake valley in Utah, arriving in 1847. Their strong central leadership, cooperative organisation and irrigation skill let them found a successful, self-sufficient settlement, contrasting sharply with the Donner Party's disorganisation and disaster.

#### P3.1.2c American West: development and problems of early settlement
Early settlers, once arrived, faced building shelter (often sod houses on the treeless plains), breaking tough prairie soil, securing water, and surviving harsh winters and dry summers with few established supply networks. Isolation was severe: neighbours could be many miles away, medical help was scarce, and initial crop yields were often poor until settlers learned to farm unfamiliar land. The Mormons at Salt Lake overcame aridity through organised irrigation channels and communal labour, becoming a model of successful planning. Many other early settlements struggled with poor initial harvests, disease and conflict over land and water rights with existing Plains peoples. Government support was minimal in this period, so success depended heavily on preparation, cooperation and luck with weather.

#### P3.1.3a American West: tension with Plains Indians - Permanent Indian Frontier, Fort Laramie 1851, Indian Appropriations Act 1851
As migration increased, the US government tried to manage relations with Plains peoples. The idea of a 'Permanent Indian Frontier', a line beyond which white settlement would not expand, had already begun breaking down by the 1830s-40s as migrants crossed Indian land on trails such as the Oregon Trail. The Fort Laramie Treaty of 1851 tried a new approach: the government negotiated with several tribes to define specific tribal territories in return for allowing safe passage for settlers along the trails and permitting roads and forts, with promised annual payments. The Indian Appropriations Act of 1851 funded the reservation system, formally establishing that tribes would be moved onto set areas of land. These agreements were poorly understood or enforced, and initial tension came from settlers' trail use of Indian land and hunting grounds.

#### P3.1.3b American West: lawlessness in early towns and settlements
Early settlements, especially boom towns linked to trails and later mining, had almost no formal law enforcement: there was no organised police force, and communities were often small, transient and far from established government. Vigilante 'committees' sometimes formed to punish suspected wrongdoers without trial, reflecting both a desire for order and the absence of it. Disputes over land claims, water rights, gambling debts and alcohol frequently turned violent, and horse or cattle theft could be punished severely, including lynching, because horses were essential to survival. This early lawlessness set the pattern the specification returns to in later Key Topics, as government-appointed law officers (sheriffs and marshals) only gradually extended real authority into these fast-growing communities.
