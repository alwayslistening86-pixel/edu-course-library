# S06_Eighteenth_and_Nineteenth_Century_Crime - Lesson: Crime, law enforcement and punishment, c1700-c1900

## Goal
The learner can describe continuity and change in crime (highway robbery, poaching, smuggling), explain the end of witchcraft prosecutions and the Tolpuddle Martyrs, trace the growth of professional policing, and explain changing purposes of punishment including prison reform.

## Syllabus items taught here
- 10.3.1a - Highway robbery, poaching and smuggling in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries: Describe continuity and change in crime, including social crimes
- 10.3.1b - Changing definitions of crime: the end of witchcraft prosecutions; the Tolpuddle Martyrs: Explain the end of witchcraft trials and the Tolpuddle case
- 10.3.2a - Law enforcement: the Fielding brothers, development of police forces, beginning of CID: Trace the move from parish constables to professional police
- 10.3.2b - Purpose of punishment: transportation, public execution, Bloody Code, prison reform (Howard, Fry): Explain changing views of punishment and reformers' influence
- 10.3.3a - Case study: Pentonville prison, mid nineteenth century - the separate system: Explain why Pentonville was built and how the separate system worked
- 10.3.3b - Key individual: Robert Peel - penal reform and the Metropolitan Police: Assess Peel's contribution

## How to teach this
Open with: 'By 1900 England had a professional police force and no Bloody Code. In 1700 it had neither. What changed, and who or what made it change?' Build a two-column list (1700 / 1900) as the stage proceeds.

#### 10.3.1a Highway robbery, poaching and smuggling in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries
Highway robbery peaked in the eighteenth century: mounted robbers such as Dick Turpin (executed 1739) held up travellers on routes into London; it declined after 1800 as roads improved, mounted patrols (the Bow Street Horse Patrol, 1805) increased and banks reduced cash travel. Poaching remained a social crime: the 1723 Black Act created around 50 capital poaching offences after armed poachers blacked their faces to raid deer parks, but many communities still saw poaching as a right, not a crime, especially after enclosure removed common land. Smuggling grew hugely because high duties on tea, spirits and tobacco made it profitable; gangs such as the Hawkhurst Gang operated on the south coast. All three show 'social crime': ordinary people often supported offenders against unpopular laws that protected the property of the rich.

#### 10.3.1b Changing definitions of crime: the end of witchcraft prosecutions; the Tolpuddle Martyrs
The Witchcraft Act of 1736 abolished witchcraft as a crime in England and made claiming magical powers a minor offence instead: the Enlightenment's emphasis on reason and science, plus more scientific and legal scepticism, ended two centuries of prosecutions. In 1834, six Dorset farm labourers formed a trade union (the Friendly Society of Agricultural Labourers) to protest wage cuts. They were arrested not for unionising, which was legal, but for swearing a secret oath under the Unlawful Oaths Act of 1797, a law aimed at mutiny; they were transported to Australia for seven years. This shows the state defining new crimes to suppress a genuine grievance: growing agricultural unemployment and falling wages. Public protest secured pardons in 1836, and the case became a landmark in trade union history.

#### 10.3.2a Law enforcement: the Fielding brothers, development of police forces, beginning of CID
Henry Fielding, a Bow Street magistrate, founded the Bow Street Runners around 1749 as London's first paid, professional thief-takers; his blind half-brother John Fielding expanded them and created a 'Flying Squad' and a circulated list of wanted suspects. Industrialisation, urban growth and fear of disorder (riots such as the 1780 Gordon Riots) pushed reformers to argue that voluntary, unpaid parish policing could not cope. Robert Peel's Metropolitan Police Act of 1829 created London's first full-time, salaried, uniformed police force of around 3,200 constables under two commissioners. Other cities and counties gradually copied it (County and Borough Police Act 1856 made police forces compulsory nationwide). In 1878 the Metropolitan Police formed the Criminal Investigation Department (CID) of plain-clothes detectives to investigate crime rather than simply patrol.

#### 10.3.2b Purpose of punishment: transportation, public execution, Bloody Code, prison reform (Howard, Fry)
By 1800 over 200 offences carried the death penalty (the Bloody Code), but juries increasingly refused to convict for minor thefts, so Parliament progressively repealed capital statutes from the 1820s to 1861, when only murder, treason and a handful of others remained. Public executions, meant to deter through spectacle, were abolished in 1868 after crowds treated hangings as entertainment; executions moved inside prison walls. Transportation, mainly to Australia, dealt with the offenders juries would still convict; it ended in 1868 as colonies objected and prisons became a credible alternative. John Howard's 1777 'The State of the Prisons' exposed filth, disease and mixing of the innocent with the hardened, and Elizabeth Fry, from 1813, campaigned for separating men and women, education and religious instruction at Newgate, shifting official thinking from pure deterrence towards reform.

#### 10.3.3a Case study: Pentonville prison, mid nineteenth century - the separate system
Pentonville Prison opened in 1842 in north London, purpose-built to apply the 'separate system': each of its roughly 520 prisoners lived, worked, exercised and worshipped almost entirely alone, in individual cells, with contact between prisoners deliberately prevented (even chapel had separate stalls, and exercise yards had partitions) and masks or hoods sometimes used when prisoners were moved. The system was designed by reformers who believed isolation, combined with religious instruction, hard labour and reflection, would make prisoners genuinely penitent and reform their character, rather than simply punishing or displaying them as earlier public punishments had done. It reflected the shift, following Howard and Fry's campaigning, from deterrence and retribution towards a belief that prison itself, properly designed, could reform. In practice, prolonged isolation caused serious mental health problems for many prisoners, and the strict separate system was gradually relaxed in later decades.

#### 10.3.3b Key individual: Robert Peel - penal reform and the Metropolitan Police
Robert Peel, Home Secretary in the 1820s and later Prime Minister, made two major, connected contributions to crime and punishment. As Home Secretary he reformed the criminal law by reducing the number of capital offences (the Bloody Code) as part of a wider shift towards more proportionate, consistently enforced punishment, and reformed prisons through the Gaols Act 1823, setting minimum standards for prisoner treatment, inspection and gaoler pay. His most famous achievement was the Metropolitan Police Act of 1829, creating London's first full-time, salaried, uniformed police force, deliberately designed to prevent crime through visible patrolling rather than only respond after the fact; officers were nicknamed 'bobbies' or 'peelers' after him. Peel's combined legacy of penal moderation and professional, preventive policing marks a decisive turning point that the specification treats as a named key individual case study.
