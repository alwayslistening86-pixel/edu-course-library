# S08_Whitechapel_Context_and_Policing - Lesson: Whitechapel, c1870-c1900: environment, population and policing

## Goal
The learner can describe housing and overcrowding in Whitechapel, its population and migration, how H Division policed the area, how the Jack the Ripper investigation was conducted, and the national context of policing and public attitudes.

## Syllabus items taught here
- 10.W1a - Whitechapel: local context - housing, overcrowding, Peabody Estate, workhouses, environment and crime: Describe the environment of Whitechapel and links to crime
- 10.W1b - Whitechapel: inhabitants - employment, poverty, lodging houses, pubs, migration: Describe the population and migration
- 10.W1c - Whitechapel: organisation of policing - beat constable, H Division, rookeries, alleys and courts, crimes and antisocial behaviour: Describe the policing of Whitechapel and its problems
- 10.W1d - Whitechapel: investigative policing, Jack the Ripper, media, Vigilance Committee: Explain investigation methods and the Ripper case
- 10.W1e - Whitechapel: national and regional context - the Met, recruits, CID, Home Secretary, Sir Charles Warren, public attitudes: Place Whitechapel in the national policing context

## How to teach this
Open with: 'You are a police constable starting a beat in Whitechapel in 1888. What problems would you face that a constable in a wealthy suburb would not?' Build from housing, crowding and alleys towards policing difficulty.

#### 10.W1a Whitechapel: local context - housing, overcrowding, Peabody Estate, workhouses, environment and crime
Whitechapel, in London's East End, was one of the poorest and most overcrowded districts in Victorian Britain. Much housing was in a poor state: slum courts and lodging houses, with families often sharing single rooms and little sanitation, and 'rookeries' (dense networks of narrow streets, alleys and courts) that were dark, unlit and hard for outsiders, including police, to navigate. Philanthropic housing schemes, notably the Peabody Trust's Peabody Estate (built from 1881), tried to improve conditions with better-built blocks of flats for the 'respectable' poor, but rents excluded the poorest, so slums persisted alongside them. Whitechapel Union Workhouse housed those unable to support themselves in harsh, deliberately unattractive conditions. Poverty, poor housing and dense, maze-like streets all made crime easier to commit and harder to detect.

#### 10.W1b Whitechapel: inhabitants - employment, poverty, lodging houses, pubs, migration
Whitechapel's population was overwhelmingly poor and casually employed: many worked in the docks, in the 'sweated trades' (tailoring, matchmaking, furniture-making) for very low pay, or, for some women, in casual and precarious ways including prostitution, driven by extreme poverty. Common lodging houses ('doss houses') let a bed for a few pence a night to the very poor and transient; pubs and gin palaces were central to social life and often the last place victims were seen. Migration reshaped the area: a large Irish community had settled from the mid-nineteenth century, and from the 1880s Jewish immigrants fleeing persecution (pogroms) in the Russian Empire arrived in large numbers, especially after 1881, bringing new trades and religious life but also facing prejudice and, at times, being wrongly blamed for crime.

#### 10.W1c Whitechapel: organisation of policing - beat constable, H Division, rookeries, alleys and courts, crimes and antisocial behaviour
Whitechapel was policed by the Metropolitan Police's H Division, chronically understaffed and undertrained relative to the area's size and problems, patrolling fixed beats on foot, alone, largely at night. The tangled rookeries, alleys and courts made effective patrolling and pursuit extremely difficult, and offered easy escape routes for offenders; poor street lighting worsened this. Crime included theft, robbery, drunkenness and violent, antisocial behaviour connected to poverty, overcrowding and heavy drinking, alongside prostitution, which was itself not illegal but associated street activity often was. Community relations were strained: an impoverished, transient and partly immigrant population was often mistrustful of, and sometimes hostile to, the police, and H Division's resources could not match the scale of the area's problems, which shaped how effectively (or not) it could respond to the Ripper murders.

#### 10.W1d Whitechapel: investigative policing, Jack the Ripper, media, Vigilance Committee
From August to November 1888 an unidentified killer, dubbed 'Jack the Ripper' by the press, murdered at least five women (the 'canonical five': Mary Ann Nichols, Annie Chapman, Elizabeth Stride, Catherine Eddowes and Mary Jane Kelly) in and around Whitechapel, with extreme mutilation. CID detectives used still-developing methods: house-to-house enquiries, interviewing witnesses, examining wounds for clues to the killer's possible anatomical knowledge, and circulating descriptions, but lacked fingerprinting or forensic science, and no one was ever charged. Sensationalist newspaper coverage spread fear and criticised the police's failure to catch the killer, while letters claiming to be from the murderer (such as the 'Dear Boss' letter) added to the panic, though most are now thought hoaxes. Local residents formed the Whitechapel Vigilance Committee to patrol streets and offer a reward, reflecting a loss of public confidence in police protection.

#### 10.W1e Whitechapel: national and regional context - the Met, recruits, CID, Home Secretary, Sir Charles Warren, public attitudes
The Metropolitan Police, founded in 1829, covered all of London including Whitechapel, but recruitment and retention were difficult: pay was low, hours long and the work unglamorous, so many recruits were inexperienced and turnover was high. CID, created in 1878, was still a young, relatively small detective branch when the Ripper murders occurred, and coordination between uniformed and plain-clothes branches was imperfect. Sir Charles Warren, Metropolitan Police Commissioner from 1886, faced criticism for his handling of the case (including ordering graffiti connected to the murders washed away before it could be photographed) and for earlier heavy-handed policing of the 1887 'Bloody Sunday' protest, and resigned in November 1888. The Home Secretary answered to Parliament for police failures. Public attitudes mixed fear, fascination and growing anger at social conditions the case exposed.
