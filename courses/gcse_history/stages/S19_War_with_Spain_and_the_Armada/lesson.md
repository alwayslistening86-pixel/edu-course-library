# S19_War_with_Spain_and_the_Armada - Lesson: Early Elizabethan England: outbreak of war and the Spanish Armada

## Goal
The learner can explain Drake's privateering, the outbreak of war in the Netherlands, the raid on Cadiz, the course of the Armada campaign, and the reasons for the English victory.

## Syllabus items taught here
- B4.2.2b - Elizabeth: privateering and the activities of Drake: Explain the significance of privateering
- B4.2.3a - Elizabeth: outbreak of war 1585-88 - the Netherlands and Robert Dudley: Explain deteriorating relations
- B4.2.3b - Elizabeth: Drake and the raid on Cadiz, 1587: Explain 'singeing the King of Spain's beard'
- B4.2.4a - Elizabeth: Spanish invasion plans and the key events of the Armada: Narrate the Armada campaign
- B4.2.4b - Elizabeth: reasons for the English victory: Explain and rank reasons for victory

## How to teach this
Open with: 'In 1588 Spain sent the largest fleet Europe had ever seen against tiny England, and lost. Was that luck, English skill, or Spanish mistakes?' Keep the question open until the stage's final item.

#### B4.2.2b Elizabeth: privateering and the activities of Drake
Privateers were privately owned ships licensed (or unofficially tolerated) by the crown to raid enemy shipping, letting Elizabeth profit from and weaken Spain without formally declaring war. Sir Francis Drake became the most famous English privateer: he raided Spanish ports and treasure ships in the Caribbean during the 1570s, and from 1577-80 became the first Englishman to circumnavigate the globe, seizing huge amounts of Spanish treasure en route (covered in full in the exploration stage). His raids directly damaged Spanish finances and prestige, provoked Spanish fury and demands for compensation or punishment, and gave Elizabeth deniability, since she could claim, not always convincingly, that privateers acted without full royal authority, while quietly sharing in the profits.

#### B4.2.3a Elizabeth: outbreak of war 1585-88 - the Netherlands and Robert Dudley
The Protestant Dutch had been rebelling against Spanish (Catholic) rule in the Netherlands for years; Elizabeth, wary of provoking Spain but equally wary of a fully Spanish-controlled Netherlands just across the Channel, signed the Treaty of Nonsuch (1585), agreeing to send troops and money to support the Dutch rebels. Robert Dudley, Earl of Leicester, a long-time favourite of Elizabeth, led the English expeditionary force to the Netherlands from 1585, though the campaign achieved limited military success and strained relations with the Dutch over Leicester's assumption of excessive authority. This open military support for Spain's rebellious subjects was a major step towards outright war, since Philip II now had clear cause to see England as a direct military enemy rather than merely a rival.

#### B4.2.3b Elizabeth: Drake and the raid on Cadiz, 1587
With Spain visibly preparing an invasion fleet, Drake led a pre-emptive raid on the Spanish port of Cadiz in April 1587, destroying or capturing dozens of ships and vast quantities of supplies intended for the Armada, an action Drake himself famously described as 'singeing the King of Spain's beard'. The raid significantly delayed Spanish invasion preparations, forcing Philip II to postpone the Armada's planned departure by roughly a year, giving England valuable extra time to strengthen its own defences and fleet. It demonstrated both Drake's aggressive, opportunistic style of naval warfare and England's willingness to strike first, and is used on the specification as clear evidence of how privateering and formal strategy overlapped in Elizabethan naval policy.

#### B4.2.4a Elizabeth: Spanish invasion plans and the key events of the Armada
Philip II's plan for the 1588 Armada was for a huge fleet (around 130 ships) to sail up the Channel, link with the Duke of Parma's army waiting in the Spanish Netherlands, and escort an invasion force across to England. The English fleet, using faster, more manoeuvrable ships and longer-range guns under commanders including Lord Howard of Effingham, Drake and Hawkins, harried the Armada up the Channel without a decisive battle. At Calais, the English sent fireships into the anchored Spanish fleet at night, forcing panicked captains to cut anchor and scatter, breaking the planned link-up with Parma. A partial battle followed off Gravelines, after which storms ('the Protestant wind') drove the scattered Spanish fleet north around Scotland and Ireland, wrecking many ships, so barely half the Armada limped home.

#### B4.2.4b Elizabeth: reasons for the English victory
Historians and the specification point to several combined reasons. English ships were generally faster, more manoeuvrable and carried longer-range guns, favouring hit-and-run tactics over close boarding, which Spain's larger, more troop-laden ships were better suited to. English commanders (Howard, Drake, Hawkins) showed skilful, aggressive leadership, including the decisive fireship attack at Calais. Spanish planning had weaknesses: the Armada depended on a difficult and ultimately failed link-up with Parma's land army, and communication between the fleet and shore was poor. Finally, exceptionally severe storms during the fleet's long retreat around Scotland and Ireland caused more Spanish losses than combat did. A strong answer ranks these factors rather than listing them, arguing, for instance, that tactics created the initial disruption but weather turned defeat into disaster.
