# S29_Nazi_Youth_Policy_and_the_Economy - Lesson: Weimar and Nazi Germany: youth, education and the economy

## Goal
The learner can explain aims and policies towards young people including the Hitler Youth and League of German Maidens, explain Nazi control of education, assess policies to reduce unemployment, and assess workers' standard of living under the Nazis.

## Syllabus items taught here
- 31.4.2a - Life in Nazi Germany: aims and policies towards the young - Hitler Youth, League of German Maidens: Explain youth organisations
- 31.4.2b - Life in Nazi Germany: control of the young through education - curriculum and teachers: Explain Nazi education
- 31.4.3a - Life in Nazi Germany: reducing unemployment - labour service, autobahns, rearmament, invisible unemployment: Assess Nazi economic policy
- 31.4.3b - Life in Nazi Germany: standard of living - Labour Front, Strength Through Joy, Beauty of Labour: Assess workers' living standards

## How to teach this
Open with: 'The Nazis wanted every young German shaped from school age, and every unemployed adult given work. How far do you think they actually succeeded at both?'

#### 31.4.2a Life in Nazi Germany: aims and policies towards the young - Hitler Youth, League of German Maidens
The Nazis aimed to indoctrinate young people from an early age to secure loyalty for the future and to prepare boys for military service and girls for motherhood. The Hitler Youth (Hitlerjugend), for boys aged roughly 14-18 (with the younger Deutsches Jungvolk below that), and the League of German Maidens (Bund Deutscher Mädel) for girls, offered activities including camping, sport, marching and ideological instruction, blending genuinely popular outdoor activity with systematic indoctrination in Nazi racial and nationalist ideas. Membership became compulsory in practice through the Hitler Youth Law (1936, tightened 1939), and non-membership could disadvantage a family, while refusal or later dissent (as with the Edelweiss Pirates) carried real risk, showing the movement's mix of voluntary appeal and increasing compulsion.

#### 31.4.2b Life in Nazi Germany: control of the young through education - curriculum and teachers
Schools were reshaped to serve Nazi ideology. The curriculum emphasised physical education (to prepare boys especially for military fitness), 'racial science' (teaching pseudo-scientific Nazi ideas about racial hierarchy and antisemitism), and history and German lessons rewritten to glorify Nazi ideology and national struggle, while subjects such as religious education were reduced. Teachers were required to join the Nazi Teachers' League and demonstrate ideological loyalty, and Jewish teachers were dismissed under the 1933 Law for the Restoration of the Professional Civil Service. Textbooks were rewritten along Nazi lines, including explicitly antisemitic material in some subjects, so that education became a deliberate, systematic tool for shaping children's beliefs from the earliest years, working alongside the Hitler Youth outside school hours.

#### 31.4.3a Life in Nazi Germany: reducing unemployment - labour service, autobahns, rearmament, invisible unemployment
The Nazis reduced official unemployment dramatically, from around six million in 1933 to very low levels by the late 1930s, through several measures. The National Labour Service (Reichsarbeitsdienst) put young men to work on public projects, often manual labour such as land reclamation. Major public works, especially the autobahn motorway construction programme from 1933, created substantial employment and boosted national pride and mobility. From the mid-1930s, rearmament (building up the army, navy and air force in defiance of Versailles) created large numbers of jobs in industry. However, official figures concealed 'invisible unemployment': women pushed out of the workforce and not counted as unemployed, Jews dismissed from jobs and excluded from statistics, and men conscripted into the army or labour service, so real economic improvement, while genuine in scale, was somewhat exaggerated by the headline figures.

#### 31.4.3b Life in Nazi Germany: standard of living - Labour Front, Strength Through Joy, Beauty of Labour
With independent trade unions banned, the German Labour Front (Deutsche Arbeitsfront) replaced them as the only permitted body representing (and controlling) workers, setting wages and conditions with little genuine worker bargaining power, and wages for many workers rose only modestly while hours sometimes increased, especially as rearmament accelerated demands on industry. To offset this and build loyalty, the Labour Front ran the Strength Through Joy (Kraft durch Freude) scheme, subsidising leisure activities, concerts, holidays and cheap cruises for workers, and the Beauty of Labour (Schönheit der Arbeit) programme improved factory conditions such as lighting, canteens and washing facilities. These schemes won genuine popularity among many workers even as real wages and independent bargaining power were curtailed, illustrating the regime's characteristic mix of tangible benefit and reduced freedom.
