# S25_Depression_and_Hitlers_Rise_to_Power - Lesson: Weimar and Nazi Germany: the Depression and Hitler's rise to power, 1929-33

## Goal
The learner can explain the causes and impact of unemployment and the growth of the Communist Party, explain reasons for growth in Nazi support, explain the 1932 elections and Hitler's appointment as Chancellor, and analyse interpretations of the Weimar Republic and Hitler's rise.

## Syllabus items taught here
- 31.2.3a - Hitler: unemployment, its causes and impact; growth of the Communist Party: Explain the Depression's political effects
- 31.2.3b - Hitler: reasons for growth in Nazi support - appeal, propaganda, SA: Explain Nazi electoral growth
- 31.2.4a - Hitler: 1932 elections and how Hitler became Chancellor - Hindenburg and von Papen: Explain the appointment of January 1933
- 31.INT1 - Historians' interpretations: the Weimar Republic and Hitler's rise (Kolb, Peukert, Evans, Turner, Kershaw): Analyse and evaluate interpretations of Weimar and the rise of the Nazis

## How to teach this
Open with: 'In 1928 the Nazis won under 3% of the vote. By 1932 they were the largest party in the Reichstag. What single event in between explains most of that change?'

#### 31.2.3a Hitler: unemployment, its causes and impact; growth of the Communist Party
The Wall Street Crash (October 1929) caused American banks to recall the short-term loans that had underpinned Weimar's recovery, triggering a severe German economic crisis: industrial production collapsed, businesses failed, and unemployment rose from around 1.3 million in 1929 to over six million by early 1933, roughly a third of the workforce. Chancellor Brüning's government responded with spending cuts and tax rises (deflationary policy) that worsened hardship for many, deepening public anger at mainstream parties and parliamentary government generally. The crisis also boosted support for the Communist Party (KPD), which offered a radical alternative and grew significantly in this period, in turn frightening many middle-class and business voters, who increasingly saw the Nazis as a firmer bulwark against a feared communist revolution.

#### 31.2.3b Hitler: reasons for growth in Nazi support - appeal, propaganda, SA
Nazi support grew rapidly from 1929 for several combined reasons. The party's message adapted to different fears and hopes: promising work and dignity to the unemployed, order and anti-communism to the middle class and business, and national revival and an end to Versailles' humiliation to nationalists generally, a broad, adaptable appeal that other parties struggled to match. Skilful, modern propaganda, including Joseph Goebbels' organisation of mass rallies, posters and Hitler's own dramatic, emotionally charged speaking tours (using aeroplane travel to cover many cities rapidly, the 'Hitler over Germany' campaign), presented the Nazis as dynamic and decisive against a Republic seen as weak and divided. The SA's visible, disciplined presence suggested order and strength to some voters, even as its street violence alarmed others, and Nazi Reichstag seats rose from 12 in 1928 to 230 in July 1932.

#### 31.2.4a Hitler: 1932 elections and how Hitler became Chancellor - Hindenburg and von Papen
Two Reichstag elections in 1932 (July and November) confirmed the Nazis as the largest single party, though never with an outright majority, while political instability continued as coalition governments repeatedly failed and President Hindenburg ruled increasingly by Article 48 emergency decree. Franz von Papen, a conservative former Chancellor who had lost Hindenburg's confidence, schemed to regain power by persuading the elderly Hindenburg that Hitler could be made Chancellor but controlled and restrained by conservative ministers surrounding him in Cabinet, including von Papen himself as Vice-Chancellor, believing Hitler could be used and then discarded. Hindenburg, reluctantly and only after this conservative persuasion, appointed Hitler Chancellor on 30 January 1933, a legal, constitutional appointment gained through backstairs political manoeuvring rather than election victory alone or violent seizure of power.

#### 31.INT1 Historians' interpretations: the Weimar Republic and Hitler's rise (Kolb, Peukert, Evans, Turner, Kershaw)
Historians disagree on how far Weimar's collapse was inevitable. Some, following earlier pessimistic readings, argue the Republic was fatally flawed from birth (an unstable constitution, hostile elites, and a population that never fully accepted democracy), so its failure was close to inevitable; this view is associated with older syntheses drawing on work such as Eberhard Kolb's survey history. Others, including Detlev Peukert and Ian Kershaw, stress specific contingent factors: the Depression's severity, the choices of conservative elites (Hindenburg, von Papen) who preferred Hitler to a strengthened left, and Hitler's own political skill and propaganda, arguing collapse was not predetermined but resulted from a combination of structural weakness and particular decisions in a genuine crisis. Henry Ashby Turner and Richard Evans likewise emphasise the crucial, avoidable role of elite miscalculation in January 1933, rather than treating Nazi success as simply the inevitable outcome of German history or culture.
