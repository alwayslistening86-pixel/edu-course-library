# S05_Economy_Society_Culture_and_Interpretations - Lesson: Trade; the changing structure of society; cultural change; the 1589-1603 'crisis of government' debate

## Goal
The learner explains patterns of trade, the changing structure of society (population growth, monastic closure, enclosure, urbanisation, professional classes), cultural change (education, printing, religion, patronage, the arts), and evaluates the historiographical debate over whether there was a 'general crisis of government' in 1589-1603.

## Syllabus items taught here
- 1B.4.1 - Patterns of domestic and foreign trade
- 1B.4.2 - The changing structure of society, 1509-88
- 1B.4.3 - Cultural change: education, printing, religion, patronage, the 'cult of Gloriana'
- 1B.INT - Historical interpretations: was there a general crisis of government, 1589-1603?

## How to teach this
Ask: which changed English society more by 1600 - the closure of the monasteries or the growth of the population? Teach each key topic as: what happened (facts, names, dates, precise detail examiners reward), then why it matters (analysis: cause, consequence, change, significance), then how historians read it (interpretations, where relevant). Push the learner to write timed, planned answers regularly, not just to recall facts: Edexcel's essays are marked by levels that reward explained, weighed argument reaching a supported judgement, not narrative or a list of points. Historical facts here (names, dates, figures) do not change with time in the way current-affairs content would, but historians' interpretations do develop; treat the interpretations content as a live debate, not a settled answer.

#### 1B.4.1 Patterns of domestic and foreign trade
**Trade.** The wool and cloth trade dominated English exports throughout the period; from the 1560s, 'new draperies' (lighter, cheaper cloths, often introduced by Protestant refugee weavers from the Low Countries fleeing Spanish persecution) diversified and revived the textile industry as the older heavy-broadcloth trade faced difficulty. London grew as the dominant national market and port, channelling an ever-larger share of trade and drawing wealth and population from the regions. Exploration and overseas trade expanded from mid-century (Muscovy Company 1555, later ventures towards the Americas and Asia), though most Tudor trade remained European, especially with Antwerp until its decline (from the 1560s-70s, owing to war in the Netherlands).

#### 1B.4.2 The changing structure of society, 1509-88
**The changing structure of society.** Population roughly doubled across the sixteenth century (from c.2.3 million to c.4 million+), the fundamental driver of falling real wages, rising poverty and rising land hunger/rents. The dissolution of the monasteries (1536-40) removed a major landowner and charitable/educational provider, transferring land into lay hands (often minor gentry, who became a newly enriched and enlarged class with a stake in the religious settlement) and ending monastic almsgiving and hospitality relied on by some poor. Enclosure (converting open arable land to enclosed pasture, chiefly for sheep) accelerated in places, provoking resentment where it caused depopulation and loss of common rights (a central grievance in Kett's Rebellion, 1549), though its extent and impact varied greatly by region. Urbanisation grew, above all in London (from c.50,000 in 1500 to over 200,000 by 1600). A growing professional/middling class (lawyers, officials, merchants, better-off yeomen) expanded, benefiting from Crown service, land purchase and trade.

#### 1B.4.3 Cultural change: education, printing, religion, patronage, the 'cult of Gloriana'
**Cultural change.** Grammar schools and universities expanded significantly (partly refounded using ex-monastic endowments, partly new Elizabethan foundations), widening access to Latin-based education among the gentry and middling sort and feeding the growing professional classes. The printing press (established in England by Caxton from 1476) transformed the spread of ideas: it was essential to spreading Reformation arguments (Tyndale's Bible, Protestant tracts) as well as royal propaganda and, later, the printed Book of Common Prayer. Religious change reshaped culture directly: parish churches were stripped of images, shrines and much decorative art under Edward and again after 1559; a more word- and sermon-centred piety replaced much visual and ritual religion. Royal and noble patronage drove a flowering of drama (the public playhouses from the 1570s-90s, and playwrights including Shakespeare and Marlowe by the 1590s), music (composers such as Tallis and Byrd, who remained Catholic yet retained royal patronage) and poetry (Spenser's *The Faerie Queene*, an explicit celebration of Elizabeth). The 'cult of Gloriana' - the deliberate cultivation of Elizabeth's image as the Virgin Queen, Astraea, Belphoebe - used portraiture, pageantry, poetry and progress to compensate propagandistically for the perceived vulnerabilities of unmarried female rule.

#### 1B.INT Historical interpretations: was there a general crisis of government, 1589-1603?
**Historical interpretations: was there a 'general crisis of government' in Elizabeth's last years, 1589-1603?** Historians disagree sharply. Case for crisis: national security threats from Spain (the Armada, 1588, was followed by renewed Spanish attempts and support for Ireland) and from Ireland (Tyrone's Rebellion/Nine Years' War, 1594-1603, was hugely costly and difficult); growing faction at court as ageing courtiers (Burghley, then his son Robert Cecil, versus the volatile Earl of Essex) competed bitterly for influence, culminating in Essex's failed rebellion (1601); an unresolved succession question (Elizabeth never named an heir) bred anxiety; the parliamentary session of 1601 saw unusually sharp Commons criticism of royal monopolies, forcing a rare climbdown ('Golden Speech'); repeated harvest failures in the mid-1590s caused severe social distress, dearth and some renewed disorder. Case against/for continuity: the regime survived every one of these challenges without a successful rebellion or invasion; the succession passed peacefully to James VI/I in 1603; Elizabeth's basic political skill and the settled machinery of conciliar/local government (built up over four decades) proved resilient. Historians such as Christopher Haigh have stressed real strain and decline in the 1590s, while others argue the regime's fundamental stability was never seriously in doubt; a strong answer weighs the severity of each strand of evidence rather than simply listing problems.
