# S09_Spread_Survival_and_Exam_Technique - Lesson: Failed negotiations; the princes and the Schmalkaldic League; Charles V and the Peace of Augsburg; the Papacy's response

## Goal
The learner explains the failure of Lutheran-Catholic negotiations (Diets of Speyer 1526/1529, Regensburg 1541), the role of converted princes and the Schmalkaldic League (1531), Charles V's competing priorities and the Schmalkaldic War, the Peace of Passau (1552) and Peace of Augsburg (1555), and the Papacy's response including Paul III and the summoning of the Council of Trent; and applies Paper 2 essay technique.

## How to teach this
Ask: why might a Catholic Emperor who won a war against the Lutheran princes (1547) still end up legally tolerating Lutheranism by 1555? Teach each key topic as: what happened (facts, names, dates, precise detail examiners reward), then why it matters (analysis: cause, consequence, change, significance), then how historians read it (interpretations, where relevant). Push the learner to write timed, planned answers regularly, not just to recall facts: Edexcel's essays are marked by levels that reward explained, weighed argument reaching a supported judgement, not narrative or a list of points. Historical facts here (names, dates, figures) do not change with time in the way current-affairs content would, but historians' interpretations do develop; treat the interpretations content as a live debate, not a settled answer.

#### 2B1.KT4.1 Failure of Lutheran-Catholic negotiations: Speyer 1526/1529, Regensburg 1541
**Failure of negotiations.** Catholic and Lutheran positions diverged too far for compromise across several attempted settlements. The first Diet of Speyer (1526), with Charles V distracted by war with France and the Ottomans, effectively let each territory decide its own religious policy pending a General Council - a pragmatic pause that let Lutheranism consolidate. The second Diet of Speyer (1529) reversed this, reasserting Catholic uniformity; Lutheran princes formally 'protested' (the origin of the term 'Protestant'), showing negotiation had broken down into confrontation. The Diet/Colloquy of Regensburg (1541), a serious late attempt at doctrinal compromise (with moderates on both sides seeking common ground on justification), ultimately failed over unresolved differences on the Eucharist, the papacy and other issues, confirming that a negotiated religious reunification of Germany was not achievable.

#### 2B1.KT4.2 The role of the German princes and the Schmalkaldic League, 1531
**The role of the German princes.** Frederick the Wise's protection of Luther from 1521 remained foundational. Key princely conversions to Lutheranism - Albrecht of Hohenzollern (who secularised the Teutonic Order's Prussian lands into a Lutheran duchy, 1525), Philip of Hesse, and John of Saxony (Frederick's successor) - gave the movement powerful territorial protectors with real armies and treasuries, not just individual believers. In 1531 Lutheran princes and cities formed the Schmalkaldic League, a defensive military and political alliance explicitly to protect Lutheran territories against forced Catholic reimposition; its formation transformed Lutheranism from a scattered set of territorial movements into an organised, armed political bloc that Charles V had to treat as a serious power, not merely a religious dispute.

#### 2B1.KT4.3 Charles V's competing priorities, the Schmalkaldic War, and the Peace of Augsburg, 1555
**Charles V and Lutheranism.** Charles V's attention and resources were persistently divided between Germany, near-constant war with France (rival Valois-Habsburg dynastic conflict), and the Ottoman threat to his eastern and Mediterranean territories (including the siege of Vienna, 1529) - this repeatedly forced him to compromise or delay action against the Lutherans in order to secure German support/troops against more urgent external threats. Even many Catholic princes gave Charles only limited support against the Schmalkaldic League, wary of increased imperial power over princely rights regardless of the religious question. Charles finally defeated the League militarily in the Schmalkaldic War (1546-47, winning decisively at Mühlberg), but could not translate battlefield victory into a lasting religious settlement: renewed war (led by a defecting Lutheran prince, Maurice of Saxony) forced Charles to accept the Peace of Passau (1552, a truce restoring Lutheran rights) and finally the Peace of Augsburg (1555), which established the principle *cuius regio, eius religio* ('whose realm, his religion') - each prince could choose Lutheranism or Catholicism for his territory - giving Lutheranism its first permanent legal recognition within the Empire, though excluding other Protestant groups (e.g. Calvinists) from its terms.

#### 2B1.KT4.4 The Papacy's response: Paul III and the summoning of the Council of Trent
**The Papacy and Lutheranism.** The early papal response (under Leo X) was slow and underestimated the threat; subsequent popes struggled to coordinate an effective Catholic response partly because a general council (which many, including some cardinals, saw as necessary to address abuses and define doctrine clearly) was repeatedly delayed by political rivalries between the Papacy, France and the Emperor, each fearing a council might weaken their own position. Pope Paul III (from 1534) took reform more seriously, commissioning the *Consilium de Emendanda Ecclesia* (1537), a critical internal report on Church corruption, and finally summoned the Council of Trent, which opened in 1545 - beginning the Catholic (Counter-)Reformation's doctrinal and institutional response, though far too late to prevent Lutheranism's legal establishment in Germany by 1555.
