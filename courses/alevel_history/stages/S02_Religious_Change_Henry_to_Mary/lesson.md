# S02_Religious_Change_Henry_to_Mary - Lesson: Henry VIII and the break with Rome; Edward VI and Mary I; early Protestant influence and puritanism

## Goal
The learner explains Henry VIII's break with Rome and the end of papal power, religious change under Edward VI and reversal under Mary, and early Protestant influence in England including Anne Boleyn's significance.

## Syllabus items taught here
- 1B.2.1 - Henry VIII and the end of papal power in England
- 1B.2.2 - Religious changes under Edward VI and Mary I
- 1B.2.3 - Protestant influence, Anne Boleyn's significance, and the growth of puritanism

## How to teach this
Ask: could Henry VIII have got his annulment without breaking from Rome? What made the pope say no? Teach each key topic as: what happened (facts, names, dates, precise detail examiners reward), then why it matters (analysis: cause, consequence, change, significance), then how historians read it (interpretations, where relevant). Push the learner to write timed, planned answers regularly, not just to recall facts: Edexcel's essays are marked by levels that reward explained, weighed argument reaching a supported judgement, not narrative or a list of points. Historical facts here (names, dates, figures) do not change with time in the way current-affairs content would, but historians' interpretations do develop; treat the interpretations content as a live debate, not a settled answer.

#### 1B.2.1 Henry VIII and the end of papal power in England
**Henry VIII and the end of papal power.** Henry sought an annulment of his marriage to Catherine of Aragon (no surviving son; his conscience troubled by Leviticus) from the late 1520s; Pope Clement VII, under the influence/control of Catherine's nephew Emperor Charles V after the Sack of Rome (1527), would not grant it. Wolsey's failure to secure it via legatine court (1529) led to his fall. The 'Reformation Parliament' (1529-36), driven by Thomas Cromwell, dismantled papal authority in England by statute: Act in Restraint of Appeals (1533) ended appeals to Rome; Act of Supremacy (1534) made Henry 'Supreme Head of the Church of England'; dissolution of the monasteries (1536-40) transferred vast wealth and land to the Crown and to those who bought it, creating a class with a vested interest in preventing a Catholic restoration. Doctrinally Henry remained largely conservative (Act of Six Articles, 1539, upheld transubstantiation and clerical celibacy): this was a break in institutional authority, not a full doctrinal Reformation.

#### 1B.2.2 Religious changes under Edward VI and Mary I
**Religious change under Edward and Mary.** Edward VI (regents Somerset, then Northumberland, and Archbishop Cranmer) oversaw a genuine Protestant Reformation: chantries dissolved (1547), clergy allowed to marry, images and altars removed, the Book of Common Prayer introduced (1549, revised in a more Protestant direction 1552), the Forty-Two Articles (1553) set out Protestant doctrine. This provoked resistance, notably the Western (Prayer Book) Rebellion of 1549. Mary I reversed this decisively: restored papal authority (1554, with Cardinal Pole as legate), restored Catholic doctrine and practice, and pursued heresy prosecutions that burned around 280 Protestants (the 'Marian persecutions', later mythologised in Foxe's *Book of Martyrs*), which arguably hardened rather than eliminated Protestant sympathy. Mary's unpopular marriage to Philip II of Spain (1554) triggered Wyatt's Rebellion and coloured contemporary and later views of her reign.

#### 1B.2.3 Protestant influence, Anne Boleyn's significance, and the growth of puritanism
**Protestant influences and puritanism.** Protestant ideas reached England from the 1520s via Lutheran and later Calvinist writings, William Tyndale's English New Testament (smuggled in from the Continent), and returning exiles. Anne Boleyn's significance: her own sympathy for reformist and evangelical ideas and her patronage of reform-minded clergy and texts gave Protestant influence access to the court in the crucial years before the break with Rome, even though her fall (1536) predates full doctrinal Reformation. Protestantism advanced further and more openly under Edward VI. Puritanism (those wanting further reform of the Elizabethan Church towards Calvinist practice) grew in significance during Elizabeth's reign, especially from the 1570s-80s: figures like Thomas Cartwright argued for a presbyterian church structure; the movement caused tension with the Elizabethan settlement's more conservative, episcopal compromise, though most puritans stayed within the Church rather than separating from it.
