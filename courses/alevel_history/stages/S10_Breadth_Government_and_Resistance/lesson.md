# S10_Breadth_Government_and_Resistance - Lesson: Breadth: government methods of control and the typology of rebel grievance, 1485-1603

## Goal
The learner explains, in breadth across 1485-1603, how Tudor governments sought to control the country and the recurring typology of grievance (economic/social, religious, political/dynastic) behind popular resistance, as groundwork for the in-depth case studies that follow.

## Syllabus items taught here
- 31.BREADTH.1 - Breadth: Tudor government methods of control, 1485-1603
- 31.BREADTH.2 - Breadth: the typology of grievance behind Tudor rebellion, 1485-1603

## How to teach this
Ask: list every reason you can think of that might make ordinary sixteenth-century people take up arms against their government. Which of those reasons recur across a whole century? Teach each key topic as: what happened (facts, names, dates, precise detail examiners reward), then why it matters (analysis: cause, consequence, change, significance), then how historians read it (interpretations, where relevant). Push the learner to write timed, planned answers regularly, not just to recall facts: Edexcel's essays are marked by levels that reward explained, weighed argument reaching a supported judgement, not narrative or a list of points. Historical facts here (names, dates, figures) do not change with time in the way current-affairs content would, but historians' interpretations do develop; treat the interpretations content as a live debate, not a settled answer.

#### 31.BREADTH.1 Breadth: Tudor government methods of control, 1485-1603
**Government methods of control, 1485-1603 (breadth).** Across the period, Tudor governments relied on broadly similar tools to maintain order, though their reach and sophistication grew: the nobility and gentry as local power-brokers, expected to enforce royal authority and, if needed, raise armed retainers; regional councils (the Council of the North, refounded and strengthened after 1537) extending central authority into distant or historically unruly regions; Justices of the Peace, whose administrative and judicial role expanded steadily through the century; royal progresses and patronage, binding regional elites to the Crown through visible favour and reward; and, when prevention failed, a combination of negotiation (buying time, offering partial concessions or pardons) followed by military suppression and selective, exemplary punishment of ringleaders once a rising had been contained. Government responses became progressively faster and more effective across the century: Henry VII and the early Henry VIII faced risings with only limited standing military resources and relied heavily on regional magnates; by Elizabeth's reign, lords lieutenant and county militias gave the Crown a far more direct and rapid capacity to mobilise force.

#### 31.BREADTH.2 Breadth: the typology of grievance behind Tudor rebellion, 1485-1603
**The typology of grievance behind rebellion, 1485-1603 (breadth).** Historians studying Tudor rebellions distinguish, while noting real overlap, three broad categories of grievance. Economic and social grievances: resentment of taxation/subsidies, enclosure and loss of common land, rising rents and dues, and hardship from poor harvests - central to Kett's Rebellion (1549) and present as an undercurrent in several others. Religious grievances: opposition to reform (as in the Pilgrimage of Grace, 1536-37, and the Western/Prayer Book Rebellion, 1549) or, later, the desire to restore Catholicism (the Northern Earls' Revolt, 1569-70); religion could unite disparate social groups behind a single cause more effectively than economic grievance alone. Political/dynastic grievances: challenges to the legitimacy of the ruling monarch, from the early pretender risings under Henry VII (Lambert Simnel, Perkin Warbeck) to factional court politics later (Wyatt's Rebellion, 1554, partly dynastic/anti-Spanish; the Earl of Essex's revolt, 1601, court faction turned armed rising; Tyrone's Rebellion, 1594-1603, combining Irish political autonomy with religious and dynastic elements involving Spain). Most major risings in fact combined more than one type of grievance, and assessing *which* mattered most in each case, and how effectively government responded, is the central analytical skill this topic requires.
