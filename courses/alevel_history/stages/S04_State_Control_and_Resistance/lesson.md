# S04_State_Control_and_Resistance - Lesson: Tudor control of the country; the state and the poor; resistance to Tudor rule

## Goal
The learner explains Tudor methods of controlling the country (the Marcher Council, Council of the North, the nobility, justices of the peace, Elizabethan lords lieutenant), causes of poverty and provision for the poor, and the nature and decline of popular resistance to Tudor rule, 1536-88.

## Syllabus items taught here
- 1B.3.1 - Tudor control of the country: regional councils, the nobility, JPs, lords lieutenant
- 1B.3.2 - The growth of poverty and provision for the poor
- 1B.3.3 - Resistance to Tudor rule, 1536-88, and reasons for its decline

## How to teach this
Ask: with no police force and no standing army, how did five Tudor monarchs keep control of a country of several million people? Teach each key topic as: what happened (facts, names, dates, precise detail examiners reward), then why it matters (analysis: cause, consequence, change, significance), then how historians read it (interpretations, where relevant). Push the learner to write timed, planned answers regularly, not just to recall facts: Edexcel's essays are marked by levels that reward explained, weighed argument reaching a supported judgement, not narrative or a list of points. Historical facts here (names, dates, figures) do not change with time in the way current-affairs content would, but historians' interpretations do develop; treat the interpretations content as a live debate, not a settled answer.

#### 1B.3.1 Tudor control of the country: regional councils, the nobility, JPs, lords lieutenant
**Tudor control of the country.** Regional councils extended royal authority into problem areas: the Council in the Marches of Wales oversaw Wales and the border counties; the Council of the North (refounded 1537, after the Pilgrimage of Grace) governed the North under a royal-appointed president, becoming an effective instrument of Tudor authority there. The nobility remained essential local power-brokers, expected to maintain order, raise troops and enforce royal policy in their regions in return for royal favour and office; a monarch who alienated key noble families (as Henry VIII sometimes did through faction) risked regional instability. Justices of the Peace (unpaid local gentry) grew steadily more important through the century, taking on ever more administrative and judicial duties (enforcing statutes, licensing alehouses, administering poor relief) - the crucial 'backbone' of everyday Tudor government. Under Elizabeth, lords lieutenant (a formalised version of an older role) took charge of county militias and defence, tightening the link between central government and local military mobilisation, especially important once war with Spain began (1585).

#### 1B.3.2 The growth of poverty and provision for the poor
**The state and the poor.** Poverty grew across the century owing to sustained population growth (roughly doubling, c.1500-1600), which pushed down real wages and pushed up food prices and rents faster than incomes for the poor; enclosure and enclosure-driven depopulation in some regions; and periodic harvest failure and disease. Vagrancy and 'sturdy beggars' (those judged able-bodied but workless) were treated with escalating severity in early Tudor law (whipping, branding, and even, briefly, under a 1547 statute, enslavement for repeat offenders, soon repealed as unworkable), reflecting fear of disorder as much as compassion. From the 1530s-1601, poor relief gradually became more systematic: local authorities and parishes, supported by compulsory local rates from 1572 and consolidated in the Elizabethan Poor Laws (1597-1601), distinguished the 'deserving' poor (old, sick, orphaned - to be relieved) from the 'undeserving' (able-bodied idle - to be punished or set to work), with churches, guilds and private charity also providing relief alongside this growing state framework.

#### 1B.3.3 Resistance to Tudor rule, 1536-88, and reasons for its decline
**Resistance to Tudor rule, 1536-88.** Popular risings were relatively frequent in the first half of the period and became rarer after 1570. Grievances were rarely purely economic, religious or political in isolation - most risings combined elements: resistance to demands for subsidies/taxation could fuse with religious or local grievances (as in the Amicable Grant resistance of 1525, just before this period, and echoed in 1536). Major risings (the Pilgrimage of Grace 1536-37, the Western Rebellion and Kett's Rebellion 1549, the Northern Earls' Revolt 1569-70) posed real if usually short-lived threats, exploiting regional grievance and (in 1536 and 1569) noble or gentry leadership, but were generally contained by a mixture of negotiation, royal armies and, afterwards, exemplary punishment. Reasons for the decline of popular resistance after 1570: growing government confidence and administrative reach (JPs, lords lieutenant, better-organised county militias); the discrediting and destruction of the last aristocratic leadership capable of organising a large rising (after 1569-70); the psychological and religious effect of decades of Tudor rule normalising obedience; and greater social control through parish and poor-law structures that gave local elites a stake in order.
