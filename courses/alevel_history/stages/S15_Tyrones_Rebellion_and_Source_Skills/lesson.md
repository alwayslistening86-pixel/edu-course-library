# S15_Tyrones_Rebellion_and_Source_Skills - Lesson: Tyrone's Rebellion, 1594-1603, and Paper 3 source-evaluation technique

## Goal
The learner explains the causes, course and consequences of Tyrone's Rebellion (the Nine Years' War, 1594-1603) in Ireland, and applies Paper 3 Section A source-evaluation technique (utility, provenance and own knowledge) to a primary source extract.

## Syllabus items taught here
- 31.CASE.TYRONE - Case study: Tyrone's Rebellion (the Nine Years' War), 1594-1603

## How to teach this
Ask: why might a rebellion in Ireland, far from Whitehall, still be one of the most expensive and dangerous crises of Elizabeth's entire reign? Teach each key topic as: what happened (facts, names, dates, precise detail examiners reward), then why it matters (analysis: cause, consequence, change, significance), then how historians read it (interpretations, where relevant). Push the learner to write timed, planned answers regularly, not just to recall facts: Edexcel's essays are marked by levels that reward explained, weighed argument reaching a supported judgement, not narrative or a list of points. Historical facts here (names, dates, figures) do not change with time in the way current-affairs content would, but historians' interpretations do develop; treat the interpretations content as a live debate, not a settled answer.

#### 31.CASE.TYRONE Case study: Tyrone's Rebellion (the Nine Years' War), 1594-1603
**Tyrone's Rebellion (the Nine Years' War), 1594-1603.** The largest and costliest challenge to English authority under Elizabeth, led by Hugh O'Neill, Earl of Tyrone, in Ulster, combining Gaelic Irish political autonomy, resistance to intensifying English administrative and cultural control (plantation, anglicisation, undermining traditional Gaelic lordship), and Catholic religious solidarity against a Protestant English crown, with direct Spanish military and diplomatic backing (part of the wider Anglo-Spanish war). O'Neill proved a highly capable military and political leader, adopting modern military organisation and tactics and inflicting a major defeat on English forces at the Battle of the Yellow Ford (1598), the worst English military defeat in Ireland during the entire Tudor period, which briefly threatened English control of the whole island. The rebellion dragged on for years, consuming enormous royal expenditure and English manpower at exactly the moment the ageing Elizabeth's government was also managing faction, succession anxiety and the Essex crisis (the Earl of Essex's own failed command against Tyrone in Ireland, 1599, and subsequent disgrace, fed directly into his own rebellion in London in 1601). A Spanish expeditionary force landed at Kinsale (1601) to support O'Neill, but the decisive Battle of Kinsale (1601) ended in a crushing English victory over the combined Irish/Spanish forces, breaking the rebellion's military back. O'Neill formally submitted in the Treaty of Mellifont (1603, ironically signed just days after Elizabeth's death, of which O'Neill was reportedly not yet informed), ending the war on relatively lenient terms but marking the effective destruction of the old Gaelic lordship system and paving the way for far more intensive English/Scottish plantation of Ulster in the following decades.
