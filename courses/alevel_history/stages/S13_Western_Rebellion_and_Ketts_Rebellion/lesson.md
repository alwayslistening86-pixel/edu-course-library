# S13_Western_Rebellion_and_Ketts_Rebellion - Lesson: The Western Rebellion, 1549, and the in-depth study of Kett's Rebellion, 1549

## Goal
The learner explains the causes, course and consequences of the Western (Prayer Book) Rebellion (1549) and, as an in-depth case study, of Kett's Rebellion (1549), including Robert Kett's leadership, the rebels' grievances and demands, and the government's response.

## Syllabus items taught here
- 31.CASE.WESTERN - Case study: the Western (Prayer Book) Rebellion, 1549
- 31.DEPTH.KETT - In-depth study: Kett's Rebellion, 1549

## How to teach this
Ask: two rebellions broke out in England within weeks of each other in summer 1549 - one over religion, one over land. What did that tell Edward VI's government about the state of the country? Teach each key topic as: what happened (facts, names, dates, precise detail examiners reward), then why it matters (analysis: cause, consequence, change, significance), then how historians read it (interpretations, where relevant). Push the learner to write timed, planned answers regularly, not just to recall facts: Edexcel's essays are marked by levels that reward explained, weighed argument reaching a supported judgement, not narrative or a list of points. Historical facts here (names, dates, figures) do not change with time in the way current-affairs content would, but historians' interpretations do develop; treat the interpretations content as a live debate, not a settled answer.

#### 31.CASE.WESTERN Case study: the Western (Prayer Book) Rebellion, 1549
**The Western (Prayer Book) Rebellion, 1549.** Broke out in Devon and Cornwall in direct response to the imposition of Archbishop Cranmer's new English-language Book of Common Prayer (1549), replacing the familiar Latin mass; rebels (particularly in strongly conservative, and in Cornwall partly non-English-speaking, communities) demanded the restoration of the old Latin service and other traditional Catholic practices, articulated in their own list of demands presented to the government. The rising besieged Exeter for over a month; government forces under Lord Russell, reinforced by foreign mercenaries, eventually defeated the rebels (notably at the Battle of Sampford Courtenay, August 1549), with several thousand rebels killed and exemplary executions of leaders (including a vicar hanged from his own church tower) intended to deter further religious resistance during the more thoroughgoing Edwardian Reformation.

#### 31.DEPTH.KETT In-depth study: Kett's Rebellion, 1549
**Kett's Rebellion, 1549 (in-depth study).** Centred on Norfolk, this rising is the classic case study of economic/social grievance, though it also contained wider political criticism of local governance. The core grievance was enclosure - the conversion of common arable and grazing land into enclosed sheep pasture by landlords, which reduced poorer villagers' access to land they depended on for subsistence, compounded by rising rents, food prices and a sense that local gentry and officials were failing to enforce existing anti-enclosure law. Robert Kett, himself a modest local landowner (not a peasant, illustrating that grievance crossed some social lines), took leadership of a spontaneous local protest against enclosures near Wymondham and transformed it into an organised camp of perhaps 16,000 people at Mousehold Heath outside Norwich, where the rebels drew up a detailed list of 29 articles setting out specific grievances (against enclosure, unfair rents, and abuses by landlords and local officials) and administered a form of rough justice/oversight themselves, while consistently professing loyalty to the young King Edward VI, not seeking to overthrow him. The camp captured Norwich (England's second city) after defeating an initial government force under the Marquess of Northampton, but was ultimately crushed by a larger royal army under the Earl of Warwick (John Dudley, later Duke of Northumberland) at the Battle of Dussindale (August 1549), with several thousand rebels killed. Consequences: Kett was executed (hanged from Norwich Castle); the rebellion's scale and the government's alarm contributed to Protector Somerset's political fall shortly afterwards (blamed by rivals for sympathising too readily with rebel grievances and failing to suppress disorder promptly), replaced by Warwick/Northumberland; despite the defeat, the episode kept enclosure a live political and legal issue for the rest of the century.
