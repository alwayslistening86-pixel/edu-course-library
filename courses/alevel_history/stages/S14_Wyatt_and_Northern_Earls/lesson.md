# S14_Wyatt_and_Northern_Earls - Lesson: Wyatt's Rebellion, 1554, and the Northern Earls' Revolt, 1569-70

## Goal
The learner explains the causes, course and consequences of Wyatt's Rebellion (1554) and of the Northern Earls' Revolt (1569-70), and compares political/dynastic and religious motivation across the two risings.

## Syllabus items taught here
- 31.CASE.WYATT - Case study: Wyatt's Rebellion, 1554
- 31.CASE.NORTHERN_EARLS - Case study: the Northern Earls' Revolt, 1569-70

## How to teach this
Ask: what did Mary I's marriage plans and Elizabeth I's religious settlement each threaten strongly enough to provoke armed rebellion? Teach each key topic as: what happened (facts, names, dates, precise detail examiners reward), then why it matters (analysis: cause, consequence, change, significance), then how historians read it (interpretations, where relevant). Push the learner to write timed, planned answers regularly, not just to recall facts: Edexcel's essays are marked by levels that reward explained, weighed argument reaching a supported judgement, not narrative or a list of points. Historical facts here (names, dates, figures) do not change with time in the way current-affairs content would, but historians' interpretations do develop; treat the interpretations content as a live debate, not a settled answer.

#### 31.CASE.WYATT Case study: Wyatt's Rebellion, 1554
**Wyatt's Rebellion, 1554.** Triggered by Mary I's announced marriage to Philip II of Spain (1554), which provoked fears of English subjection to Spanish/Habsburg interests, loss of independence in foreign policy, and (for Protestants) renewed and stronger Catholic religious pressure backed by a powerful foreign Catholic power. Sir Thomas Wyatt the Younger led a rising in Kent, part of a wider, loosely coordinated plan for simultaneous risings in several counties (most of which failed to materialise effectively), combining genuine popular anti-Spanish feeling with some Protestant gentry involvement and a hoped-for (but unproven) link to Princess Elizabeth as an alternative, Protestant figurehead. Wyatt's forces reached London itself before being defeated at the gates of the city (February 1554) when London's citizens did not rise to join him as hoped. Consequences: Wyatt and other leaders were executed; the rebellion's alleged connection to Elizabeth (never proven) placed her under serious suspicion and brief imprisonment in the Tower, though Mary ultimately spared her; the episode hardened Mary's determination to proceed with both the Spanish marriage and firmer religious policy, and is often seen by historians as contributing to the harsher, more repressive character of the rest of her reign.

#### 31.CASE.NORTHERN_EARLS Case study: the Northern Earls' Revolt, 1569-70
**The Northern Earls' Revolt, 1569-70.** The most serious rebellion of Elizabeth's reign, led by the Catholic Earls of Northumberland and Westmorland, aiming to restore Catholicism and, connected to this, to free and potentially marry Mary, Queen of Scots (then held in England after fleeing Scotland) to the Duke of Norfolk, creating an alternative Catholic succession claim - combining religious and dynastic/political motivation inseparably. The Earls raised forces in the North, celebrated Catholic mass at Durham Cathedral in open defiance of the settlement, but failed to attract the wide, sustained support they needed (much of the country, including many Catholic gentry, did not rise), and government forces suppressed the revolt fairly quickly (by early 1570) as it advanced only as far as briefly besieging Barnard Castle before retreating. Consequences: harsh reprisals followed, with several hundred executions ordered across the North as a deterrent - a markedly more severe government response than after earlier Tudor risings, reflecting the seriousness with which Elizabeth's government viewed a Catholic, dynastically-linked threat; the revolt effectively destroyed the last capacity of the English Catholic aristocracy to mount an armed challenge to the regime, and was followed shortly afterwards by the papal bull *Regnans in Excelsis* (1570) excommunicating Elizabeth, which hardened the association between Catholicism and treason for the rest of the reign.
