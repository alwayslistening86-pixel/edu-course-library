# S12_Pilgrimage_of_Grace - Lesson: In-depth study: the Pilgrimage of Grace, 1536-37

## Goal
The learner explains, as an in-depth case study, the causes (religious, economic and political), course and consequences of the Pilgrimage of Grace (1536-37), including Robert Aske's leadership, the scale of the rising, and Henry VIII's response.

## Syllabus items taught here
- 31.DEPTH.PILGRIMAGE - In-depth study: the Pilgrimage of Grace, 1536-37

## How to teach this
Ask: with c.30,000-40,000 rebels in the field and only a small royal army available, why did the Pilgrimage of Grace still fail? Teach each key topic as: what happened (facts, names, dates, precise detail examiners reward), then why it matters (analysis: cause, consequence, change, significance), then how historians read it (interpretations, where relevant). Push the learner to write timed, planned answers regularly, not just to recall facts: Edexcel's essays are marked by levels that reward explained, weighed argument reaching a supported judgement, not narrative or a list of points. Historical facts here (names, dates, figures) do not change with time in the way current-affairs content would, but historians' interpretations do develop; treat the interpretations content as a live debate, not a settled answer.

#### 31.DEPTH.PILGRIMAGE In-depth study: the Pilgrimage of Grace, 1536-37
**The Pilgrimage of Grace, 1536-37 (in-depth study).** The largest and most dangerous Tudor rebellion, triggered most immediately by the dissolution of the smaller monasteries (from 1536) under Thomas Cromwell, which threatened a valued local institution providing charity, education, employment and communal identity, especially in the conservative North. Underlying causes were broader: resentment at rapid religious change since 1533 (the break with Rome, the royal supremacy, fears of further attacks on traditional practice); economic grievances (new taxation, enclosure and rising rents in some areas, and resentment of new financial exactions); and political/regional grievance (Northern elites felt marginalised by a government dominated by 'new men' like Cromwell, and resented central interference in regional affairs). The rising began in Lincolnshire (October 1536) and spread rapidly and much more seriously into Yorkshire and the North, where it took the name 'Pilgrimage of Grace' under the leadership of Robert Aske, a lawyer who gave the movement disciplined, articulate leadership and framed it explicitly as a loyal petition to the King against 'evil counsellors' (Cromwell and reforming bishops), not a rebellion against Henry himself. At its peak the Pilgrimage mobilised perhaps 30,000-40,000 men, including significant gentry and noble participation, vastly outnumbering the royal forces (under the Duke of Norfolk) sent against it. Consequences: lacking artillery, facing a swollen river preventing immediate battle, and trusting Norfolk's negotiated promises (a general pardon and a future Parliament at York to hear grievances), the Pilgrims dispersed peacefully in December 1536 - a fatal misjudgement, since Henry never intended to honour these concessions. A further, smaller, unauthorised rising in early 1537 (Bigod's Rebellion) gave Henry the pretext to abandon the pardon entirely: Aske and other leaders were arrested and executed (Aske hanged in chains at York, 1537) and the dissolution of the monasteries proceeded to completion (by 1540) regardless. The episode showed both the real scale of danger a religiously and regionally motivated rising could pose, and the Crown's willingness to negotiate in bad faith once the immediate military threat had dispersed.
