# S11_Simnel_and_Warbeck - Lesson: The Simnel and Warbeck pretender conspiracies, 1486-99

## Goal
The learner explains the causes, course and consequences of the Lambert Simnel conspiracy (1486-87) and the Perkin Warbeck conspiracy (1491-99), and why dynastic insecurity made Henry VII's early reign especially vulnerable to pretender risings.

## Syllabus items taught here
- 31.CASE.SIMNEL - Case study: the Lambert Simnel conspiracy, 1486-87
- 31.CASE.WARBECK - Case study: the Perkin Warbeck conspiracy, 1491-99

## How to teach this
Ask: why would foreign rulers bother sponsoring an English boy claiming to be a dead prince? Teach each key topic as: what happened (facts, names, dates, precise detail examiners reward), then why it matters (analysis: cause, consequence, change, significance), then how historians read it (interpretations, where relevant). Push the learner to write timed, planned answers regularly, not just to recall facts: Edexcel's essays are marked by levels that reward explained, weighed argument reaching a supported judgement, not narrative or a list of points. Historical facts here (names, dates, figures) do not change with time in the way current-affairs content would, but historians' interpretations do develop; treat the interpretations content as a live debate, not a settled answer.

#### 31.CASE.SIMNEL Case study: the Lambert Simnel conspiracy, 1486-87
**Lambert Simnel, 1486-87.** Henry VII's victory at Bosworth (1485) left the new Tudor dynasty with a weak hereditary claim and many surviving Yorkist claimants and sympathisers, creating persistent dynastic insecurity in his early reign. Lambert Simnel, a boy of humble origins, was coached by disaffected Yorkists (notably the priest Richard Symonds) to impersonate first Richard, Duke of York, then the Earl of Warwick (a genuine Yorkist claimant Henry actually held prisoner in the Tower - a claim Henry could disprove by publicly displaying the real Warwick, which he did, with limited effect). Simnel gained real backing: crowned 'Edward VI' in Dublin (1487) with support from the Yorkist Earl of Kildare and the Irish establishment, and reinforced with German/Swiss mercenaries funded by Margaret of Burgundy (Edward IV's sister, an implacable Yorkist exile committed to unseating Henry). The invasion force landed in Lancashire and was decisively defeated at the Battle of Stoke Field (June 1487) - arguably the last real battle of the Wars of the Roses. Consequences: Henry treated Simnel himself with notable leniency (pardoned and given a menial royal kitchen job, later a falconer), a pragmatic move to defuse the pretender as a martyr figure, while genuine conspirators faced harsher punishment; the episode exposed the continuing danger from Yorkist Ireland and Burgundy, both of which would resurface in the Warbeck affair.

#### 31.CASE.WARBECK Case study: the Perkin Warbeck conspiracy, 1491-99
**Perkin Warbeck, 1491-99.** A far more sustained and dangerous threat: Warbeck, a Flemish-born young man, was groomed to impersonate Richard, Duke of York (the younger of the 'Princes in the Tower', whose fate remained genuinely uncertain, making this a harder claim to simply disprove than Simnel's). Warbeck secured an unusually broad and sustained coalition of foreign backing across eight years, reflecting how useful a Yorkist pretender was to rulers wanting leverage over Henry VII: Margaret of Burgundy (again), Emperor Maximilian I, King Charles VIII of France (briefly, for diplomatic leverage before the Treaty of Étaples), and, most seriously, James IV of Scotland, who received Warbeck at his court, married him to a Scottish noblewoman, and launched a cross-border invasion of England on his behalf (1496) - a level of state sponsorship Simnel never achieved. Warbeck also landed personally in Cornwall (1497), exploiting local anger after the Cornish tax rebellion earlier that year, but found little real support and was captured. Consequences: initially treated with relative leniency (held at court), Warbeck was executed in 1499 alongside the imprisoned Earl of Warwick, after an alleged escape plot - a harsher final resolution than Simnel's, reflecting the cumulative danger Warbeck had posed and Henry's wish to remove Yorkist claimants entirely before arranging his son Arthur's marriage to Catherine of Aragon (for which a securely stable succession was diplomatically essential).
