# S07_Luthers_Early_Challenge - Lesson: The Ninety-Five Theses; the shaping of Luther's beliefs; excommunication

## Goal
The learner explains Luther's rejection of indulgences and the publication of the Ninety-Five Theses (1517), the role of the printing press, the shaping of Luther's beliefs through his protest to Albrecht of Mainz and the Cajetan (1518) and Eck (1519) debates including his acceptance of justification by faith alone, and the process of his excommunication (1519-21).

## Syllabus items taught here
- 2B1.KT2.1 - The Ninety-Five Theses, 1517, and the role of the printing press
- 2B1.KT2.2 - Shaping of Luther's beliefs, 1518-19: Albrecht, Cajetan, Eck, justification by faith
- 2B1.KT2.3 - Luther's excommunication, 1519-21: the 1520 pamphlets and the papal bulls

## How to teach this
Ask: Luther said he only wanted an academic debate in 1517. What turned a local dispute among theologians into a European crisis within three years? Teach each key topic as: what happened (facts, names, dates, precise detail examiners reward), then why it matters (analysis: cause, consequence, change, significance), then how historians read it (interpretations, where relevant). Push the learner to write timed, planned answers regularly, not just to recall facts: Edexcel's essays are marked by levels that reward explained, weighed argument reaching a supported judgement, not narrative or a list of points. Historical facts here (names, dates, figures) do not change with time in the way current-affairs content would, but historians' interpretations do develop; treat the interpretations content as a live debate, not a settled answer.

#### 2B1.KT2.1 The Ninety-Five Theses, 1517, and the role of the printing press
**The Ninety-Five Theses, 1517.** Luther, an Augustinian friar and professor at Wittenberg, drew up 95 theses (propositions for academic debate) attacking the theology and practice of indulgences (payments believed to reduce time in Purgatory for oneself or the dead), provoked directly by Tetzel's nearby indulgence campaign. He sent them, in the conventional academic manner, to the relevant church authority (Archbishop Albrecht of Mainz, who was himself financially implicated in the indulgence sale) and, by tradition, posted them on the Wittenberg castle church door on 31 October 1517. Luther's stated aim was academic debate, not schism. The theses were quickly translated from Latin and printed without Luther's initial control; the printing press turned what he intended as a local scholarly challenge into a Germany-wide, then European, sensation within weeks - an entirely new speed and scale of dissemination that the Church had no established way of containing.

#### 2B1.KT2.2 Shaping of Luther's beliefs, 1518-19: Albrecht, Cajetan, Eck, justification by faith
**Shaping of Luther's beliefs, 1518-19.** Luther's protest to Albrecht of Mainz (1517) went unanswered constructively, escalating the dispute. Pope Leo X initially treated the matter as a squabble among friars rather than a serious threat, delaying an effective response. At the Diet of Augsburg (1518), Cardinal Cajetan, acting for the Pope, demanded Luther recant; Luther refused, instead appealing to a general council of the Church over the Pope's head - a significant escalation, since it directly challenged papal authority rather than just indulgence practice. In the Leipzig Debate (1519) with the theologian Johann Eck, Luther was pushed to defend increasingly radical positions and was manoeuvred into admitting sympathy with some views of Jan Hus (condemned as a heretic a century earlier), making Luther's own heresy hard to deny. Through this period Luther's core theology crystallised around justification by faith alone (sola fide) - the belief that salvation comes through faith in God's grace, not through good works, penance or purchased indulgences - which undercut the entire theological basis of the indulgence trade and much of the sacramental authority of the Church.

#### 2B1.KT2.3 Luther's excommunication, 1519-21: the 1520 pamphlets and the papal bulls
**Luther's excommunication, 1520-21.** The imperial election of 1519 (which brought the young, staunchly Catholic Charles V to the imperial throne, defeating other candidates including, briefly considered, Frederick the Wise of Saxony - Luther's own protector) meant Luther would face a hostile Emperor, though Frederick's continuing protection remained crucial. In 1520 Luther published three major pamphlets addressed to the clergy, the German nobility and the wider German people, setting out his most developed and radical arguments (rejecting most sacraments except baptism and the Eucharist, attacking the papacy directly, and appealing to German national and anti-Roman sentiment as well as theology) - these reached a wide audience through print and marked the point of no return. Pope Leo X responded with the bull *Exsurge Domine* (1520), threatening excommunication unless Luther recanted within 60 days; Luther publicly burned the bull, and the Pope issued the final excommunication bull *Decet Romanum Pontificem* in January 1521, formally expelling him from the Church.
