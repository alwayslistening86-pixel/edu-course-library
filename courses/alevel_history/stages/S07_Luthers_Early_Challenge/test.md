## Test items
1. Explain why Luther's dispute with the Church escalated so rapidly between 1517 and 1520. [6 marks]
2. 'Luther's own theological conviction, rather than the failures of the Catholic Church's response, best explains his excommunication by 1521.' Assess the validity of this view. [20 marks]

## Answer key (for the tutor only)
1. [6] B1 rapid, uncontrolled printing/translation of the Theses spread the dispute far beyond its intended academic audience; B1 Leo X's slow, dismissive early response ('a squabble among friars') allowed the issue to grow; B1 Cajetan's demand for recantation and Luther's refusal/appeal to a general council directly challenged papal authority; B1 the Leipzig Debate with Eck pushed Luther into Hussite-adjacent, clearly heretical positions; B1 Luther's 1520 pamphlets appealed to German national and anti-Roman feeling as well as theology, widening his audience beyond theologians; B1 the crystallising of justification by faith alone as core doctrine made compromise on indulgences (and much else) theologically impossible.
2. [20] Mark out of 20 using Edexcel's generic levels for extended writing: Level 1 (1-4) generalised/descriptive, little or no analysis; Level 2 (5-8) limited explanation/analysis or a mostly narrative answer with some analytical comment; Level 3 (9-12) explains relevant factors with some support but uneven coverage, reaching a partially supported judgement; Level 4 (13-16) a well-organised answer explaining a range of factors with good evidence and a supported judgement, though balance may be slightly uneven; Level 5 (17-20) fully focused, explains and weighs a range of factors with precise, wide-ranging evidence, sustained analysis and a clearly substantiated judgement. Credit: Luther's own doctrinal development (sola fide, the challenge to papal authority via appeal to a council, the 1520 pamphlets) as a self-driven radicalisation; against this, Leo X's slow and dismissive initial response, Cajetan's confrontational approach that pushed Luther towards defiance rather than compromise, and Eck's Leipzig tactics that forced Luther into openly Hussite ground, as Church failures that accelerated the break; a strong answer weighs how far each side's choices interacted rather than treating either as sole cause.
