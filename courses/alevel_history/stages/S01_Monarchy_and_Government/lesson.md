# S01_Monarchy_and_Government - Lesson: Tudor monarchs, succession and gender; Parliament; the Crown's principal servants

## Goal
The learner explains the personal and political qualities of the five Tudor monarchs (1509-88), the disputed successions and the significance of gender for Mary and Elizabeth, the changing role and growing confidence of Parliament, and the influence of the principal servants of the Crown (Wolsey, Cromwell, Burghley).

## Syllabus items taught here
- 1B.1.1 - The Tudor monarchs, succession and gender, 1509-88
- 1B.1.2 - The changing role of Parliament, 1509-88
- 1B.1.3 - The principal servants of the Crown: Wolsey, Cromwell, Burghley

## How to teach this
Ask: how could a teenage king (Edward VI) and two queens rule a kingdom that had never had an unmarried female monarch? Teach each key topic as: what happened (facts, names, dates, precise detail examiners reward), then why it matters (analysis: cause, consequence, change, significance), then how historians read it (interpretations, where relevant). Push the learner to write timed, planned answers regularly, not just to recall facts: Edexcel's essays are marked by levels that reward explained, weighed argument reaching a supported judgement, not narrative or a list of points. Historical facts here (names, dates, figures) do not change with time in the way current-affairs content would, but historians' interpretations do develop; treat the interpretations content as a live debate, not a settled answer.

#### 1B.1.1 The Tudor monarchs, succession and gender, 1509-88
**The Tudor monarchs and succession, 1509-88.** Henry VIII (r.1509-47): charismatic, athletic, vain, obsessed with a male heir and with his 'Great Matter' (the annulment of his marriage to Catherine of Aragon); increasingly autocratic and suspicious in his last decade. Edward VI (r.1547-53): boy-king, aged nine at accession; government run by a Lord Protector (Somerset, then Northumberland); genuinely committed Protestant by his teens; his death at 15 triggered the succession crisis of 1553. Mary I (r.1553-58): first crowned queen regnant of England; devout Catholic, determined to reverse the Reformation; married Philip II of Spain, which was deeply unpopular and provoked Wyatt's Rebellion (1554); died childless. Elizabeth I (r.1558-1603): cautious, politically skilful, deliberately unmarried ('married to England'); cultivated the 'Gloriana' image to compensate for the novelty and perceived weakness of female rule. Significance of gender: contemporaries doubted a woman could rule alone or lead an army; both queens faced pressure to marry to secure succession and provide 'male' authority through a husband, yet both (in different ways) used unmarried or widowed status to retain personal power. Popular attitudes: generally deferential to the dynasty (which had ended the Wars of the Roses), but obedience was not unconditional, as risings under all five monarchs show.

#### 1B.1.2 The changing role of Parliament, 1509-88
**The changing role of Parliament, 1509-88.** Before 1529, Henry VIII's Parliaments met rarely and mainly to vote taxation ('King and Parliament', with the Crown clearly dominant). The Reformation Parliament (1529-36) transformed this: statutes (Act in Restraint of Appeals 1533, Act of Supremacy 1534) were needed to legalise the break with Rome, giving Parliament an unprecedented role in defining religious authority - the shift from 'King and Parliament' to 'King-in-Parliament', where statute law required royal assent plus the consent of Lords and Commons, became the accepted basis of major change. Under Edward and Mary, Parliament continued to legislate on religion (reversing and re-reversing the Reformation). Under Elizabeth, the Commons grew more confident: MPs like Peter Wentworth pushed for freedom of speech on succession and religion, and puritan MPs raised further reform of the Church, causing friction, though Elizabeth retained firm control through her Privy Council managers and by vetoing or blocking unwelcome bills.

#### 1B.1.3 The principal servants of the Crown: Wolsey, Cromwell, Burghley
**The principal servants of the Crown.** Thomas Wolsey (Lord Chancellor to 1529): cardinal and papal legate, dominated domestic and foreign policy under Henry VIII, expanded conciliar courts (Star Chamber), fell after failing to secure the annulment. Thomas Cromwell (chief minister c.1532-40): engineered the break with Rome through statute, is credited by some historians (G.R. Elton's 'Tudor revolution in government' thesis) with modernising administration (Court of Augmentations to handle monastery lands, a more bureaucratic Privy Council), fell and was executed in 1540 after the failed Cleves marriage and factional attack. William Cecil, Lord Burghley (Elizabeth's chief minister for most of her reign): cautious, hard-working, dominated the Privy Council and built a durable, professional conciliar government; his long tenure gave Elizabethan government unusual continuity and stability. Historians debate how far these ministers changed the *structure* of government versus how far government remained personal, dependent on the monarch's favour and liable to sudden change (as Wolsey's and Cromwell's falls show).
