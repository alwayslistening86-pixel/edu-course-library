# S08_Development_and_Leadership - Lesson: The Diet of Worms; radicalism and the Peasants' War; Lutheran beliefs and practice; Melanchthon

## Goal
The learner explains the Diet of Worms (1521) and Luther's protection at the Wartburg, his attitude to religious and political radicalism (Carlstadt, the Zwickau Prophets, the Imperial Knights' revolt, the Peasants' War of 1525), the development of Lutheran beliefs and practice (justification by faith, the German Mass, the Real Presence, Bible translation, the Catechisms), and Melanchthon's influence including the Augsburg Confession (1530).

## How to teach this
Ask: why might a reformer who challenged the Pope also condemn a peasant revolt that used some of his own religious language? Teach each key topic as: what happened (facts, names, dates, precise detail examiners reward), then why it matters (analysis: cause, consequence, change, significance), then how historians read it (interpretations, where relevant). Push the learner to write timed, planned answers regularly, not just to recall facts: Edexcel's essays are marked by levels that reward explained, weighed argument reaching a supported judgement, not narrative or a list of points. Historical facts here (names, dates, figures) do not change with time in the way current-affairs content would, but historians' interpretations do develop; treat the interpretations content as a live debate, not a settled answer.

#### 2B1.KT3.1 The Diet of Worms, 1521, and Luther's protection at the Wartburg
**The Diet of Worms and its aftermath, 1521.** Summoned before the Imperial Diet at Worms (1521), Luther refused to recant, reportedly declaring his conscience was captive to the Word of God (traditionally rendered 'Here I stand'); Charles V, a devout Catholic determined to preserve religious unity in the Empire, condemned him as a heretic and outlaw in the Edict of Worms, making it technically legal for anyone to kill him without penalty. Frederick the Wise, Elector of Saxony, arranged Luther's protective 'kidnapping' and concealment at Wartburg Castle (1521-22), where Luther, in hiding, began his German translation of the New Testament - both protecting Luther personally and giving Frederick's territory a central, ongoing role in sheltering the Reformation regardless of imperial hostility.

#### 2B1.KT3.2 Luther's attitude to religious and political radicalism, including the Peasants' War, 1525
**Luther and radicalism.** In Luther's absence at Wartburg, Andreas Carlstadt and the 'Zwickau Prophets' pushed reform in Wittenberg further and faster than Luther intended (attacking images, altering the mass more radically, claiming direct spiritual revelation) - Luther returned in 1522 specifically to check this, distinguishing his own reform (rooted in scripture, gradual, orderly) from radical enthusiasm. Luther gave no support to the Imperial Knights' revolt (1522-23, led by Franz von Sickingen), a failed attempt by lesser nobility to use reforming rhetoric for their own political/economic ends against princes and bishops. Most significantly, Luther denounced the Peasants' War (1525) - a massive uprising across southern and central Germany combining economic grievances (over dues, enclosure of common land, feudal obligations) with religious language partly drawn from his own writings (the Twelve Articles cited scripture and 'the Gospel' to justify peasant demands). Luther's pamphlet 'Against the Murderous, Thieving Hordes of Peasants' (1525) urged princes to crush the revolt without mercy, fearing that association with social revolution would destroy his movement's respectability and its essential political protection; the brutal suppression that followed (perhaps 100,000 peasants killed) alienated many ordinary supporters but reassured princes that Lutheranism was not a threat to social order - a political conservatism that shaped how far German princes were willing to adopt Lutheranism afterwards.

#### 2B1.KT3.3 The development of Lutheran belief and practice: the German Mass, the Real Presence, the Bible, the Catechisms
**Lutheran beliefs and their influence.** Justification by faith alone remained the theological core. Luther replaced the Latin mass with a German-language liturgy (the 'German Mass', 1526) so ordinary worshippers could understand it, though he kept more traditional ceremonial elements than more radical reformers (e.g. Zwingli) wanted. On the Eucharist, Luther held to a doctrine of the Real Presence (Christ genuinely present 'in, with and under' the bread and wine - consubstantiation), a middle position between Catholic transubstantiation and Zwinglian symbolic memorialism, which later split the wider Protestant movement (the Marburg Colloquy, 1529, failed to reconcile Luther and Zwingli on this issue). Luther's German Bible translation (New Testament 1522, full Bible 1534) standardised and shaped the German language itself and put scripture directly into lay hands. His Large and Small Catechisms (1529) provided accessible teaching tools for clergy and households, spreading basic Lutheran doctrine at parish level and contributing significantly to the movement's growing popularity and embeddedness in everyday religious life.

#### 2B1.KT3.4 Melanchthon's influence and the Augsburg Confession, 1530
**Melanchthon's influence.** Philip Melanchthon, Luther's close colleague at Wittenberg, gave Lutheranism its first systematic theological exposition in *Loci Communes* (1521), making Luther's ideas more accessible and organised for a wider academic and clerical audience. Melanchthon drafted the Augsburg Confession (1530), presented to Charles V at the Diet of Augsburg as the definitive statement of Lutheran belief, deliberately framed in a moderate, conciliatory tone to minimise the apparent gap with Catholic doctrine and maximise the chance of toleration - it became the foundational doctrinal document of Lutheranism. Melanchthon was also central to the awkward 'Philip of Hesse affair' (1540), when he (with Luther) controversially and secretly sanctioned Philip of Hesse's bigamous second marriage on scriptural-pragmatic grounds, a decision that badly damaged Lutheran leaders' moral authority and unity when it became public.
