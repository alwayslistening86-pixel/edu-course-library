# S03_Catholicism_Elizabethan_Settlement - Lesson: The Elizabethan religious settlement; the survival of Catholicism

## Goal
The learner explains the Elizabethan religious settlement of 1558-63, popular attitudes to Catholicism and its survival in the regions including recusancy and Jesuit missions, and the role of the Catholic nobility.

## Syllabus items taught here
- 1B.2.4 - The Elizabethan religious compromise, 1558-63
- 1B.2.5 - The survival of Catholicism: popular attitudes, recusancy, Jesuit missions, the Catholic nobility

## How to teach this
Ask: if you were Elizabeth in 1559, how would you design a church that most Catholics and most Protestants could just about live with? Teach each key topic as: what happened (facts, names, dates, precise detail examiners reward), then why it matters (analysis: cause, consequence, change, significance), then how historians read it (interpretations, where relevant). Push the learner to write timed, planned answers regularly, not just to recall facts: Edexcel's essays are marked by levels that reward explained, weighed argument reaching a supported judgement, not narrative or a list of points. Historical facts here (names, dates, figures) do not change with time in the way current-affairs content would, but historians' interpretations do develop; treat the interpretations content as a live debate, not a settled answer.

#### 1B.2.4 The Elizabethan religious compromise, 1558-63
**The Elizabethan religious compromise, 1558-63.** Elizabeth I sought a settlement broad enough to avoid the instability of the previous three reigns. The Act of Supremacy (1559) made her 'Supreme Governor' (not 'Head', a concession to those uneasy with a woman heading the Church) of the Church of England; the Act of Uniformity (1559) imposed a Book of Common Prayer that was doctrinally ambiguous, notably over the meaning of Communion, allowing conservative and more Protestant clergy to use words they could each interpret; the Thirty-Nine Articles (1563, finalised 1571) set out doctrine in a Protestant but not fully Calvinist form. The settlement was a political compromise rather than a theological statement designed to satisfy purists on either side, and it succeeded in providing decades of comparative religious stability, though it left dissatisfied minorities on both wings (recusant Catholics and puritans).

#### 1B.2.5 The survival of Catholicism: popular attitudes, recusancy, Jesuit missions, the Catholic nobility
**Catholicism and its survival.** Many ordinary people initially had conservative, Catholic sympathies (a slow, uneven 'long Reformation' rather than instant national conversion), and popular attachment to traditional practice persisted longest in remoter regions (the North and West). Recusancy (refusing to attend the required Anglican services) became a criminal offence with fines under Elizabeth, rising sharply after the 1570s. Jesuit and seminary priest missions from the 1570s-80s (trained abroad, e.g. at Douai; figures like Edmund Campion, executed 1581) tried to sustain English Catholicism and were treated as a security threat, especially after the papal bull *Regnans in Excelsis* (1570) excommunicated Elizabeth and released subjects from allegiance, and after Spain became an open enemy. The Catholic nobility (e.g. some Northern earls) provided protection and continuity for Catholic worship on their estates, though after the failed Northern Earls' Revolt (1569-70) many aristocratic Catholics chose quiet conformity or exile over open resistance, and government pressure and fines gradually eroded gentry Catholicism outside a hard core of recusant families.
