# S07_Research_Design_and_Sources_of_Data - Lesson: Quantitative/qualitative methods, research design and sampling; questionnaires and interviews

## Goal
The learner explains quantitative and qualitative research, the stages of research design, and the main sources of data (questionnaires and interviews) sociologists use.

## Syllabus items taught here
- TM.1 - Quantitative and qualitative methods; research design, operationalisation and sampling
- TM.2 - Sources of data: questionnaires and interviews (structured, unstructured, semi-structured)

## How to teach this
Ask: if you wanted to know how many hours a week teenagers spend on social media, versus how it actually feels to be a teenager online, would you use the same method for both questions? Every named theorist and study in this course (e.g. Durkheim, Parsons, Bowles and Gintis, Althusser, Bourdieu, Bernstein, Rosenthal and Jacobson, Ball, Willis, Mac an Ghaill, Gillborn, Merton, Cohen, Cloward and Ohlin, Becker, Lemert, Chambliss, Taylor Walton and Young, Lea and Young, Wilson and Kelling, Murray, Heidensohn, Pollak, Messerschmidt, Situ and Emmons, Green and Ward, Foucault, Miers, Murdock, Engels, Zaretsky, Delphy and Leonard, Young and Willmott, Oakley, Duncombe and Marsden, Bott, Pahl, Aries, Postman, Palmer, Smart, Giddens, Stacey, Weber, Troeltsch, Niebuhr, Wallis, Heelas, Davie, Bruce, Wilson, Berger, Stark and Bainbridge, Comte, Popper, Kuhn, Mead, Blumer, Lyotard, Baudrillard, Beck, Gouldner) is a genuine, correctly attributed piece of sociology, checked against real published sociological knowledge and AQA's own specification content when this course was built -- present it as real scholarship, never as illustrative fiction, and correct the learner explicitly if they misattribute a theory or misstate a finding. Theory and Methods (3.1.3/3.3.2) is genuinely common content examined on both Paper 1 (10 marks) and Paper 3 (30 marks) -- teach it once (S07-S11) and remind the learner it will reappear, at a different tariff, on both papers. AQA's extended-writing items (4/6-mark 'Outline...' items are point-based, scored per developed reason; 10/20/30-mark items are marked by best-fit levels of response, not summed points) -- teach the learner to write in continuous, structured prose for 10-mark-and-above items, always explicitly using the given Item's own detail where a question asks the learner to apply material from it, and to build toward a substantiated evaluative conclusion on 20/30-mark essays rather than a simple list of points. Some content in this course (e.g. current UK divorce-rate or migration figures) is genuinely time-bound; where flagged, check the latest ONS release rather than relying on a fixed number, since the sociological explanation/theory matters far more for the exam than any single current statistic.

#### TM.1 Quantitative and qualitative methods; research design, operationalisation and sampling
**Quantitative and qualitative methods; research design.** Quantitative research produces numerical data (e.g. statistics, survey results) and is associated with positivist approaches (S13) that favour reliability, representativeness and the ability to establish correlations/patterns across large samples; qualitative research produces descriptive, non-numerical data (e.g. words, observed behaviour, meanings) and is associated with interpretivist approaches (S13) that favour validity -- a rich, authentic understanding of how people themselves see and experience their social world. Research design: sociologists begin with an aim or hypothesis (a testable statement, common in more positivist/quantitative research) or a more open research question (common in interpretivist/qualitative research); concepts must be operationalised -- turned into something measurable (e.g. 'social class' operationalised via occupation, income or postcode) -- a process interpretivists criticise as potentially imposing the researcher's own assumptions rather than respondents' meanings. Sampling: since studying an entire target population (sampling frame) is rarely possible, researchers select a sample -- random sampling (every member of the sampling frame has an equal chance of selection, e.g. via a random number generator), systematic sampling (every nth person on a list), stratified sampling (the population is divided into relevant strata, e.g. by age or class, and sampled proportionately within each, improving representativeness), and non-random methods including opportunity sampling (whoever is available) and snowball sampling (existing contacts recommend further contacts, useful for hard-to-reach groups) -- each involves trade-offs between representativeness/generalisability and practicality/access. A pilot study (a small-scale trial run) helps identify problems with question wording, method or design before the main study is carried out.

#### TM.2 Sources of data: questionnaires and interviews (structured, unstructured, semi-structured)
**Sources of data: questionnaires and interviews.** Questionnaires: a pre-set list of written questions; structured (closed) questionnaires use fixed-choice answers (e.g. tick-box), giving quantifiable, easily comparable data with high reliability (the same questions asked identically each time make replication straightforward) but lower validity (fixed categories may not capture what respondents really mean, and cannot probe or clarify); good for positivist, large-scale research, cheap and quick to administer (e.g. by post or online) to large samples, but often suffer low response rates and cannot verify who actually completed them. Interviews: structured interviews resemble a questionnaire read aloud, with fixed questions in a fixed order, giving similar reliability/comparability advantages and disadvantages to structured questionnaires but a normally higher response rate since a real person is present; unstructured interviews are more like a guided conversation, with the interviewer able to probe, follow up and let the respondent's own priorities shape the discussion, favoured by interpretivists for the rich, valid, in-depth data and rapport ('Verstehen', empathetic understanding) they can produce, but they are time-consuming, hard to replicate/generalise from, and depend heavily on the individual interviewer's skill; semi-structured interviews combine some fixed core questions with room to probe, a common compromise. Interviewer effect/bias -- an interviewer's characteristics (age, class, ethnicity, gender, perceived authority) can influence how a respondent answers, particularly in face-to-face and unstructured formats, a threat to validity that both closed and unstructured formats must consider, though it is generally considered a bigger risk for the more personal, in-depth unstructured interview.
