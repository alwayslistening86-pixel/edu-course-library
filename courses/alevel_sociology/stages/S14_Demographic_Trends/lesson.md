# S14_Demographic_Trends - Lesson: UK demographic trends since 1900 and their effects on family life

## Goal
The learner explains UK demographic trends since 1900 in birth rates, death rates, family size, life expectancy, an ageing population, and migration, and their effects on family life.

## Syllabus items taught here
- FAM.5 - UK demographic trends since 1900: birth/death rates, family size, ageing population and migration

## How to teach this
Ask: why does a falling birth rate combined with people living much longer create a genuinely new kind of social problem that didn't exist a century ago? Every named theorist and study in this course (e.g. Durkheim, Parsons, Bowles and Gintis, Althusser, Bourdieu, Bernstein, Rosenthal and Jacobson, Ball, Willis, Mac an Ghaill, Gillborn, Merton, Cohen, Cloward and Ohlin, Becker, Lemert, Chambliss, Taylor Walton and Young, Lea and Young, Wilson and Kelling, Murray, Heidensohn, Pollak, Messerschmidt, Situ and Emmons, Green and Ward, Foucault, Miers, Murdock, Engels, Zaretsky, Delphy and Leonard, Young and Willmott, Oakley, Duncombe and Marsden, Bott, Pahl, Aries, Postman, Palmer, Smart, Giddens, Stacey, Weber, Troeltsch, Niebuhr, Wallis, Heelas, Davie, Bruce, Wilson, Berger, Stark and Bainbridge, Comte, Popper, Kuhn, Mead, Blumer, Lyotard, Baudrillard, Beck, Gouldner) is a genuine, correctly attributed piece of sociology, checked against real published sociological knowledge and AQA's own specification content when this course was built -- present it as real scholarship, never as illustrative fiction, and correct the learner explicitly if they misattribute a theory or misstate a finding. Theory and Methods (3.1.3/3.3.2) is genuinely common content examined on both Paper 1 (10 marks) and Paper 3 (30 marks) -- teach it once (S07-S11) and remind the learner it will reappear, at a different tariff, on both papers. AQA's extended-writing items (4/6-mark 'Outline...' items are point-based, scored per developed reason; 10/20/30-mark items are marked by best-fit levels of response, not summed points) -- teach the learner to write in continuous, structured prose for 10-mark-and-above items, always explicitly using the given Item's own detail where a question asks the learner to apply material from it, and to build toward a substantiated evaluative conclusion on 20/30-mark essays rather than a simple list of points. Some content in this course (e.g. current UK divorce-rate or migration figures) is genuinely time-bound; where flagged, check the latest ONS release rather than relying on a fixed number, since the sociological explanation/theory matters far more for the exam than any single current statistic.

#### FAM.5 UK demographic trends since 1900: birth/death rates, family size, ageing population and migration
**Demographic trends in the UK since 1900.** Birth rate (births per 1,000 of the population per year) and fertility rate have fallen substantially over the long term since 1900 (with fluctuations, e.g. post-war 'baby booms'), reflecting factors including: greater availability and effectiveness of contraception giving women more reproductive choice; women's rising educational and career opportunities raising the opportunity cost of childbearing and delaying/reducing family size; the changing economic role of children (from Victorian-era economic assets/child labour, to modern children being financially costly for far longer, given extended education); secularisation reducing religious pressure toward large families. Death rate has also fallen substantially over the long term, due to improved medicine and public health (vaccination, antibiotics, sanitation), better nutrition, and safer working conditions, though it has recently ticked up somewhat as the population ages. Together, a falling birth rate and rising life expectancy (people living, on average, far longer than in 1900) have produced an ageing population, with a rising proportion of over-65s and a shrinking working-age population supporting them (raising sociological/policy debate about pensions, health/social care costs, and the 'dependency ratio'). Family size has correspondingly fallen (smaller average household/family size than a century ago), while the rise of lone-parent households, reconstituted families and single-person households (including many elderly people living alone) means the 'average' UK household has diversified well beyond a large nuclear family. Migration: the UK has experienced significant net immigration in various periods since 1900 (including post-war Commonwealth immigration and, more recently, EU/international migration), with migrant families sometimes showing different (often initially higher) fertility patterns than the existing population, contributing to the population's continuing growth and ethnic diversity, and interacting with globalisation more broadly (e.g. transnational families maintaining ties across countries) -- exact current migration figures shift with policy and events and should be checked against the latest ONS release rather than treated as fixed by this course.
