# S12_Family_Perspectives_and_Changing_Patterns - Lesson: Family perspectives, and changing patterns of marriage/cohabitation/divorce/family diversity

## Goal
The learner explains functionalist, Marxist and feminist perspectives on the family's relationship to the wider social structure, and changing patterns of marriage, cohabitation, divorce, childbearing and family diversity.

## Syllabus items taught here
- FAM.1 - The family and social structure/change: functionalist, Marxist and feminist perspectives
- FAM.2 - Changing patterns of marriage, cohabitation, divorce, childbearing and family diversity (the Rapoports, Giddens, Stacey)

## How to teach this
Ask: is the nuclear family (two parents, their children) still 'normal' in the UK today, or has it become just one option among many equally common family types? Every named theorist and study in this course (e.g. Durkheim, Parsons, Bowles and Gintis, Althusser, Bourdieu, Bernstein, Rosenthal and Jacobson, Ball, Willis, Mac an Ghaill, Gillborn, Merton, Cohen, Cloward and Ohlin, Becker, Lemert, Chambliss, Taylor Walton and Young, Lea and Young, Wilson and Kelling, Murray, Heidensohn, Pollak, Messerschmidt, Situ and Emmons, Green and Ward, Foucault, Miers, Murdock, Engels, Zaretsky, Delphy and Leonard, Young and Willmott, Oakley, Duncombe and Marsden, Bott, Pahl, Aries, Postman, Palmer, Smart, Giddens, Stacey, Weber, Troeltsch, Niebuhr, Wallis, Heelas, Davie, Bruce, Wilson, Berger, Stark and Bainbridge, Comte, Popper, Kuhn, Mead, Blumer, Lyotard, Baudrillard, Beck, Gouldner) is a genuine, correctly attributed piece of sociology, checked against real published sociological knowledge and AQA's own specification content when this course was built -- present it as real scholarship, never as illustrative fiction, and correct the learner explicitly if they misattribute a theory or misstate a finding. Theory and Methods (3.1.3/3.3.2) is genuinely common content examined on both Paper 1 (10 marks) and Paper 3 (30 marks) -- teach it once (S07-S11) and remind the learner it will reappear, at a different tariff, on both papers. AQA's extended-writing items (4/6-mark 'Outline...' items are point-based, scored per developed reason; 10/20/30-mark items are marked by best-fit levels of response, not summed points) -- teach the learner to write in continuous, structured prose for 10-mark-and-above items, always explicitly using the given Item's own detail where a question asks the learner to apply material from it, and to build toward a substantiated evaluative conclusion on 20/30-mark essays rather than a simple list of points. Some content in this course (e.g. current UK divorce-rate or migration figures) is genuinely time-bound; where flagged, check the latest ONS release rather than relying on a fixed number, since the sociological explanation/theory matters far more for the exam than any single current statistic.

#### FAM.1 The family and social structure/change: functionalist, Marxist and feminist perspectives
**The family and social structure/change; perspectives.** Functionalism (Murdock, Parsons): Murdock argued the nuclear family performs four essential functions for society and its members found, in some form, in virtually every known society -- sexual, reproductive, economic and educational (primary socialisation); Parsons's 'functional fit' thesis argued the isolated nuclear family (small, geographically mobile, not tied to an extended kin network) specifically fits the needs of modern industrial society, which requires a mobile workforce, and performs two 'irreducible' functions -- primary socialisation of children into society's norms and values, and the stabilisation of adult personalities (the family as a 'warm bath', relieving the stresses of the wider competitive economy). Marxism (Engels, Zaretsky): Engels linked the emergence of monogamous marriage and the patriarchal nuclear family historically to the rise of private property, needed by men to ensure legitimate heirs to inherit their property; Zaretsky argued the modern family serves capitalism by providing a private haven that absorbs the frustrations produced by alienating work (concealing capitalism's harm and reducing pressure for change) and by reproducing and maintaining the workforce (unpaid domestic labour) at no cost to the employer. Feminism: liberal feminists argue gradual legal/cultural reform (equal pay, anti-discrimination law) is reducing family inequality over time; Marxist feminists (Benston, Barrett) argue women's unpaid domestic labour and role reproducing the next generation of workers specifically serves capitalism, so family inequality cannot be solved without also overturning capitalism; radical feminists argue the family is the primary site of patriarchy, where men as a group benefit directly from women's domestic labour and, in some analyses, from the threat or reality of domestic violence, regardless of the economic system; Delphy and Leonard's materialist feminism analysed the family itself as a economic system in which men exploit women's labour, similar to Marx's analysis of class.

#### FAM.2 Changing patterns of marriage, cohabitation, divorce, childbearing and family diversity (the Rapoports, Giddens, Stacey)
**Changing patterns of marriage, cohabitation, divorce, childbearing and family diversity.** Since the mid-20th century the UK has seen: a long-term decline in the marriage rate and a rise in the average age at first marriage; a large rise in cohabitation, now a common precursor to or alternative to marriage; a substantial rise in the divorce rate following the Divorce Reform Act 1969 (which widened legal grounds) and later legislative changes, alongside declining social stigma around divorce, changing women's economic independence enabling exit from unhappy marriages, and secularisation reducing religious objection (S22-S25) -- sociologists caution that exact current divorce-rate figures shift and should be checked against the latest ONS data rather than treated as fixed; a fall in the birth rate and a rise in the average age of first childbirth, with more women remaining childless or delaying children for education/career reasons; a rise in the average age of leaving the parental home reversing, and 'boomerang' adult children returning home. Family diversity: the Rapoports identified five types of increasing family diversity -- organisational (differing internal roles/structures, e.g. dual-earner households), cultural (differing ethnic/religious family traditions), social class, life-stage (e.g. newlywed, retired), and generational (differing values/experience between generations); beyond the traditional nuclear family, recognised family/household forms now include lone-parent families, reconstituted/step-families, same-sex families (given full legal recognition following the Marriage (Same Sex Couples) Act 2013), and single-person households. The sociology of personal life (Smart) and late-modern theories of the pure relationship and confluent love (Giddens) argue relationships today are increasingly chosen and sustained for their own sake, based on ongoing negotiated satisfaction rather than external obligation or tradition, making them more equal but also more fragile/likely to end; postmodernists (Stacey) similarly emphasise this greater choice and diversity ('the negotiated family'), while critics (functionalists and some feminists) argue this celebration of diversity can understate continuing patterned inequality (e.g. by class or gender) within families.
