# S09_Choosing_Methods_Theoretical_Practical_and_Ethical - Lesson: Theoretical, practical and ethical factors in choosing a method

## Goal
The learner explains the theoretical (validity, reliability, representativeness), practical and ethical factors that influence a sociologist's choice of research method.

## Syllabus items taught here
- TM.6 - Theoretical, practical and ethical factors affecting choice of research method

## How to teach this
Ask: could a method be perfectly reliable but almost worthless -- and if so, what would that look like? Every named theorist and study in this course (e.g. Durkheim, Parsons, Bowles and Gintis, Althusser, Bourdieu, Bernstein, Rosenthal and Jacobson, Ball, Willis, Mac an Ghaill, Gillborn, Merton, Cohen, Cloward and Ohlin, Becker, Lemert, Chambliss, Taylor Walton and Young, Lea and Young, Wilson and Kelling, Murray, Heidensohn, Pollak, Messerschmidt, Situ and Emmons, Green and Ward, Foucault, Miers, Murdock, Engels, Zaretsky, Delphy and Leonard, Young and Willmott, Oakley, Duncombe and Marsden, Bott, Pahl, Aries, Postman, Palmer, Smart, Giddens, Stacey, Weber, Troeltsch, Niebuhr, Wallis, Heelas, Davie, Bruce, Wilson, Berger, Stark and Bainbridge, Comte, Popper, Kuhn, Mead, Blumer, Lyotard, Baudrillard, Beck, Gouldner) is a genuine, correctly attributed piece of sociology, checked against real published sociological knowledge and AQA's own specification content when this course was built -- present it as real scholarship, never as illustrative fiction, and correct the learner explicitly if they misattribute a theory or misstate a finding. Theory and Methods (3.1.3/3.3.2) is genuinely common content examined on both Paper 1 (10 marks) and Paper 3 (30 marks) -- teach it once (S07-S11) and remind the learner it will reappear, at a different tariff, on both papers. AQA's extended-writing items (4/6-mark 'Outline...' items are point-based, scored per developed reason; 10/20/30-mark items are marked by best-fit levels of response, not summed points) -- teach the learner to write in continuous, structured prose for 10-mark-and-above items, always explicitly using the given Item's own detail where a question asks the learner to apply material from it, and to build toward a substantiated evaluative conclusion on 20/30-mark essays rather than a simple list of points. Some content in this course (e.g. current UK divorce-rate or migration figures) is genuinely time-bound; where flagged, check the latest ONS release rather than relying on a fixed number, since the sociological explanation/theory matters far more for the exam than any single current statistic.

#### TM.6 Theoretical, practical and ethical factors affecting choice of research method
**Factors affecting choice of method.** Theoretical factors: validity -- whether a method genuinely captures what it claims to measure, the true, authentic picture of social reality (qualitative, in-depth methods like unstructured interviews and participant observation are generally considered higher-validity); reliability -- whether a method, repeated by another researcher under the same conditions, would produce the same results (quantitative, standardised methods like structured questionnaires/interviews are generally considered higher-reliability, since they can be precisely replicated); representativeness -- whether a sample/method's findings can be generalised to the wider population it claims to represent (large-scale, randomly/stratified-sampled quantitative research tends to be more representative than small-scale, in-depth qualitative studies); a sociologist's own theoretical perspective (positivist versus interpretivist, S13) tends to shape which of these they prioritise -- there is a persistent trade-off, since methods that maximise validity (closeness to real, natural behaviour/meaning) often sacrifice reliability and representativeness, and vice versa. Practical factors: time and cost (large-scale surveys and long-term participant observation both have real resource costs but of different kinds); access to the research subject (some groups/settings are easier or harder to reach, e.g. via gatekeepers, S06); the characteristics of the researcher relative to the group being studied (e.g. an outsider researching a closed subculture); the nature of the topic itself (e.g. sensitive topics may suit a more private questionnaire over a face-to-face interview, or vice versa if trust/rapport matters more). Ethical factors: informed consent (subjects understanding and agreeing to take part, with covert research raising particular problems, S08); confidentiality and anonymity; protection from physical or psychological harm; the right to withdraw at any point; special care with vulnerable groups (e.g. children, as in S06); professional bodies like the British Sociological Association publish ethical guidelines sociologists are expected to follow, though covert research is sometimes still defended where it is the only way to study a genuinely important, otherwise inaccessible topic (a value-judgement debated within the discipline).
