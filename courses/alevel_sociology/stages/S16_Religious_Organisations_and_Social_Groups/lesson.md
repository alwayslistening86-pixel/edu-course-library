# S16_Religious_Organisations_and_Social_Groups - Lesson: Religious organisations, and religion by class/gender/ethnicity/age

## Goal
The learner explains the main types of religious organisation (churches, denominations, sects, cults and New Age movements) and patterns of religious participation across class, gender, ethnicity and age.

## Syllabus items taught here
- BEL.3 - Religious organisations: churches, denominations, sects, cults and New Age movements
- BEL.4 - Religion, class, gender, ethnicity and age: patterns of participation

## How to teach this
Ask: what actually distinguishes a 'church' from a 'cult' sociologically -- is it about what members believe, or about how the group relates to wider society? Every named theorist and study in this course (e.g. Durkheim, Parsons, Bowles and Gintis, Althusser, Bourdieu, Bernstein, Rosenthal and Jacobson, Ball, Willis, Mac an Ghaill, Gillborn, Merton, Cohen, Cloward and Ohlin, Becker, Lemert, Chambliss, Taylor Walton and Young, Lea and Young, Wilson and Kelling, Murray, Heidensohn, Pollak, Messerschmidt, Situ and Emmons, Green and Ward, Foucault, Miers, Murdock, Engels, Zaretsky, Delphy and Leonard, Young and Willmott, Oakley, Duncombe and Marsden, Bott, Pahl, Aries, Postman, Palmer, Smart, Giddens, Stacey, Weber, Troeltsch, Niebuhr, Wallis, Heelas, Davie, Bruce, Wilson, Berger, Stark and Bainbridge, Comte, Popper, Kuhn, Mead, Blumer, Lyotard, Baudrillard, Beck, Gouldner) is a genuine, correctly attributed piece of sociology, checked against real published sociological knowledge and AQA's own specification content when this course was built -- present it as real scholarship, never as illustrative fiction, and correct the learner explicitly if they misattribute a theory or misstate a finding. #### BEL.3 Religious organisations: churches, denominations, sects, cults and New Age movements
**Religious organisations.** Troeltsch's influential church-sect typology distinguishes: churches -- large, bureaucratic, formally organised institutions, often with a close, respectable relationship to the state and wider society, open to all members of society (inclusive membership), and typically conservative, legitimating the existing social order; sects -- smaller, exclusive groups demanding genuine, often costly commitment from members, frequently in tension with or rejecting wider society, sometimes led by a charismatic leader, and often appealing to marginalised or deprived groups (Weber linked sect membership to groups experiencing 'theodicy of disprivilege', seeking to make sense of and find meaning in their disadvantage). Niebuhr added denominations (a middle category between church and sect -- less exclusive/demanding than a sect, more than a church, e.g. Methodism, requiring some active commitment but not full-society reach) and observed that sects, if they survive across generations, often gradually 'routinise' into denominations or even churches as the original radical fervour cools. Cults (Wallis's later typology, avoiding the more loaded popular meaning of 'cult') are typically small, loosely organised, individualistic groups with no sharp boundary of membership, often focused on this-worldly personal fulfilment/self-improvement rather than salvation, and tolerant of other belief systems -- Wallis further classified new religious movements by their orientation to the world as world-rejecting (demanding a sect-like break with mainstream society, e.g. some millenarian groups), world-accommodating (allowing followers to maintain an otherwise ordinary life alongside inner spiritual practice), or world-affirming (offering techniques for success/fulfilment within, not against, mainstream society, functioning almost like a service or therapy). New Age movements (Heelas) are a further, distinct, loosely-bounded category of contemporary spirituality -- typically emphasising individual, 'self-spirituality' (finding the sacred within oneself rather than in an external institution or god), eclectic borrowing from multiple traditions, and personal choice over authority or doctrine -- linked by some sociologists to a broader postmodern shift (TM.9) away from single, authoritative 'grand narrative' religious institutions toward individualised, pick-and-mix belief.

#### BEL.4 Religion, class, gender, ethnicity and age: patterns of participation
**Religion, class, gender, ethnicity and age.** Class: sects and world-rejecting new religious movements have historically drawn disproportionately on marginalised, economically disadvantaged groups (Weber's theodicy of disprivilege -- religion offering meaning/compensation for relative deprivation), while world-affirming movements have tended to attract more affluent, often middle-class members seeking personal fulfilment/self-improvement rather than material compensation. Gender: women are consistently found to participate in religion, especially churchgoing and sect membership, at higher rates than men across most studies and traditions (Davie), variously explained by women's traditionally greater involvement in the family/caring roles that religious institutions and teaching often reinforce and address, women's historically more constrained access to other sources of status/power (making religious roles/community relatively more significant), and (per Woodhead) some new religious/spiritual movements offering women specific compensation for or alternatives to patriarchal structures elsewhere in society; feminist sociologists nonetheless highlight most major religious institutions' historically male-dominated leadership as itself a source of gender inequality. Ethnicity: minority ethnic groups in the UK, particularly first-generation migrants, often show higher rates of religious participation than the White British population on average, explained by religion's role providing community, cultural identity and continuity/support in the migration experience (Bruce), and by some minority ethnic religious traditions/communities being demographically younger and more recently established, factors distinct from (though sometimes entangled with) religiosity itself. Age: religious participation, especially traditional churchgoing, is generally higher among older age groups in the UK, with younger generations showing markedly lower involvement in traditional organised religion -- though some New Age/alternative spiritual practices and, internationally, some evangelical/Pentecostal and other growing traditions, attract meaningfully younger followings, complicating any simple 'religion is just for the old' generalisation.
