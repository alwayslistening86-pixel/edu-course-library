## How to teach this
Ask: who is the 'criminal' when a government itself commits or covers up serious harm -- and does the criminal justice system even have the power to hold it accountable? Every named theorist and study in this course (e.g. Durkheim, Parsons, Bowles and Gintis, Althusser, Bourdieu, Bernstein, Rosenthal and Jacobson, Ball, Willis, Mac an Ghaill, Gillborn, Merton, Cohen, Cloward and Ohlin, Becker, Lemert, Chambliss, Taylor Walton and Young, Lea and Young, Wilson and Kelling, Murray, Heidensohn, Pollak, Messerschmidt, Situ and Emmons, Green and Ward, Foucault, Miers, Murdock, Engels, Zaretsky, Delphy and Leonard, Young and Willmott, Oakley, Duncombe and Marsden, Bott, Pahl, Aries, Postman, Palmer, Smart, Giddens, Stacey, Weber, Troeltsch, Niebuhr, Wallis, Heelas, Davie, Bruce, Wilson, Berger, Stark and Bainbridge, Comte, Popper, Kuhn, Mead, Blumer, Lyotard, Baudrillard, Beck, Gouldner) is a genuine, correctly attributed piece of sociology, checked against real published sociological knowledge and AQA's own specification content when this course was built -- present it as real scholarship, never as illustrative fiction, and correct the learner explicitly if they misattribute a theory or misstate a finding. Theory and Methods (3.1.3/3.3.2) is genuinely common content examined on both Paper 1 (10 marks) and Paper 3 (30 marks) -- teach it once (S07-S11) and remind the learner it will reappear, at a different tariff, on both papers. AQA's extended-writing items (4/6-mark 'Outline...' items are point-based, scored per developed reason; 10/20/30-mark items are marked by best-fit levels of response, not summed points) -- teach the learner to write in continuous, structured prose for 10-mark-and-above items, always explicitly using the given Item's own detail where a question asks the learner to apply material from it, and to build toward a substantiated evaluative conclusion on 20/30-mark essays rather than a simple list of points. Some content in this course (e.g. current UK divorce-rate or migration figures) is genuinely time-bound; where flagged, check the latest ONS release rather than relying on a fixed number, since the sociological explanation/theory matters far more for the exam than any single current statistic.

#### CD.7 Globalisation and crime, green crime, and human rights/state crime
**Globalisation, green crime, human rights and state crime.** Globalisation has expanded the scale and reach of crime: increased international movement of people, capital, goods and information has enabled transnational organised crime (e.g. drug and people trafficking, cybercrime) that crosses national jurisdictions and is harder for any single national criminal justice system to control -- Held and McGrew's broader globalisation theory, and Castells's concept of a 'global criminal economy', are applied by sociologists to explain crime's increasingly transnational character. Green crime (Situ and Emmons) extends the definition of crime beyond national legal codes to include environmentally harmful acts, whether or not they are technically illegal in a given jurisdiction -- a distinction sociologists frame as traditional (narrow, legally-defined) criminology versus green (broader, harm-based) criminology; primary green crimes (direct environmental harm, e.g. pollution, deforestation, species extinction) are distinguished from secondary green crimes (crime arising from breaking rules meant to prevent/regulate environmental harm, e.g. illegal dumping to avoid regulated disposal costs); green crime is often committed by powerful state and corporate actors, again raising the same under-policing concerns as white-collar crime (S20). Human rights and state crime: Green and Ward define state crime as illegal or deviant activities perpetrated by, or with the complicity of, state agencies -- including genocide, torture, war crimes, and corruption -- uniquely serious because the state is normally the body expected to define and enforce law, making state crime harder to detect, prosecute or hold accountable through ordinary criminal justice mechanisms (which the state itself typically controls), and often requiring international law/institutions (e.g. the International Criminal Court) or investigative journalism/whistleblowing to expose.

#### CD.8 Crime and the media
**Crime and the media.** The media both reports and represents crime in ways sociologists argue distort public perception relative to official statistics: news media over-represent violent and sexual crime relative to their real statistical frequency (while under-representing far more common but less dramatic offences), often selecting and framing crime stories using news values (e.g. unexpectedness, a clear victim/villain, celebrity/deviance-amplifying appeal) that favour sensational, atypical cases -- creating a media-constructed picture of crime that can differ sharply from official statistics or victim-survey evidence (linking to TM.5/S23's caution about the validity of any single crime measure). This can drive moral panics (Cohen, S19) around particular groups constructed as 'folk devils', and, per cultivation theory, may shape public fear of crime disproportionately to real risk, particularly for groups (e.g. the elderly) statistically less likely to be victims but who report higher fear of crime. The relationship between media and crime causation is itself debated: some (with limited, contested evidence) argue media content (e.g. violent content) can directly influence/desensitise audiences toward copying crime (imitation/effect models), a view most sociologists treat sceptically given weak/inconsistent evidence and audiences' active interpretation of media rather than passive absorption; media can also, however, genuinely facilitate crime more directly, e.g. providing new opportunities/methods for cybercrime, or (through relative-deprivation-style processes) showcasing a consumerist lifestyle that intensifies strain/relative deprivation for those unable to afford it (linking back to S18-S19). Newburn and others also study the media as a form of informal social control in its own right, e.g. through naming-and-shaming coverage, surveillance-style 'true crime' formats, and campaigning journalism that can itself trigger policy change or a moral panic.
