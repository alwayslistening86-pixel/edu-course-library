# S04_Pupil_Subcultures_Hidden_Curriculum_and_Organisation - Lesson: Pupil subcultures, the hidden curriculum, and streaming/setting

## Goal
The learner explains pro-school and anti-school pupil subcultures, the concept of the hidden curriculum, and how streaming/setting organises teaching and learning.

## Syllabus items taught here
- EDU.6 - Pupil identities and subcultures: pro-school and anti-school responses (Lacey, Willis, Mac an Ghaill)
- EDU.7 - The hidden curriculum, and the organisation of teaching and learning (streaming, setting, mixed ability)

## How to teach this
Ask: why might a group of pupils who are told, in effect, 'you will fail', respond by deliberately rejecting school's rules rather than trying harder? Every named theorist and study in this course (e.g. Durkheim, Parsons, Bowles and Gintis, Althusser, Bourdieu, Bernstein, Rosenthal and Jacobson, Ball, Willis, Mac an Ghaill, Gillborn, Merton, Cohen, Cloward and Ohlin, Becker, Lemert, Chambliss, Taylor Walton and Young, Lea and Young, Wilson and Kelling, Murray, Heidensohn, Pollak, Messerschmidt, Situ and Emmons, Green and Ward, Foucault, Miers, Murdock, Engels, Zaretsky, Delphy and Leonard, Young and Willmott, Oakley, Duncombe and Marsden, Bott, Pahl, Aries, Postman, Palmer, Smart, Giddens, Stacey, Weber, Troeltsch, Niebuhr, Wallis, Heelas, Davie, Bruce, Wilson, Berger, Stark and Bainbridge, Comte, Popper, Kuhn, Mead, Blumer, Lyotard, Baudrillard, Beck, Gouldner) is a genuine, correctly attributed piece of sociology, checked against real published sociological knowledge and AQA's own specification content when this course was built -- present it as real scholarship, never as illustrative fiction, and correct the learner explicitly if they misattribute a theory or misstate a finding. #### EDU.6 Pupil identities and subcultures: pro-school and anti-school responses (Lacey, Willis, Mac an Ghaill)
**Pupil identities and subcultures.** Interactionist sociologists argue that being labelled (S03) does not simply happen to passive pupils -- pupils actively respond, and one common response is forming a subculture with others in a similar position. Lacey distinguished pro-school subcultures (pupils, often those placed in higher streams, who accept the school's values and definition of success, gaining status through academic achievement) from anti-school subcultures (pupils, often those placed in lower streams and labelled as failures, who invert the school's values -- gaining status among peers through behaviour the school punishes, such as truanting, messing about or open defiance) -- Lacey called this process of subcultures forming around stream position 'differentiation and polarisation'. Willis's classic ethnographic study Learning to Labour (1977) followed a group of twelve working-class boys ('the lads') who formed a strongly anti-school counter-culture, mocking conformist pupils ('ear'oles'), rejecting the value of qualifications, and valuing the same qualities (toughness, resistance to authority, sexism) that would later suit them for the unskilled manual factory work they expected to enter -- Willis's key insight was that the lads' rebellion was not irrational failure but, ironically, prepared them to slot into working-class manual jobs, partially reproducing class inequality through their own agency rather than simple external imposition. Mac an Ghaill identified multiple, more varied masculine identities among pupils (not just one anti-school type), including 'macho lads' similar to Willis's group but also 'academic achievers' and others, showing subcultural responses are more diverse than a simple pro/anti split. Girls' subcultures have also been studied (e.g. by Mac an Ghaill and others), including ways girls negotiate femininity alongside or against academic identities.

#### EDU.7 The hidden curriculum, and the organisation of teaching and learning (streaming, setting, mixed ability)
**Hidden curriculum; organisation of teaching and learning.** The hidden curriculum refers to the implicit lessons and values school teaches through its structure, routines and relationships, rather than through what is explicitly taught in lessons -- e.g. hierarchy and deference to authority (bells, uniforms, following instructions without question), competitiveness (grades, league tables), punctuality and time discipline, and acceptance of a fixed, externally imposed timetable. Marxists (Bowles and Gintis) argue the hidden curriculum's real function is to prepare pupils, especially working-class pupils, for the discipline and hierarchy of capitalist workplaces -- part of the correspondence principle covered in S01. Feminists have highlighted a gendered hidden curriculum: historically, subject choices, images in textbooks, and teacher expectations reinforced traditional gender roles (though this has been challenged by equal opportunities policies, S03). Organisation of teaching and learning: streaming places pupils in one fixed ability group across all or most subjects, based on a judgement (not always accurate) of general ability; setting places pupils in ability groups separately for each subject, allowing for uneven ability across subjects; both are criticised (Ball's classic study of 'Beachside Comprehensive' found streaming/banding could itself create the very achievement differences it claimed only to reflect, disadvantaging working-class pupils placed in lower bands regardless of true ability) as a mechanism that can produce labelling and self-fulfilling prophecies (S02-S03) rather than simply respond to pre-existing differences; mixed-ability teaching is the main alternative, intended to avoid this, though critics argue it can make differentiated teaching harder and may hold back higher-attaining pupils.
