# S08_Observation_Experiments_and_Documentary_Sources - Lesson: Observation, experiments, documents and official statistics

## Goal
The learner explains participant and non-participant observation, laboratory/field/comparative experiments, and documentary and official-statistics sources of data.

## Syllabus items taught here
- TM.3 - Observation: participant and non-participant, overt and covert
- TM.4 - Experiments: laboratory, field and the comparative method
- TM.5 - Documents and official statistics as sources of data

## How to teach this
Ask: could you honestly study a street gang by handing out a questionnaire -- and if not, what would you have to do instead, and what would it cost you? Every named theorist and study in this course (e.g. Durkheim, Parsons, Bowles and Gintis, Althusser, Bourdieu, Bernstein, Rosenthal and Jacobson, Ball, Willis, Mac an Ghaill, Gillborn, Merton, Cohen, Cloward and Ohlin, Becker, Lemert, Chambliss, Taylor Walton and Young, Lea and Young, Wilson and Kelling, Murray, Heidensohn, Pollak, Messerschmidt, Situ and Emmons, Green and Ward, Foucault, Miers, Murdock, Engels, Zaretsky, Delphy and Leonard, Young and Willmott, Oakley, Duncombe and Marsden, Bott, Pahl, Aries, Postman, Palmer, Smart, Giddens, Stacey, Weber, Troeltsch, Niebuhr, Wallis, Heelas, Davie, Bruce, Wilson, Berger, Stark and Bainbridge, Comte, Popper, Kuhn, Mead, Blumer, Lyotard, Baudrillard, Beck, Gouldner) is a genuine, correctly attributed piece of sociology, checked against real published sociological knowledge and AQA's own specification content when this course was built -- present it as real scholarship, never as illustrative fiction, and correct the learner explicitly if they misattribute a theory or misstate a finding. Theory and Methods (3.1.3/3.3.2) is genuinely common content examined on both Paper 1 (10 marks) and Paper 3 (30 marks) -- teach it once (S07-S11) and remind the learner it will reappear, at a different tariff, on both papers. AQA's extended-writing items (4/6-mark 'Outline...' items are point-based, scored per developed reason; 10/20/30-mark items are marked by best-fit levels of response, not summed points) -- teach the learner to write in continuous, structured prose for 10-mark-and-above items, always explicitly using the given Item's own detail where a question asks the learner to apply material from it, and to build toward a substantiated evaluative conclusion on 20/30-mark essays rather than a simple list of points. Some content in this course (e.g. current UK divorce-rate or migration figures) is genuinely time-bound; where flagged, check the latest ONS release rather than relying on a fixed number, since the sociological explanation/theory matters far more for the exam than any single current statistic.

#### TM.3 Observation: participant and non-participant, overt and covert
**Observation.** Participant observation: the researcher joins the group being studied and takes part in its activities, either overtly (the group knows they are a researcher) or covertly (the group is not told, and the researcher poses as a genuine member); favoured especially by interpretivists for very high validity -- seeing behaviour first-hand, in its natural setting, rather than relying on what people say about their behaviour -- and famous examples (e.g. studies of gangs, cults or subcultures inaccessible by other means) show its value for hard-to-reach or secretive groups. Drawbacks: very time-consuming and hard to replicate (low reliability); covert observation raises serious ethical issues (deception, no informed consent, potential harm if the researcher must participate in illegal/immoral acts to maintain cover); overt observation risks the observer effect (people behaving differently because they know they are watched, the 'Hawthorne effect'); going native (the researcher over-identifies with the group, losing objectivity) is a further risk. Non-participant observation: the researcher observes without joining in (e.g. watching from outside a classroom), reducing the risk of going native and easing note-taking, but sacrificing some of the closeness/validity of full participation, and still facing the observer effect if overt.

#### TM.4 Experiments: laboratory, field and the comparative method
**Experiments.** Laboratory experiments: conducted in a controlled, artificial setting where the researcher manipulates an independent variable and measures effects on a dependent variable while controlling extraneous variables, allowing precise measurement of cause and effect and straightforward replication (favoured by positivists as the most 'scientific' method) -- but rare and ethically fraught in sociology, since manipulating real social behaviour under lab conditions is often impractical or unethical, and the artificial setting risks the Hawthorne effect and very low ecological validity (behaviour in a lab may not reflect real-world behaviour). Field experiments: take place in a real-life setting but the researcher still manipulates a variable, often covertly (e.g. sending matched fictitious job applications differing only by a name signalling ethnicity, to test for discrimination in real hiring practice) -- higher ecological validity than lab experiments since the setting is natural, but harder to control extraneous variables and often raises ethical issues of consent (subjects are usually unaware they are part of an experiment). Comparative method: rather than manipulating variables directly, the researcher compares naturally occurring cases that differ on a relevant variable (Durkheim's classic study of suicide statistics across different social groups/countries is the paradigm example) -- used where direct experimentation on society is impossible, but cause and effect is harder to establish with certainty than in a true experiment, since unobserved confounding differences between the compared cases may explain the pattern instead.

#### TM.5 Documents and official statistics as sources of data
**Documents and official statistics.** Documents: personal documents (diaries, letters, photographs) and public/historical documents (government reports, newspapers, organisational records) provide data the researcher did not generate, useful for studying the past or where direct research access is impossible; qualitative content/thematic analysis can draw out meanings, though authenticity (is it genuine?), credibility (is it accurate/sincere?), representativeness (is it typical?) and meaning (can it be correctly interpreted, especially across historical/cultural distance) must all be assessed (Scott's four criteria) -- a key practical/theoretical strength is that documents are unobtrusive (no risk of the researcher affecting what they study) but a key weakness is the researcher cannot question or clarify with the original author. Official statistics: numerical data collected by the state or other large organisations (e.g. crime statistics, exam results, unemployment figures, the Census); a major practical strength is that they are freely available, cover huge, representative samples (sometimes whole populations) at a fraction of the cost of primary research, and allow tracking trends over time; positivists treat 'hard' statistics (e.g. births, deaths) as generally reliable social facts suitable for establishing correlations, but interpretivists and others (S13-S14, applied especially to crime statistics) argue many official statistics (e.g. recorded crime, unemployment definitions) are themselves social constructions, reflecting the definitions, priorities and practices of the agencies that produce them rather than an objective count -- reducing their validity for some research purposes even where their reliability (consistency of recording method) is high.
