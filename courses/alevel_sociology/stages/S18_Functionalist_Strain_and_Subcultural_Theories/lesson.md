# S18_Functionalist_Strain_and_Subcultural_Theories - Lesson: Functionalist and strain theories, and subcultural theories of crime

## Goal
The learner explains functionalist and strain theories of crime and deviance, and subcultural theories that build on strain theory.

## Syllabus items taught here
- CD.1 - Functionalist theory of crime, and Merton's strain theory
- CD.2 - Subcultural theories of crime and deviance (Cohen; Cloward and Ohlin)

## How to teach this
Ask: could a society with literally zero crime actually be an unhealthy, over-controlled society -- and if so, what does that say about crime's role? Every named theorist and study in this course (e.g. Durkheim, Parsons, Bowles and Gintis, Althusser, Bourdieu, Bernstein, Rosenthal and Jacobson, Ball, Willis, Mac an Ghaill, Gillborn, Merton, Cohen, Cloward and Ohlin, Becker, Lemert, Chambliss, Taylor Walton and Young, Lea and Young, Wilson and Kelling, Murray, Heidensohn, Pollak, Messerschmidt, Situ and Emmons, Green and Ward, Foucault, Miers, Murdock, Engels, Zaretsky, Delphy and Leonard, Young and Willmott, Oakley, Duncombe and Marsden, Bott, Pahl, Aries, Postman, Palmer, Smart, Giddens, Stacey, Weber, Troeltsch, Niebuhr, Wallis, Heelas, Davie, Bruce, Wilson, Berger, Stark and Bainbridge, Comte, Popper, Kuhn, Mead, Blumer, Lyotard, Baudrillard, Beck, Gouldner) is a genuine, correctly attributed piece of sociology, checked against real published sociological knowledge and AQA's own specification content when this course was built -- present it as real scholarship, never as illustrative fiction, and correct the learner explicitly if they misattribute a theory or misstate a finding. Theory and Methods (3.1.3/3.3.2) is genuinely common content examined on both Paper 1 (10 marks) and Paper 3 (30 marks) -- teach it once (S07-S11) and remind the learner it will reappear, at a different tariff, on both papers. AQA's extended-writing items (4/6-mark 'Outline...' items are point-based, scored per developed reason; 10/20/30-mark items are marked by best-fit levels of response, not summed points) -- teach the learner to write in continuous, structured prose for 10-mark-and-above items, always explicitly using the given Item's own detail where a question asks the learner to apply material from it, and to build toward a substantiated evaluative conclusion on 20/30-mark essays rather than a simple list of points. Some content in this course (e.g. current UK divorce-rate or migration figures) is genuinely time-bound; where flagged, check the latest ONS release rather than relying on a fixed number, since the sociological explanation/theory matters far more for the exam than any single current statistic.

#### CD.1 Functionalist theory of crime, and Merton's strain theory
**Functionalist and strain theories.** Durkheim argued a certain amount of crime and deviance is normal and even functional for society (not simply a pathology to be eliminated entirely): it can reinforce social solidarity and reaffirm the boundaries of acceptable behaviour (society reacts collectively against a crime, strengthening shared values, per Durkheim's concept of 'boundary maintenance'), acts as a 'safety valve' releasing built-up social tension, and can be a source of positive social change (today's deviant may become tomorrow's respected reformer, e.g. early suffragette activism was criminalised at the time). Durkheim also introduced anomie -- a state of normlessness where society's shared rules lose their regulatory grip on individuals, e.g. during periods of rapid social change, which he linked to rising deviance (including suicide, in his own statistical study). Merton's strain theory developed anomie into an American-context explanation of crime: society sets culturally approved goals (e.g. the 'American Dream' of material success) but unequally distributes the legitimate, institutionalised means (education, good jobs) to achieve them; the resulting strain between goals and means produces deviant adaptations for those blocked from legitimate success, including innovation (accepting the goal but using illegitimate means, e.g. theft/fraud -- Merton's key explanation of much utilitarian crime), ritualism (abandoning the goal but rigidly following the means), retreatism (rejecting both, e.g. some drug subcultures), and rebellion (rejecting both and substituting new goals/means, e.g. political radicals) -- alongside conformity (accepting both, the non-deviant majority). Strain theory is criticised for focusing mainly on utilitarian, financially-motivated working-class crime, largely ignoring white-collar/corporate crime and non-utilitarian deviance (e.g. vandalism, violence for its own sake), a gap subcultural theories partly address.

#### CD.2 Subcultural theories of crime and deviance (Cohen; Cloward and Ohlin)
**Subcultural theories.** Building on Merton but focused specifically on group/gang deviance rather than individual adaptations, Cohen's status frustration theory argued working-class boys, denied legitimate status in a middle-class-dominated school system (facing 'status frustration'), collectively invert mainstream values to form a delinquent subculture in which non-utilitarian, malicious, negativistic behaviour (vandalism, defiance for its own sake, not for material gain) becomes a new source of status among peers -- directly explaining the non-utilitarian crime Merton's theory struggled with. Cloward and Ohlin extended this by arguing access to illegitimate as well as legitimate opportunity structures varies, producing different subcultural responses depending on what illegitimate means are locally available: criminal subcultures (in areas with an established adult criminal hierarchy providing role models/apprenticeship into utilitarian crime), conflict subcultures (in more disorganised areas lacking stable adult criminal structures, where status is instead won through gang violence/territorial conflict), and retreatist subcultures (for 'double failures' who cannot succeed even in illegitimate structures, turning to drug use). These subcultural theories are criticised (from an interactionist perspective, S19) for accepting official statistics/labels about who is deviant at face value, for over-predicting working-class male delinquency without adequately explaining why most young people in the same disadvantaged circumstances do not offend, and for insufficiently explaining female and middle-class/white-collar deviance -- limitations later theories (feminist and Marxist criminology, S19; interactionism, S19) directly address.
