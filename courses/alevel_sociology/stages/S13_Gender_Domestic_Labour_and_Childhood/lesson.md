# S13_Gender_Domestic_Labour_and_Childhood - Lesson: Gender roles, domestic labour and power; the social construction of childhood

## Goal
The learner explains debates about gender roles, the division of domestic labour and power within relationships, and changing views of the nature and status of childhood.

## Syllabus items taught here
- FAM.3 - Gender roles, domestic labour and power within the family
- FAM.4 - The social construction of childhood, and debates on its changing status

## How to teach this
Ask: if both partners in a household work full-time, is domestic labour actually shared equally today -- and how would a sociologist even measure that fairly? Every named theorist and study in this course (e.g. Durkheim, Parsons, Bowles and Gintis, Althusser, Bourdieu, Bernstein, Rosenthal and Jacobson, Ball, Willis, Mac an Ghaill, Gillborn, Merton, Cohen, Cloward and Ohlin, Becker, Lemert, Chambliss, Taylor Walton and Young, Lea and Young, Wilson and Kelling, Murray, Heidensohn, Pollak, Messerschmidt, Situ and Emmons, Green and Ward, Foucault, Miers, Murdock, Engels, Zaretsky, Delphy and Leonard, Young and Willmott, Oakley, Duncombe and Marsden, Bott, Pahl, Aries, Postman, Palmer, Smart, Giddens, Stacey, Weber, Troeltsch, Niebuhr, Wallis, Heelas, Davie, Bruce, Wilson, Berger, Stark and Bainbridge, Comte, Popper, Kuhn, Mead, Blumer, Lyotard, Baudrillard, Beck, Gouldner) is a genuine, correctly attributed piece of sociology, checked against real published sociological knowledge and AQA's own specification content when this course was built -- present it as real scholarship, never as illustrative fiction, and correct the learner explicitly if they misattribute a theory or misstate a finding. Theory and Methods (3.1.3/3.3.2) is genuinely common content examined on both Paper 1 (10 marks) and Paper 3 (30 marks) -- teach it once (S07-S11) and remind the learner it will reappear, at a different tariff, on both papers. AQA's extended-writing items (4/6-mark 'Outline...' items are point-based, scored per developed reason; 10/20/30-mark items are marked by best-fit levels of response, not summed points) -- teach the learner to write in continuous, structured prose for 10-mark-and-above items, always explicitly using the given Item's own detail where a question asks the learner to apply material from it, and to build toward a substantiated evaluative conclusion on 20/30-mark essays rather than a simple list of points. Some content in this course (e.g. current UK divorce-rate or migration figures) is genuinely time-bound; where flagged, check the latest ONS release rather than relying on a fixed number, since the sociological explanation/theory matters far more for the exam than any single current statistic.

#### FAM.3 Gender roles, domestic labour and power within the family
**Gender roles, domestic labour and power.** Young and Willmott's 'march of progress' view argued family life has become more equal over time, culminating in the 'symmetrical family' -- an increasingly joint conjugal role relationship in which husbands and wives share housework, childcare and decision-making relatively equally, and both may go out to work, a trend they linked to rising affluence, geographic mobility away from extended kin (reducing wider family support/interference) and women's greater workforce participation. Feminist critics strongly dispute this: Oakley's research found housework and childcare remained overwhelmingly women's responsibility even where women also worked outside the home, coining the idea of women's 'dual burden'; the concept of the 'triple shift' (Duncombe and Marsden) extends this further to include emotion work -- the unpaid, often invisible work of managing a family's emotional wellbeing -- alongside paid work and domestic labour. Bott's distinction between segregated conjugal roles (separate, clearly gender-divided leisure and domestic tasks, and separate friendship networks) and joint conjugal roles (shared tasks and leisure, overlapping friendship networks) is used to frame this debate; research remains divided on how far genuinely joint roles have spread, and on measuring 'equality' fairly (e.g. is equal time spent on housework the same as equal responsibility/mental load for organising it?). Decision-making and power: research on who controls household finances and major decisions (e.g. Pahl's distinction between types of money management) similarly suggests continuing gendered patterns of control persist beneath an appearance of equality. Domestic violence: radical feminists argue disproportionately male-perpetrated domestic violence exposes the family as a site of patriarchal power and control rather than the harmonious haven functionalism assumes, and is under-reported/under-recorded by official statistics (linking to the validity issues in TM.5 and CD.11).

#### FAM.4 The social construction of childhood, and debates on its changing status
**Childhood.** Sociologists distinguish the biological fact of a period of physical immaturity from childhood as a social construction -- a set of ideas about what children are like, what they need, and how they should be treated, which varies across time and culture rather than being fixed by biology alone. Aries's historical argument that in medieval Europe children were seen and treated as 'mini adults', without a distinct concept of childhood as we now understand it, is influential (if disputed by some historians on evidence grounds) in showing childhood's modern, distinctively protected, child-centred meaning is historically recent, associated with industrialisation, the rise of compulsory schooling, and child protection/labour laws. The 'march of progress' view (echoing Young and Willmott's family thesis) holds that children's status has steadily improved -- from being economically exploited and having few rights, to today's child-centred society where children are protected, valued and have rights (e.g. the UN Convention on the Rights of the Child) and childhood is prolonged through extended education. Critics (the 'conflict' view) argue this progress narrative is overstated and even harmful: Postman argued mass media (television, then the internet) has eroded the boundary between adult and child knowledge/experience, producing the 'disappearance of childhood' as children are exposed early to adult content; Palmer's concept of 'toxic childhood' argues technological and cultural change (junk food, screen time, competitive testing, declining outdoor play) is damaging children's healthy development; Marxist/conflict sociologists further argue the March of Progress view ignores continuing inequality between children by class and globally (child poverty, and child labour/exploitation still widespread in some parts of the world), and note that within the family children's power remains limited relative to adults (age patriarchy), regardless of the positive rhetoric of child-centredness.
