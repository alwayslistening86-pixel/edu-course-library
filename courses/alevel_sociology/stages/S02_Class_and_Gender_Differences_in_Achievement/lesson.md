# S02_Class_and_Gender_Differences_in_Achievement - Lesson: Class and gender differences in educational achievement

## Goal
The learner explains external (home-based) and internal (in-school) explanations for social-class differences in educational achievement, and the pattern of and explanations for gender differences in achievement.

## Syllabus items taught here
- EDU.2 - Class differences in achievement: external (material/cultural deprivation) and internal (labelling, streaming) factors
- EDU.3 - Gender differences in achievement: external and internal factors, and explanations for boys' relative underachievement

## How to teach this
Ask: if two pupils have identical natural ability, what outside-school and inside-school factors could still make one of them do much better at GCSE than the other? Every named theorist and study in this course (e.g. Durkheim, Parsons, Bowles and Gintis, Althusser, Bourdieu, Bernstein, Rosenthal and Jacobson, Ball, Willis, Mac an Ghaill, Gillborn, Merton, Cohen, Cloward and Ohlin, Becker, Lemert, Chambliss, Taylor Walton and Young, Lea and Young, Wilson and Kelling, Murray, Heidensohn, Pollak, Messerschmidt, Situ and Emmons, Green and Ward, Foucault, Miers, Murdock, Engels, Zaretsky, Delphy and Leonard, Young and Willmott, Oakley, Duncombe and Marsden, Bott, Pahl, Aries, Postman, Palmer, Smart, Giddens, Stacey, Weber, Troeltsch, Niebuhr, Wallis, Heelas, Davie, Bruce, Wilson, Berger, Stark and Bainbridge, Comte, Popper, Kuhn, Mead, Blumer, Lyotard, Baudrillard, Beck, Gouldner) is a genuine, correctly attributed piece of sociology, checked against real published sociological knowledge and AQA's own specification content when this course was built -- present it as real scholarship, never as illustrative fiction, and correct the learner explicitly if they misattribute a theory or misstate a finding. Theory and Methods (3.1.3/3.3.2) is genuinely common content examined on both Paper 1 (10 marks) and Paper 3 (30 marks) -- teach it once (S07-S11) and remind the learner it will reappear, at a different tariff, on both papers. AQA's extended-writing items (4/6-mark 'Outline...' items are point-based, scored per developed reason; 10/20/30-mark items are marked by best-fit levels of response, not summed points) -- teach the learner to write in continuous, structured prose for 10-mark-and-above items, always explicitly using the given Item's own detail where a question asks the learner to apply material from it, and to build toward a substantiated evaluative conclusion on 20/30-mark essays rather than a simple list of points. Some content in this course (e.g. current UK divorce-rate or migration figures) is genuinely time-bound; where flagged, check the latest ONS release rather than relying on a fixed number, since the sociological explanation/theory matters far more for the exam than any single current statistic.

#### EDU.2 Class differences in achievement: external (material/cultural deprivation) and internal (labelling, streaming) factors
**Class differences in achievement.** External (home-based) factors: material deprivation -- poverty affects diet, housing, access to resources (a computer, a quiet study space, educational trips), and can force young people into part-time work or early exit from education (Smith and Noble); cultural deprivation -- Douglas argued working-class parents place a lower value on education and give less encouragement; Bernstein's restricted code (context-bound, informal speech, more common in working-class homes) versus elaborated code (context-free, more formal, matching the code schools and exams use) can disadvantage working-class pupils' ability to access the curriculum; Bourdieu's cultural capital -- middle-class children inherit knowledge, tastes and ways of speaking that schools value and reward, converting this into educational (and then economic) capital, while working-class 'habitus' (dispositions, tastes) is less valued by schools. Internal (in-school) factors: labelling -- teachers may (often unconsciously) label pupils by class-based stereotypes of ability and behaviour, producing a self-fulfilling prophecy (Rosenthal and Jacobson's classic 'Pygmalion' experiment found pupils randomly labelled as 'spurters' showed real IQ gains, suggesting teacher expectation itself affects outcomes); streaming and setting can entrench this, with working-class pupils disproportionately placed in lower streams and receiving a 'watered-down' curriculum (Ball); pupil subcultures can develop in response (anti-school subcultures forming among those labelled/streamed as failures, e.g. Willis's 'lads' -- covered fully in S04); the ethos and organisational habitus of a school can itself embody middle-class norms and values that disadvantage working-class pupils (Reay).

#### EDU.3 Gender differences in achievement: external and internal factors, and explanations for boys' relative underachievement
**Gender differences in achievement.** The pattern: girls have, since the late 1980s, consistently outperformed boys at GCSE and increasingly at A-level and university entry, reversing an earlier gap favouring boys; this shift is a major, well-evidenced trend, though a gap in favour of girls remains smaller at the very top grades in some subjects. External factors: the impact of feminism raising girls' expectations and self-image (McRobbie contrasted girls' magazines from the 1970s, which emphasised marriage and appearance, with more recent ones emphasising careers and independence); changes in the family (more lone-parent, female-headed households where mothers may be positive role models of female independence, and rising divorce rates encouraging girls to see financial independence as important); changes in women's employment (equal pay legislation, the decline of traditional 'male' manual jobs and growth of the service sector, raising girls' career aspirations, per Sharpe's comparison of 1970s and 1990s girls' priorities). Internal factors: equal opportunities policies (GIST and WISE encouraging girls into science/engineering) and a more gender-neutral curriculum since the National Curriculum removed some subject differentiation; teacher attention and the effect of coursework/exam style (some research, though contested, links a shift toward more coursework historically favouring girls' more sustained, organised approach); positive role models of female teachers and head teachers; challenges to gender stereotyping in learning materials since the 1980s. Explanations for boys' relative underachievement: laddish subcultures (Epstein) where academic effort risks being seen as 'feminine' or 'uncool', particularly by working-class boys; a 'crisis of masculinity' (Mac an Ghaill) linked to the decline of traditional manual work that once gave working-class men a clear identity and purpose without needing school success; literacy -- boys' relatively weaker early literacy skills, and less parental encouragement to read.
