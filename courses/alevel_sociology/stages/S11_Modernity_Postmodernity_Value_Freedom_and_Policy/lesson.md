# S11_Modernity_Postmodernity_Value_Freedom_and_Policy - Lesson: Modernity/postmodernity, value freedom, and theory/methods and social policy

## Goal
The learner explains modernist and postmodernist views of society in relation to sociological theory, the debate over value freedom, and the relationship between sociological theory/methods and social policy.

## Syllabus items taught here
- TM.9 - Modernity and postmodernity in relation to sociological theory
- TM.10 - Value freedom, and the relationship between sociological theory/methods and social policy

## How to teach this
Ask: do the old, big theories (Marxism, functionalism) still make sense of a society organised around the internet, globalisation and endless consumer choice -- or has society itself changed too much for them? Every named theorist and study in this course (e.g. Durkheim, Parsons, Bowles and Gintis, Althusser, Bourdieu, Bernstein, Rosenthal and Jacobson, Ball, Willis, Mac an Ghaill, Gillborn, Merton, Cohen, Cloward and Ohlin, Becker, Lemert, Chambliss, Taylor Walton and Young, Lea and Young, Wilson and Kelling, Murray, Heidensohn, Pollak, Messerschmidt, Situ and Emmons, Green and Ward, Foucault, Miers, Murdock, Engels, Zaretsky, Delphy and Leonard, Young and Willmott, Oakley, Duncombe and Marsden, Bott, Pahl, Aries, Postman, Palmer, Smart, Giddens, Stacey, Weber, Troeltsch, Niebuhr, Wallis, Heelas, Davie, Bruce, Wilson, Berger, Stark and Bainbridge, Comte, Popper, Kuhn, Mead, Blumer, Lyotard, Baudrillard, Beck, Gouldner) is a genuine, correctly attributed piece of sociology, checked against real published sociological knowledge and AQA's own specification content when this course was built -- present it as real scholarship, never as illustrative fiction, and correct the learner explicitly if they misattribute a theory or misstate a finding. Theory and Methods (3.1.3/3.3.2) is genuinely common content examined on both Paper 1 (10 marks) and Paper 3 (30 marks) -- teach it once (S07-S11) and remind the learner it will reappear, at a different tariff, on both papers. AQA's extended-writing items (4/6-mark 'Outline...' items are point-based, scored per developed reason; 10/20/30-mark items are marked by best-fit levels of response, not summed points) -- teach the learner to write in continuous, structured prose for 10-mark-and-above items, always explicitly using the given Item's own detail where a question asks the learner to apply material from it, and to build toward a substantiated evaluative conclusion on 20/30-mark essays rather than a simple list of points. Some content in this course (e.g. current UK divorce-rate or migration figures) is genuinely time-bound; where flagged, check the latest ONS release rather than relying on a fixed number, since the sociological explanation/theory matters far more for the exam than any single current statistic.

#### TM.9 Modernity and postmodernity in relation to sociological theory
**Modernity and postmodernity.** Classical sociological theories (functionalism, Marxism, and much feminism) developed to explain modern society -- industrial, nation-state-based, with relatively stable social structures (class, family, work) that gave individuals a fairly fixed, predictable identity and life course, and a belief in progress through science/reason. Postmodernists (e.g. Lyotard) argue society has moved beyond modernity into a postmodern condition characterised by fragmentation, the collapse of 'grand narratives' (large, totalising theories like Marxism or functionalism that claim to explain the whole of society and predict its future) in favour of many smaller, competing truths/perspectives, and a media-saturated culture of images, consumption and constructed, fluid identity rather than one fixed by class or occupation. Baudrillard's concept of hyperreality argues media and consumer culture have made the distinction between the 'real' and its media representation increasingly meaningless. Critics (e.g. late-modernists such as Giddens and Beck) argue society has not broken from modernity altogether but has intensified/radicalised some modern trends (globalisation, reflexivity, risk) while structures like class and, arguably, patriarchy remain real and consequential, making postmodernism's claim of a wholesale break overstated -- Giddens's concept of reflexive modernity and Beck's 'risk society' (where modern society increasingly organises around managing risks, e.g. environmental/technological, that it has itself created) are offered as more accurate accounts of continuity-with-change than a full postmodern break. These debates matter for evaluating any theory in this course -- functionalist, Marxist or feminist claims about family, education, religion or crime can each be challenged by asking whether they assume a fixed, stable, modern society that postmodernists argue no longer exists.

#### TM.10 Value freedom, and the relationship between sociological theory/methods and social policy
**Value freedom and the relationship between theory/methods and social policy.** Value freedom is the idea, associated with positivism and with Weber's own methodological writing (even though Weber championed Verstehen), that a sociologist's research and conclusions should be kept separate from their personal political/moral opinions, so findings are objective rather than distorted by the researcher's own values -- Weber himself argued values legitimately shape which topics a sociologist chooses to study, but should not distort how the research is then conducted or its findings interpreted. Critics argue true value freedom is impossible in practice: Gouldner argued all sociology is inevitably value-laden, since choosing a topic, a theory, a method, and how to interpret findings all involve value judgements, whether or not a researcher admits it; feminist and Marxist sociologists have often explicitly rejected value freedom as an unhelpful ideal, arguing sociology should be openly committed to exposing and challenging inequality (e.g. patriarchy, capitalism) rather than pretending neutrality while implicitly reproducing existing power structures through 'objective'-sounding but actually value-laden categories. Theory, methods and social policy: functionalists have traditionally been positive about sociology informing government policy (since functionalism assumes broad social consensus about what a 'healthy' society looks like, e.g. compensatory education policies, S05); Marxists are more sceptical, arguing policy made within a fundamentally unequal capitalist system can only ever be a limited reform, not a real solution, and that state-funded research risks being shaped by what policymakers want to hear; the New Right has been especially influential in shaping recent UK social policy directly (marketisation in education, S05, and welfare policy) from an explicitly value-committed free-market position, showing that sociological theory and government policy are often closely, and controversially, entangled rather than cleanly separated.
