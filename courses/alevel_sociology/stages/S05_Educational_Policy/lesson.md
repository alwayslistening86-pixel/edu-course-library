# S05_Educational_Policy - Lesson: Selection, marketisation and privatisation; policy for equality and globalisation

## Goal
The learner explains policies of selection, marketisation and privatisation, and policies intended to achieve greater equality of opportunity, including the impact of globalisation on education policy.

## Syllabus items taught here
- EDU.8 - Educational policy: selection (tripartite/comprehensive), marketisation and privatisation
- EDU.9 - Educational policy: compensatory/equality policies and the impact of globalisation on policy

## How to teach this
Ask: if parents can choose any school and schools compete for pupils like businesses compete for customers, who benefits and who loses out? Every named theorist and study in this course (e.g. Durkheim, Parsons, Bowles and Gintis, Althusser, Bourdieu, Bernstein, Rosenthal and Jacobson, Ball, Willis, Mac an Ghaill, Gillborn, Merton, Cohen, Cloward and Ohlin, Becker, Lemert, Chambliss, Taylor Walton and Young, Lea and Young, Wilson and Kelling, Murray, Heidensohn, Pollak, Messerschmidt, Situ and Emmons, Green and Ward, Foucault, Miers, Murdock, Engels, Zaretsky, Delphy and Leonard, Young and Willmott, Oakley, Duncombe and Marsden, Bott, Pahl, Aries, Postman, Palmer, Smart, Giddens, Stacey, Weber, Troeltsch, Niebuhr, Wallis, Heelas, Davie, Bruce, Wilson, Berger, Stark and Bainbridge, Comte, Popper, Kuhn, Mead, Blumer, Lyotard, Baudrillard, Beck, Gouldner) is a genuine, correctly attributed piece of sociology, checked against real published sociological knowledge and AQA's own specification content when this course was built -- present it as real scholarship, never as illustrative fiction, and correct the learner explicitly if they misattribute a theory or misstate a finding. Theory and Methods (3.1.3/3.3.2) is genuinely common content examined on both Paper 1 (10 marks) and Paper 3 (30 marks) -- teach it once (S07-S11) and remind the learner it will reappear, at a different tariff, on both papers. AQA's extended-writing items (4/6-mark 'Outline...' items are point-based, scored per developed reason; 10/20/30-mark items are marked by best-fit levels of response, not summed points) -- teach the learner to write in continuous, structured prose for 10-mark-and-above items, always explicitly using the given Item's own detail where a question asks the learner to apply material from it, and to build toward a substantiated evaluative conclusion on 20/30-mark essays rather than a simple list of points. Some content in this course (e.g. current UK divorce-rate or migration figures) is genuinely time-bound; where flagged, check the latest ONS release rather than relying on a fixed number, since the sociological explanation/theory matters far more for the exam than any single current statistic.

#### EDU.8 Educational policy: selection (tripartite/comprehensive), marketisation and privatisation
**Selection, marketisation and privatisation.** The tripartite system (created by the 1944 Education Act) selected pupils at age 11 (the 11-plus exam) into grammar, secondary modern or technical schools, intended to match pupils to appropriately different types of education but criticised for reproducing class inequality (middle-class pupils disproportionately passed) and for the self-fulfilling effect of being labelled a '11-plus failure'. The comprehensive system (from 1965) replaced most grammar/secondary modern schools with all-ability comprehensives, intended to reduce this class-based selection, though some grammar schools remain in parts of England and streaming/setting within comprehensives can reproduce similar effects (S04). Marketisation (from the Education Reform Act 1988 onward): introduced market principles into state education -- parental choice, league tables (published exam results), Ofsted inspection, and open enrolment/funding following pupil numbers -- on the New Right assumption (S01) that competition between schools would drive up standards as in a genuine market. Ball's research and others' identified marketisation's unequal effects: 'cream-skimming' (popular, high-performing schools can effectively select pupils, e.g. via catchment areas/oversubscription criteria, favouring pupils whose parents have the cultural and economic capital -- Ball's term 'parentocracy' critiqued the assumption that choice is genuinely equal -- to navigate the system); league tables incentivise schools to focus resources on borderline grade-C/D pupils (the 'A-to-C economy', Gillborn, S03) rather than those already secure or those unlikely to reach the threshold. Privatisation: growth of academies and free schools (run independently of local authority control, though state-funded) and increasing involvement of private businesses in providing services (catering, exam marking, sponsorship) to state schools -- debated as bringing efficiency and innovation versus reducing democratic local accountability and introducing profit motives into education.

#### EDU.9 Educational policy: compensatory/equality policies and the impact of globalisation on policy
**Policies to achieve greater equality, and globalisation.** Compensatory education policies aim to offset material and cultural deprivation directly: Education Action Zones and Sure Start (targeting early-years support in deprived areas) in England; the Pupil Premium (extra funding attached to pupils eligible for free school meals, to be spent on closing the attainment gap) is a current example, though its long-term impact on closing the class gap remains debated. Policies aimed at gender equality (GIST, WISE, a more gender-neutral National Curriculum, S02) and at reducing racial inequality (multicultural/anti-racist curriculum reforms, monitoring of exclusion rates by ethnicity) are further examples of policy explicitly targeting achievement gaps identified by sociological research. Globalisation and educational policy: international comparison (e.g. league tables like PISA, run by the OECD, ranking countries' pupils' performance) has driven policy borrowing, with governments adopting practices associated with high-performing systems (e.g. aspects of the English National Curriculum reforms citing East Asian systems); the global spread of the marketisation model (parental choice, school competition, standardised testing) beyond England reflects a wider international policy trend, sometimes linked by sociologists to neoliberal economic ideology spreading with globalisation; increased international student migration and a more ethnically diverse pupil population in many countries has also shaped policy debates about multicultural education and the ethnocentric curriculum (S03).
