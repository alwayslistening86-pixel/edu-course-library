# S20_Crime_Class_Gender_and_Ethnicity - Lesson: Crime and social class, gender and ethnicity

## Goal
The learner explains patterns and sociological explanations of crime and deviance by social class, gender and ethnicity.

## Syllabus items taught here
- CD.5 - Crime and social class, and crime and gender
- CD.6 - Crime and ethnicity

## How to teach this
Ask: official statistics show far more men than women are convicted of crime -- does that mean women genuinely commit far less crime, or could it partly reflect how crime is policed, prosecuted and recorded? Every named theorist and study in this course (e.g. Durkheim, Parsons, Bowles and Gintis, Althusser, Bourdieu, Bernstein, Rosenthal and Jacobson, Ball, Willis, Mac an Ghaill, Gillborn, Merton, Cohen, Cloward and Ohlin, Becker, Lemert, Chambliss, Taylor Walton and Young, Lea and Young, Wilson and Kelling, Murray, Heidensohn, Pollak, Messerschmidt, Situ and Emmons, Green and Ward, Foucault, Miers, Murdock, Engels, Zaretsky, Delphy and Leonard, Young and Willmott, Oakley, Duncombe and Marsden, Bott, Pahl, Aries, Postman, Palmer, Smart, Giddens, Stacey, Weber, Troeltsch, Niebuhr, Wallis, Heelas, Davie, Bruce, Wilson, Berger, Stark and Bainbridge, Comte, Popper, Kuhn, Mead, Blumer, Lyotard, Baudrillard, Beck, Gouldner) is a genuine, correctly attributed piece of sociology, checked against real published sociological knowledge and AQA's own specification content when this course was built -- present it as real scholarship, never as illustrative fiction, and correct the learner explicitly if they misattribute a theory or misstate a finding. Theory and Methods (3.1.3/3.3.2) is genuinely common content examined on both Paper 1 (10 marks) and Paper 3 (30 marks) -- teach it once (S07-S11) and remind the learner it will reappear, at a different tariff, on both papers. AQA's extended-writing items (4/6-mark 'Outline...' items are point-based, scored per developed reason; 10/20/30-mark items are marked by best-fit levels of response, not summed points) -- teach the learner to write in continuous, structured prose for 10-mark-and-above items, always explicitly using the given Item's own detail where a question asks the learner to apply material from it, and to build toward a substantiated evaluative conclusion on 20/30-mark essays rather than a simple list of points. Some content in this course (e.g. current UK divorce-rate or migration figures) is genuinely time-bound; where flagged, check the latest ONS release rather than relying on a fixed number, since the sociological explanation/theory matters far more for the exam than any single current statistic.

#### CD.5 Crime and social class, and crime and gender
**Crime, class and gender.** Class: official statistics consistently show working-class people over-represented among those convicted of crime, but sociologists caution this may partly reflect differential policing/labelling (S19) of working-class areas/behaviour as much as any real difference in offending, alongside genuine explanations rooted in relative deprivation, strain and subculture (S18-S19); white-collar and corporate crime (committed by higher-status offenders, e.g. fraud, health and safety violations, tax evasion) is argued by Marxist and other critical sociologists to be significantly under-recorded and under-prosecuted relative to its real financial/physical harm, distorting the apparent class pattern of crime found in official statistics. Gender: men are convicted of crime at consistently and substantially higher rates than women across virtually all offence categories in official statistics; explanations for women's comparatively lower offending include: differential socialisation (girls traditionally socialised toward conformity, closer supervision and less opportunity/expectation for risk-taking than boys); control theory (Heidensohn) -- women's behaviour is more closely controlled at home (domestic responsibilities, restricted freedom), in public (fear of male violence restricting women's movement/behaviour), and at work, reducing opportunity to offend; the chivalry thesis (Pollak) argues the criminal justice system (disproportionately male at senior levels) treats female offenders more leniently than men for comparable offences, meaning official statistics understate real female offending -- contested by others (e.g. some research finding women can be treated more harshly for offences seen as 'doubly deviant', breaking both the law and expected gender norms). Explanations for male offending, and for the changing (though still much lower) female crime rate, include changing gender roles/opportunities (some liberal feminists linking rising recorded female crime partly to women's changing social position) and masculinity theories (Messerschmidt) arguing crime can be a resource some men use to construct/accomplish a masculine identity, particularly where other markers of masculinity (e.g. a stable job) are unavailable.

#### CD.6 Crime and ethnicity
**Crime and ethnicity.** Official statistics and criminal justice data have shown some minority ethnic groups, particularly Black people, over-represented at various stages of the criminal justice system (e.g. stop and search rates, arrest, prosecution and custodial sentencing) relative to their share of the population, a pattern that has attracted sustained sociological and policy attention (including, historically, the Macpherson Report following the Stephen Lawrence case, which controversially found institutional racism within the Metropolitan Police). Explanations divide broadly into two families. Left realist and other explanations emphasising real offending differences point to relative deprivation, marginalisation and, for some groups, disproportionate exposure to poverty/unemployment (linking back to S19's left realist framework), while being careful to avoid treating 'ethnic minorities' as a single homogeneous group, since patterns (and relative deprivation itself) vary significantly between different minority ethnic groups. Explanations emphasising differential treatment/labelling by the criminal justice system point to disproportionate stop-and-search rates (raising arrest rates independent of any underlying offending difference), possible unconscious or institutional bias in policing, charging and sentencing decisions (echoing Gillborn's institutional racism argument in education, EDU.4), and media stereotyping of crime as linked to particular ethnic groups (S21) potentially reinforcing both public perception and police practice in a self-reinforcing cycle. Sociologists studying victimisation (S23) also note some minority ethnic groups experience disproportionately higher rates of certain victimisation (e.g. racially motivated hate crime), a further, related but distinct dimension of the ethnicity-and-crime relationship. As with class and gender, the validity of official statistics themselves (what is recorded, how, and by whom) is central to interpreting any of these patterns, linking directly to S23's treatment of measuring crime.
