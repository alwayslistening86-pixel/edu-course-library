# S15_Religion_Ideology_and_Social_Change - Lesson: Ideology, science and religion; religion and social change/stability

## Goal
The learner explains ideology, science and religion as belief systems, and the relationship between religion and social change/stability, including functionalist, Marxist and Weberian perspectives.

## Syllabus items taught here
- BEL.1 - Ideology, science and religion as belief systems, and Christian/non-Christian religious traditions
- BEL.2 - Religion and social change/social stability: functionalist, Marxist, feminist and Weberian perspectives

## How to teach this
Ask: can a religious idea ever cause economic change, or does religion only ever reflect and justify the economic system that already exists? Every named theorist and study in this course (e.g. Durkheim, Parsons, Bowles and Gintis, Althusser, Bourdieu, Bernstein, Rosenthal and Jacobson, Ball, Willis, Mac an Ghaill, Gillborn, Merton, Cohen, Cloward and Ohlin, Becker, Lemert, Chambliss, Taylor Walton and Young, Lea and Young, Wilson and Kelling, Murray, Heidensohn, Pollak, Messerschmidt, Situ and Emmons, Green and Ward, Foucault, Miers, Murdock, Engels, Zaretsky, Delphy and Leonard, Young and Willmott, Oakley, Duncombe and Marsden, Bott, Pahl, Aries, Postman, Palmer, Smart, Giddens, Stacey, Weber, Troeltsch, Niebuhr, Wallis, Heelas, Davie, Bruce, Wilson, Berger, Stark and Bainbridge, Comte, Popper, Kuhn, Mead, Blumer, Lyotard, Baudrillard, Beck, Gouldner) is a genuine, correctly attributed piece of sociology, checked against real published sociological knowledge and AQA's own specification content when this course was built -- present it as real scholarship, never as illustrative fiction, and correct the learner explicitly if they misattribute a theory or misstate a finding. #### BEL.1 Ideology, science and religion as belief systems, and Christian/non-Christian religious traditions
**Ideology, science and religion.** Sociologists treat religion as one of several belief systems (alongside ideology and science) that provide a framework for understanding the world and guiding action, without assuming any is simply 'true' or 'false' -- the sociological question is about religion's social role and effects, not its theological truth. Religion is often defined either substantively (by its content -- belief in the supernatural/sacred, per Durkheim's classic distinction between the sacred, set apart and treated with reverence, and the profane, the ordinary/everyday) or functionally (by what it does for individuals/society, regardless of specific supernatural content, allowing civil religion or even some secular ideologies to be analysed in similar terms). Science, by contrast, claims to build objective, testable, falsifiable knowledge through evidence and open peer scrutiny (Popper); some sociologists (Horton) contrast religious 'closed' systems of thought (which do not accommodate challenges to core beliefs) with science's 'open' system (which revises itself in light of contrary evidence), though this distinction is itself debated, since scientific paradigms (Kuhn) also show strong resistance to challenge until a decisive paradigm shift. Ideology refers to a comprehensive, action-guiding set of beliefs and values often (in Marxist usage) understood as serving particular class interests by making a particular social arrangement seem natural, inevitable or fair -- Marx himself described religion as a form of ideology, famously calling it 'the opium of the people', numbing the working class to their real exploitation with the promise of reward in an afterlife rather than encouraging revolutionary change in this one. Both Christian traditions (e.g. Catholicism, Protestantism, with genuinely different organisational and doctrinal features relevant to Weber's argument, BEL.2) and major non-Christian world religions (e.g. Islam, Hinduism, Buddhism, Sikhism, Judaism) are studied comparatively for how their beliefs, organisation and practice relate to the social patterns covered across this topic.

#### BEL.2 Religion and social change/social stability: functionalist, Marxist, feminist and Weberian perspectives
**Religion, social change and social stability.** Functionalism (Durkheim): religion promotes social integration and value consensus by uniting believers in shared rituals and symbols that represent, and reinforce commitment to, society itself (Durkheim's totemism study argued the sacred object symbolically represents the social group/collective conscience); religion is therefore fundamentally a conservative force, maintaining existing social order and cohesion rather than driving change. Marxism: as above (BEL.1), religion is ideology that legitimates and stabilises existing class inequality by offering compensation in an imagined afterlife and by directly justifying the existing social order (e.g. historic doctrines of the 'divine right of kings', or teachings encouraging acceptance of one's earthly station) -- also fundamentally a conservative force, though serving ruling-class rather than collective interests. Feminism: religion has historically been highly patriarchal in its organisation (predominantly male religious leadership in most major traditions) and doctrine (teachings that can be used to justify traditional, subordinate female gender roles), another broadly conservative force reinforcing gender inequality, though some feminists note religion can also be a source of meaning, community, and even feminist theological reinterpretation for some women. Weber's alternative view directly challenges the 'religion is always conservative' consensus: his study of The Protestant Ethic and the Spirit of Capitalism argued that Calvinist Protestant beliefs (predestination, and the doctrine that worldly economic success could be read as a sign, though not a guarantee, of being one of God's elect, combined with an ascetic, frugal lifestyle discouraging consumption/luxury) created a distinctive psychological drive to work hard, succeed and reinvest profit rather than spend it, which Weber argued was a genuine ideological cause contributing to the rise of modern rational capitalism in Protestant parts of Europe -- showing religion could, at least at a specific historical moment, be a force for radical social change rather than pure stability. Liberation theology (a movement within Latin American Catholicism, blending Christian teaching with active support for the poor against oppressive regimes) is a further modern example sociologists cite of religion actively driving, rather than blocking, social change.
