# S23_Victims_and_Measuring_Crime - Lesson: Victims of crime, and measuring crime

## Goal
The learner explains sociological perspectives on victims of crime, and the strengths and limitations of the main ways crime is measured (official statistics, victim surveys and self-report studies).

## Syllabus items taught here
- CD.10 - Victims of crime: positivist and critical victimology, and patterns of victimisation
- CD.11 - Measuring crime: official statistics, victim surveys and self-report studies

## How to teach this
Ask: if the official crime statistics only count crimes reported to and recorded by the police, what kinds of crime and victim might be almost invisible in the data? #### CD.10 Victims of crime: positivist and critical victimology, and patterns of victimisation
**Victims of crime.** Victimology studies patterns of victimisation and society's response to victims. Positivist victimology (Miers, following an approach paralleling early positivist criminology) focuses on identifying patterns of victim proneness/precipitation -- factors that statistically make certain individuals or groups more likely to be victims (e.g. lifestyle exposure, going out at night, particular occupations) -- criticised for risking 'victim blaming' by implying the victim's own behaviour or characteristics caused their victimisation, shifting focus from the offender. Critical victimology (drawing on Marxist and feminist ideas) instead focuses on structural factors -- patterns of power, poverty and inequality -- that make some groups (e.g. women, the poor, some minority ethnic groups) disproportionately vulnerable to victimisation and, separately, on how the state's own definitions and processes (who officially counts as a 'victim', how the criminal justice system treats them) can itself constitute a form of secondary victimisation, e.g. victims re-traumatised by an unsympathetic investigation or trial process. Patterns of victimisation found by research (notably the Crime Survey for England and Wales) show, counter to some popular fear-of-crime patterns, young men are statistically among the groups most likely to be victims of violent crime (though the elderly often report higher fear of crime despite lower statistical risk), and that victimisation is often patterned by the same structural inequalities (class, ethnicity, area) associated with offending (S20), with repeat victimisation also concentrated in already-disadvantaged communities. Feminist research has been particularly influential in highlighting the under-reporting and under-recording of crimes disproportionately affecting women (e.g. domestic violence, sexual assault), where the 'dark figure' of unrecorded crime (S23's next item) is argued to be especially large.

#### CD.11 Measuring crime: official statistics, victim surveys and self-report studies
**Measuring crime: official statistics, victim surveys and self-report studies.** Official statistics (police-recorded crime) are cheap, available over long time periods (allowing trend analysis) and cover the whole country, but only capture crimes that are both reported to and then recorded by the police -- creating a substantial 'dark figure' of unrecorded crime from crimes never reported (e.g. due to embarrassment, fear, not seeing it as serious enough, or (per critical victimology) distrust of the police among some groups) and crimes reported but not recorded (due to police discretion, resourcing, or changing recording practices/definitions over time, which can make trend comparisons across years misleading even without any real change in offending). Victim surveys (notably the Crime Survey for England and Wales, formerly the British Crime Survey) ask a large, randomly sampled group of people directly about their experience of crime regardless of whether they reported it to police, generally producing a higher, and often considered more valid, estimate of overall crime levels than police-recorded statistics -- but still miss crimes the victim is unaware of or unwilling to disclose even anonymously (e.g. some domestic/sexual offences), exclude victimless/corporate crime and crimes against businesses/institutions (partly addressed by separate commercial victimisation surveys), and depend on respondents' memory and willingness to be fully honest. Self-report studies ask (often young) people directly to report their own offending, anonymously, potentially revealing types and amounts of offending (including that of higher-status or younger people less likely to appear in official statistics) invisible to both police records and victim surveys -- but reliability and validity depend heavily on respondents' honesty (both exaggeration and under-reporting are risks), and samples (often school-based) may not be representative of the whole offending population. Because each method has a different, partial 'dark figure' and different systematic biases, sociologists generally treat no single measure as a fully valid, complete picture of the real extent of crime -- a key theoretical-methods point (linking to TM.5-TM.6) that should be applied whenever a question asks about crime trends or patterns.
