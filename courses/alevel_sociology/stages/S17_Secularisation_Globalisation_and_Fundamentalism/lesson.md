# S17_Secularisation_Globalisation_and_Fundamentalism - Lesson: The secularisation debate, globalisation and religion, and fundamentalism

## Goal
The learner explains the secularisation debate, the impact of globalisation on religion, and religious/fundamentalist responses to modernity.

## Syllabus items taught here
- BEL.5 - The secularisation debate, religion and globalisation, and religious fundamentalism

## How to teach this
Ask: if fewer people attend church than a century ago but belief in 'something spiritual' hasn't disappeared, has society actually secularised -- or just changed how it is religious? Every named theorist and study in this course (e.g. Durkheim, Parsons, Bowles and Gintis, Althusser, Bourdieu, Bernstein, Rosenthal and Jacobson, Ball, Willis, Mac an Ghaill, Gillborn, Merton, Cohen, Cloward and Ohlin, Becker, Lemert, Chambliss, Taylor Walton and Young, Lea and Young, Wilson and Kelling, Murray, Heidensohn, Pollak, Messerschmidt, Situ and Emmons, Green and Ward, Foucault, Miers, Murdock, Engels, Zaretsky, Delphy and Leonard, Young and Willmott, Oakley, Duncombe and Marsden, Bott, Pahl, Aries, Postman, Palmer, Smart, Giddens, Stacey, Weber, Troeltsch, Niebuhr, Wallis, Heelas, Davie, Bruce, Wilson, Berger, Stark and Bainbridge, Comte, Popper, Kuhn, Mead, Blumer, Lyotard, Baudrillard, Beck, Gouldner) is a genuine, correctly attributed piece of sociology, checked against real published sociological knowledge and AQA's own specification content when this course was built -- present it as real scholarship, never as illustrative fiction, and correct the learner explicitly if they misattribute a theory or misstate a finding. Theory and Methods (3.1.3/3.3.2) is genuinely common content examined on both Paper 1 (10 marks) and Paper 3 (30 marks) -- teach it once (S07-S11) and remind the learner it will reappear, at a different tariff, on both papers. AQA's extended-writing items (4/6-mark 'Outline...' items are point-based, scored per developed reason; 10/20/30-mark items are marked by best-fit levels of response, not summed points) -- teach the learner to write in continuous, structured prose for 10-mark-and-above items, always explicitly using the given Item's own detail where a question asks the learner to apply material from it, and to build toward a substantiated evaluative conclusion on 20/30-mark essays rather than a simple list of points. Some content in this course (e.g. current UK divorce-rate or migration figures) is genuinely time-bound; where flagged, check the latest ONS release rather than relying on a fixed number, since the sociological explanation/theory matters far more for the exam than any single current statistic.

#### BEL.5 The secularisation debate, religion and globalisation, and religious fundamentalism
**Secularisation, globalisation and fundamentalism.** The secularisation thesis (Wilson, Bruce) argues religion's social significance has declined in modern, especially Western European, societies -- evidenced by falling church attendance/membership, declining religious influence over law, politics and everyday life (differentiation, where religion loses authority to now-separate, specialised secular institutions like science, the state and law), and religious pluralism/diversity itself undermining any single religion's authority as absolute truth (Berger's argument that the mere fact of competing religious options weakens the plausibility of any one of them). Wilson linked secularisation to rationalisation (the long-term, Weber-influenced shift from religious/traditional to scientific/rational modes of thinking and explanation) accompanying industrialisation and modernity. Critics dispute both the evidence and the underlying theory: Davie's influential concept of 'believing without belonging' argues formal religious institutional participation has declined while private belief and spirituality remain widespread, suggesting a change in the form of religiosity rather than a simple decline; Davie and others note the USA remains highly religious despite being highly modern/industrialised, challenging any simple equation of modernity with secularisation; Stark and Bainbridge's rational choice theory of religion argues religious demand is roughly constant, so apparent decline in one form (mainstream churches) is offset by growth elsewhere (New Age movements, sects, new religious movements marketing themselves competitively) -- a persistent 'religious market' rather than an overall decline. Globalisation and religion: increased global interconnection has spread religions beyond their traditional regions (e.g. Pentecostal Christianity's rapid global growth, particularly in the Global South), enabled by modern communication/media and migration, while also exposing traditional religious communities to increased contact with secular and competing religious ideas. Religious fundamentalism (Davie, Bruce): a reaction against, rather than simply a survival from, modernity -- fundamentalist movements (found across multiple traditions, not only one) typically assert the literal, absolute truth of sacred texts/teachings against modern relativism/pluralism, reject secular-liberal social changes (e.g. around gender roles or sexuality) they see as morally corrosive, and often actively use modern technology/media to organise and spread their message, making fundamentalism better understood as a genuinely modern, globalised phenomenon responding to secularisation and pluralism than as a simple throwback to the past.
