# S10_Positivism_Interpretivism_and_Sociological_Theory - Lesson: Positivism, interpretivism, sociology as science, and consensus/conflict/structural/social action theories

## Goal
The learner explains positivism and interpretivism as approaches to sociological knowledge, the debate over whether sociology can be a science, and the distinction between consensus, conflict, structural and social action theories.

## Syllabus items taught here
- TM.7 - Positivism, interpretivism, and the debate on sociology as a science
- TM.8 - Consensus, conflict, structural and social action theories

## How to teach this
Ask: can you study human beings the same way a chemist studies chemicals -- or does the fact that people have their own meanings and choices make sociology a fundamentally different kind of science, if it is a science at all? Every named theorist and study in this course (e.g. Durkheim, Parsons, Bowles and Gintis, Althusser, Bourdieu, Bernstein, Rosenthal and Jacobson, Ball, Willis, Mac an Ghaill, Gillborn, Merton, Cohen, Cloward and Ohlin, Becker, Lemert, Chambliss, Taylor Walton and Young, Lea and Young, Wilson and Kelling, Murray, Heidensohn, Pollak, Messerschmidt, Situ and Emmons, Green and Ward, Foucault, Miers, Murdock, Engels, Zaretsky, Delphy and Leonard, Young and Willmott, Oakley, Duncombe and Marsden, Bott, Pahl, Aries, Postman, Palmer, Smart, Giddens, Stacey, Weber, Troeltsch, Niebuhr, Wallis, Heelas, Davie, Bruce, Wilson, Berger, Stark and Bainbridge, Comte, Popper, Kuhn, Mead, Blumer, Lyotard, Baudrillard, Beck, Gouldner) is a genuine, correctly attributed piece of sociology, checked against real published sociological knowledge and AQA's own specification content when this course was built -- present it as real scholarship, never as illustrative fiction, and correct the learner explicitly if they misattribute a theory or misstate a finding. Theory and Methods (3.1.3/3.3.2) is genuinely common content examined on both Paper 1 (10 marks) and Paper 3 (30 marks) -- teach it once (S07-S11) and remind the learner it will reappear, at a different tariff, on both papers. AQA's extended-writing items (4/6-mark 'Outline...' items are point-based, scored per developed reason; 10/20/30-mark items are marked by best-fit levels of response, not summed points) -- teach the learner to write in continuous, structured prose for 10-mark-and-above items, always explicitly using the given Item's own detail where a question asks the learner to apply material from it, and to build toward a substantiated evaluative conclusion on 20/30-mark essays rather than a simple list of points. Some content in this course (e.g. current UK divorce-rate or migration figures) is genuinely time-bound; where flagged, check the latest ONS release rather than relying on a fixed number, since the sociological explanation/theory matters far more for the exam than any single current statistic.

#### TM.7 Positivism, interpretivism, and the debate on sociology as a science
**Positivism, interpretivism, and sociology as a science.** Positivism (associated with Comte and Durkheim) holds that society can and should be studied using the methods of the natural sciences: identifying objective 'social facts' that exist independently of individuals, measuring them quantitatively, and establishing causal laws/correlations through large-scale, reliable, representative research (e.g. Durkheim's statistical study of suicide rates across groups, treating suicide as caused by social factors like differing levels of social integration/regulation, not just individual psychology). Interpretivism (associated with Weber and the symbolic interactionist tradition) rejects the idea that human behaviour can be explained like the behaviour of unthinking objects, arguing sociology must instead achieve Verstehen (empathetic understanding) of the subjective meanings people themselves give to their actions -- the same outward act (e.g. raising an arm) can mean entirely different things depending on the actor's intent, so a valid explanation must recover that meaning, favouring qualitative, in-depth methods. The debate over whether sociology is/can be a science: the philosopher Karl Popper argued a genuine science must produce falsifiable claims (ones that could in principle be proven wrong by evidence); critics question whether large, complex, meaning-laden social theories (e.g. Marxism, or aspects of functionalism) are genuinely falsifiable, or whether they are unfalsifiable and therefore not properly scientific by this standard; Kuhn's concept of a paradigm (a broadly shared framework of assumptions and methods within a scientific community) has been applied to ask whether sociology has ever achieved the kind of single shared paradigm natural sciences like physics have, or remains inherently multi-paradigmatic (positivist, interpretivist, Marxist, feminist, etc., each with incompatible assumptions) -- a state some see as sociology's weakness and others see as reflecting the genuinely more complex, meaning-constituted nature of its subject matter compared with the natural world.

#### TM.8 Consensus, conflict, structural and social action theories
**Consensus, conflict, structural and social action theories.** Structural (macro) theories explain individual behaviour as shaped by the wider structure of society -- social institutions, norms and the individual's position within them -- rather than treating individuals as fully free agents; structural theories divide into consensus and conflict approaches. Consensus theory (functionalism, Durkheim/Parsons) sees society as fundamentally based on shared values (value consensus) that hold it together like a healthy organism's organs work together, with institutions (family, education, religion) functioning to maintain social order, stability and integration -- deviance/conflict is treated as a temporary malfunction to be explained and, where excessive, corrected, rather than as built into the system. Conflict theories instead see society as fundamentally divided by competing interests, with some groups benefiting at others' expense: Marxism sees the key division as social class, with the ruling (bourgeoisie) class controlling the means of production and using institutions (education, media, religion, the state) to maintain ideological control over the working class (proletariat), whose eventual revolutionary overthrow of capitalism Marx predicted; feminism sees the key division as gender/patriarchy, with institutions organised (historically) to benefit men at women's expense, though feminism itself divides into liberal, radical, Marxist/socialist and other strands differing on the cause and depth of this inequality (see also S16-S17 on feminist perspectives on family). Social action (micro) theories, by contrast, start from the individual and their subjective meanings and interactions rather than from society's structure -- symbolic interactionism (Mead, Blumer, applied to labelling theory in S03/S22) argues society is built up from countless small-scale interactions in which individuals interpret symbols/situations and construct meaning together, so explanation must start there rather than from an abstract structure 'above' individuals; this is closely linked to interpretivist methodology (TM.7).
