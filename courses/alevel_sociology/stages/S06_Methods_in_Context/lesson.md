# S06_Methods_in_Context - Lesson: Applying research methods specifically to studying education

## Goal
The learner explains the practical, ethical and theoretical issues specific to using sociological research methods to study education -- researching pupils, teachers and schools.

## Syllabus items taught here
- MIC.1 - Methods in Context: practical, ethical and theoretical issues specific to researching education

## How to teach this
Ask: if you wanted to find out honestly why some pupils in a school mess about in lessons, why might just asking them in a formal interview get you a misleading answer? Every named theorist and study in this course (e.g. Durkheim, Parsons, Bowles and Gintis, Althusser, Bourdieu, Bernstein, Rosenthal and Jacobson, Ball, Willis, Mac an Ghaill, Gillborn, Merton, Cohen, Cloward and Ohlin, Becker, Lemert, Chambliss, Taylor Walton and Young, Lea and Young, Wilson and Kelling, Murray, Heidensohn, Pollak, Messerschmidt, Situ and Emmons, Green and Ward, Foucault, Miers, Murdock, Engels, Zaretsky, Delphy and Leonard, Young and Willmott, Oakley, Duncombe and Marsden, Bott, Pahl, Aries, Postman, Palmer, Smart, Giddens, Stacey, Weber, Troeltsch, Niebuhr, Wallis, Heelas, Davie, Bruce, Wilson, Berger, Stark and Bainbridge, Comte, Popper, Kuhn, Mead, Blumer, Lyotard, Baudrillard, Beck, Gouldner) is a genuine, correctly attributed piece of sociology, checked against real published sociological knowledge and AQA's own specification content when this course was built -- present it as real scholarship, never as illustrative fiction, and correct the learner explicitly if they misattribute a theory or misstate a finding. Theory and Methods (3.1.3/3.3.2) is genuinely common content examined on both Paper 1 (10 marks) and Paper 3 (30 marks) -- teach it once (S07-S11) and remind the learner it will reappear, at a different tariff, on both papers. AQA's extended-writing items (4/6-mark 'Outline...' items are point-based, scored per developed reason; 10/20/30-mark items are marked by best-fit levels of response, not summed points) -- teach the learner to write in continuous, structured prose for 10-mark-and-above items, always explicitly using the given Item's own detail where a question asks the learner to apply material from it, and to build toward a substantiated evaluative conclusion on 20/30-mark essays rather than a simple list of points. Some content in this course (e.g. current UK divorce-rate or migration figures) is genuinely time-bound; where flagged, check the latest ONS release rather than relying on a fixed number, since the sociological explanation/theory matters far more for the exam than any single current statistic.

#### MIC.1 Methods in Context: practical, ethical and theoretical issues specific to researching education
**Methods in Context: researching education.** Paper 1's Methods in Context question (20 marks, Section B) asks the learner to evaluate a named research method (e.g. questionnaires, unstructured interviews, participant observation) specifically for researching a given issue in education, using both general method strengths/weaknesses (covered in S07-S11) and issues distinctive to the school/classroom setting. Access and gatekeepers: researchers need permission from head teachers, governors, and often parents (in loco parentis) before researching pupils, who as minors cannot straightforwardly give full informed consent themselves -- gatekeepers may restrict access or steer a researcher toward less sensitive topics, limiting validity. Power and status differences: the unequal power relationship between adult researchers and child/teenage pupils can distort responses -- pupils may tell a researcher (especially one who reminds them of a teacher) what they think is wanted, reducing validity of interviews/questionnaires; conversely, observation in a classroom may itself change pupil behaviour (the observer effect) since pupils are already used to being watched/assessed by teachers, which can cut both ways (some argue observation is less disruptive in a school than elsewhere, since being watched is normal there). Ethical issues are heightened with children: informed consent, protection from harm, and confidentiality all need extra care (e.g. researching bullying or teacher labelling risks distress or getting a pupil into trouble). Practical issues: the school timetable and exam pressures constrain when research can happen; the formal, hierarchical setting of a classroom can make certain methods (e.g. group interviews reproducing classroom power dynamics) less effective at generating honest, unprompted talk than an informal setting; teachers themselves are also a research population (pupils are not the only subject), with their own professional gatekeeping concerns (e.g. reluctance to be observed or criticised). These access, power, ethical and practical issues must be applied to the specific method and specific educational issue named in the exam question, not discussed generically.

## Explicitly not here
General strengths/weaknesses of research methods (not specific to education) are S07-S11.
