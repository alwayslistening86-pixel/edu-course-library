# S19_Labelling_Marxist_Criminology_and_Realism - Lesson: Labelling theory, Marxist criminology, and left/right realism

## Goal
The learner explains interactionist/labelling theory (including moral panics and deviancy amplification), Marxist and neo-Marxist criminology, and left and right realism.

## Syllabus items taught here
- CD.3 - Interactionist/labelling theory, deviancy amplification and moral panics
- CD.4 - Marxist and neo-Marxist criminology; left realism and right realism

## How to teach this
Ask: could the exact same act of shoplifting be treated as harmless mischief for one teenager and a life-defining criminal record for another, purely because of who they are? Every named theorist and study in this course (e.g. Durkheim, Parsons, Bowles and Gintis, Althusser, Bourdieu, Bernstein, Rosenthal and Jacobson, Ball, Willis, Mac an Ghaill, Gillborn, Merton, Cohen, Cloward and Ohlin, Becker, Lemert, Chambliss, Taylor Walton and Young, Lea and Young, Wilson and Kelling, Murray, Heidensohn, Pollak, Messerschmidt, Situ and Emmons, Green and Ward, Foucault, Miers, Murdock, Engels, Zaretsky, Delphy and Leonard, Young and Willmott, Oakley, Duncombe and Marsden, Bott, Pahl, Aries, Postman, Palmer, Smart, Giddens, Stacey, Weber, Troeltsch, Niebuhr, Wallis, Heelas, Davie, Bruce, Wilson, Berger, Stark and Bainbridge, Comte, Popper, Kuhn, Mead, Blumer, Lyotard, Baudrillard, Beck, Gouldner) is a genuine, correctly attributed piece of sociology, checked against real published sociological knowledge and AQA's own specification content when this course was built -- present it as real scholarship, never as illustrative fiction, and correct the learner explicitly if they misattribute a theory or misstate a finding. Theory and Methods (3.1.3/3.3.2) is genuinely common content examined on both Paper 1 (10 marks) and Paper 3 (30 marks) -- teach it once (S07-S11) and remind the learner it will reappear, at a different tariff, on both papers. AQA's extended-writing items (4/6-mark 'Outline...' items are point-based, scored per developed reason; 10/20/30-mark items are marked by best-fit levels of response, not summed points) -- teach the learner to write in continuous, structured prose for 10-mark-and-above items, always explicitly using the given Item's own detail where a question asks the learner to apply material from it, and to build toward a substantiated evaluative conclusion on 20/30-mark essays rather than a simple list of points. Some content in this course (e.g. current UK divorce-rate or migration figures) is genuinely time-bound; where flagged, check the latest ONS release rather than relying on a fixed number, since the sociological explanation/theory matters far more for the exam than any single current statistic.

#### CD.3 Interactionist/labelling theory, deviancy amplification and moral panics
**Interactionism/labelling theory.** Becker's foundational statement -- 'deviance is not a quality of the act... but a consequence of the application by others of rules and sanctions to an "offender"' -- argues no act is inherently deviant; it becomes deviant only when a social audience (especially powerful 'moral entrepreneurs' who campaign to have a rule created/enforced) successfully labels it so, meaning who gets labelled (by class, ethnicity, appearance, prior record) matters as much as what they actually did -- a direct challenge to positivist criminology's search for the causes of crime 'in' the offender. Lemert distinguished primary deviance (minor, often widespread rule-breaking with little social significance, before any label is attached) from secondary deviance (deviance that results from, and is shaped by, the labelling and social reaction that follows an act becoming known/labelled) -- someone labelled a 'criminal' may find legitimate opportunities closed off, be treated with suspicion, and increasingly organise their identity and further action around that master status, potentially deepening their deviant career (a form of self-fulfilling prophecy applied to crime, mirroring EDU.5's use in education). Deviancy amplification describes a spiral in which social/media reaction to initial deviance (over-)reacts, driving the deviant group further from mainstream society (e.g. into more organised subcultures) and paradoxically increasing rather than reducing the very behaviour being reacted against. Cohen's classic study of 1960s Mods and Rockers seaside disturbances developed the concept of a moral panic -- an exaggerated, media-fuelled overreaction to a group/event, in which the group is constructed as 'folk devils', symbolically representing a wider social threat, driving calls for tougher social control that can itself amplify the very deviance it targets; moral panic theory has since been applied to many other targets (e.g. youth subcultures, immigration, particular crimes) and is linked to media agenda-setting more broadly (S21).

#### CD.4 Marxist and neo-Marxist criminology; left realism and right realism
**Marxist and neo-Marxist criminology; left and right realism.** Traditional Marxist criminology argues capitalism itself criminogenic -- crime is a rational response to the inequality, poverty and alienation capitalism produces -- and that the criminal justice system is not neutral but selectively enforces and defines law to protect ruling-class property/interests while under-policing and under-punishing much more costly corporate/white-collar crime (Chambliss); this links to Marxist analysis of the media/state as instruments of ideological/social control more broadly (TM.8). Neo-Marxist ('critical') criminology (Taylor, Walton and Young's The New Criminology) attempted to combine Marxist structural analysis with interactionist attention to the meaning of the deviant act for the actor, arguing for a 'fully social theory of deviance' considering the wider capitalist context, the immediate origins/meaning of the act, societal reaction/labelling, and its wider effects together -- criticised (including later by Young himself) for sometimes romanticising working-class criminals as quasi-political rebels against capitalism, ignoring real crime victims (a criticism left realism directly addresses). Left realism (Lea and Young) responded by taking working-class and other victims' real experience of crime seriously (rather than treating crime concern as media-manufactured moral panic or capitalist distraction), explaining crime through the combination of relative deprivation (feeling deprived relative to others, not absolute poverty alone -- linking back to strain theory), subculture (a collective response to that deprivation) and marginalisation (lacking clear organisations/political voice to express grievance legitimately), and advocating practical, community-based crime-reduction policy. Right realism (Wilson and Kelling's broken windows thesis; Murray's underclass theory) instead emphasises individual responsibility, a rational-choice view of crime as a calculated decision when the perceived benefits outweigh the risks, and rapid, visible social control of minor disorder (broken windows -- unrepaired minor disorder signals an area is uncontrolled, inviting more serious crime) alongside zero-tolerance policing, criticised by left realists and others for ignoring the structural/economic causes of crime and for potentially targeting already-marginalised communities more heavily.
