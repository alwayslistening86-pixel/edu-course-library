# S02_Specifications_Ideas_and_the_Make_and_Evaluate_Stage - Lesson: Writing specifications, generating ideas and the make-and-evaluate stage

## Goal
The learner writes a design specification from research and ACCESS FM findings, generates a range of design ideas, and explains modelling and prototyping within the make-and-evaluate stage.

## Syllabus items taught here
- R038-1.2d - Writing a design specification: write a specification from research and ACCESS FM findings, using measurable criteria
- R038-1.2e - Generating design ideas: generate a range of design ideas from a specification using recognised idea-generation approaches
- R038-1.2f - Modelling, prototyping and comparing against requirements in the make-and-evaluate stage: explain why designers model and prototype and how outcomes are compared against the specification
- R038-2.1 - Types of specification criteria: classify specification points as needs or wants and as quantitative or qualitative

## How to teach this
Ask the learner to write one sentence a client might say ("I want a bag that's light and looks good") and then turn it into something a designer could actually test against. 'What's missing that would let someone check whether the finished bag actually meets this?' Use this to introduce specification writing as translating vague wishes into measurable criteria.

#### R038-1.2d Writing a design specification
A specification is built from research findings and ACCESS FM analysis, translated into a list of criteria the finished design must meet. Strong specification points are specific and, where possible, measurable (for example "must weigh under 500g" rather than "must be light"), and each should be traceable back to a piece of research or an ACCESS FM finding, not invented. A specification with only vague, unmeasurable points cannot be properly evaluated later against the finished prototype. Activity: turn the vague client wish "a school bag that's light and looks good" into two specific, measurable specification points.

#### R038-1.2e Generating design ideas
Idea generation follows research and specification writing: producing a genuine range of different design ideas (not minor variations of one idea) that each attempt to meet the specification in a different way, usually through quick sketches and notes. A wide range at this stage gives more to evaluate and combine later; narrowing to one idea too early risks missing a better solution. Ideas should be annotated with how they meet specific specification points, not left as unexplained drawings. Activity: sketch (or describe in words) two genuinely different ideas for the light school bag and note one specification point each meets.

#### R038-1.2f Modelling, prototyping and comparing against requirements in the make-and-evaluate stage
The make-and-evaluate stage models and prototypes chosen ideas - producing something physical or virtual before or instead of a full final product - to test whether an idea meets the specification before committing to full manufacture. Modelling reduces the cost and risk of mistakes; the outcome (model, prototype or full product) is then compared directly against every specification point to see what has been met, partly met or missed, feeding findings back into further design if gaps remain. This overview connects to the specific modelling methods and evaluation methods taught in Topic Area 4 (S04) in more depth. Activity: state one way a cheap cardboard model of the school bag could test one specification point before a real one is made.

#### R038-2.1 Types of specification criteria
Specification points can be classified two ways: needs versus wants (needs are essential requirements the product must meet; wants are desirable but not essential extras), and quantitative versus qualitative (quantitative criteria are measurable numbers, such as weight or dimensions; qualitative criteria are judged by opinion or description, such as "modern-looking"). A well-written specification is dominated by needs and includes a mix of quantitative and qualitative criteria, since some genuine requirements (like appearance) cannot always be reduced to a single number. Activity: classify four specification points for the school bag as need/want and quantitative/qualitative.
