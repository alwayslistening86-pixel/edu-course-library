# S04_CAD_and_Evaluating_Design_Ideas - Lesson: CAD drawing software and evaluating design ideas and outcomes

## Goal
The learner explains CAD's advantages and limitations against manual drawing, and evaluates design ideas and finished outcomes using named methods.

## Syllabus items taught here
- R038-3.3 - Using CAD drawing software: explain the advantages and limitations of CAD compared with manual drawing techniques
- R038-4.1 - Methods of evaluating design ideas: use models, qualitative comparison, ranking matrices and QFD to evaluate design ideas
- R038-4.2 - Modelling methods: compare virtual (3D CAD), card, block, breadboard and 3D-printed modelling methods
- R038-4.3 - Methods of evaluating the design outcome: evaluate a finished outcome by measuring dimensions and functionality, quantitative comparison, user testing and identifying modifications

## How to teach this
Ask the learner: 'Would you rather redesign a part by hand-redrawing it from scratch, or by changing one number in a computer model?' Use their answer to introduce CAD's core advantage, then balance it against real limitations, before moving into evaluation methods that decide which idea or outcome is actually best.

#### R038-3.3 Using CAD drawing software
CAD (computer-aided design) offers advantages over manual drawing: speed of editing (change one dimension and the whole drawing updates), accuracy, easy 3D visualisation, simple sharing/storage, and simulation (testing how a design behaves before manufacture). Limitations include the cost of software and hardware, the training needed to use it well, and that entirely computer-based ideas can sometimes lose the free, quick exploratory quality of hand sketching in early idea generation. A balanced answer gives genuine advantages and genuine limitations rather than treating CAD as simply better. Activity: give one advantage and one limitation of using CAD instead of manual drawing for the camping stove's final manufacturing drawings.

#### R038-4.1 Methods of evaluating design ideas
Ideas can be evaluated before manufacture using: producing quick models to test them physically, qualitative comparison (discussing strengths and weaknesses against the specification in words), ranking matrices (scoring each idea against specification criteria and totalling the scores to compare objectively), and Quality Function Deployment (QFD), a more detailed matrix relating customer requirements to design characteristics. A ranking matrix is especially useful for comparing several ideas fairly and defending the final choice with evidence rather than opinion alone. Activity: build a simple ranking matrix scoring two design ideas against three specification points each out of 5.

#### R038-4.2 Modelling methods
Modelling methods include virtual (3D CAD) modelling, card modelling (cheap, quick physical shape/scale testing), block modelling (solid material shaped to test form, e.g. foam), breadboarding (temporary electronic circuit testing without soldering) and 3D printing (producing an accurate physical prototype from a CAD file). Method choice depends on what is being tested (shape, function, electronics, final appearance), cost, and time available; a quick card model might answer a shape question that does not need a full 3D print. Activity: recommend a modelling method for testing the camping stove's overall shape early on, and a different one for testing its final look, with reasons for each.

#### R038-4.3 Methods of evaluating the design outcome
Once a model, prototype or product exists, the outcome is evaluated by measuring dimensions and functionality against the specification, quantitative comparison (does it meet the numeric criteria), user testing (getting real or representative users to try it and give feedback) and identifying modifications needed where the outcome falls short. This differs from evaluating ideas (S above, done before building) by working from evidence about something that actually exists. A specific, quantified evaluation ("the bag weighed 620g against a 500g target, so needs a lighter fabric") is stronger than a vague one ("it's not great"). Activity: write one specific, quantified evaluation sentence for a product you know, comparing it to a plausible specification point.
