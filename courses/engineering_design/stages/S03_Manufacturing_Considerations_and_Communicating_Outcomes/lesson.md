# S03_Manufacturing_Considerations_and_Communicating_Outcomes - Lesson: Manufacturing considerations, design influences and communicating design outcomes

## Goal
The learner explains how manufacturing considerations and wider influences shape design decisions, and names and produces working engineering drawings to convention.

## Syllabus items taught here
- R038-2.2 - Manufacturing considerations: explain how scale, materials, manufacturing processes and cost affect design decisions
- R038-2.3 - Influences on engineering product design: explain market pull, technology push, standards, legislation, obsolescence, the 6Rs and the circular economy
- R038-3.1 - Types of engineering drawing: name and describe freehand sketching, isometric, oblique, orthographic, exploded, assembly, block, flowchart and circuit/wiring drawings
- R038-3.2 - Working drawings: third-angle orthographic projection and BS 8888 conventions: produce and interpret orthographic working drawings using correct conventions (title block, units, scale, tolerance, dimensions, line types)

## How to teach this
Ask the learner why a product they own is made from a particular material rather than an obviously "better" one (for example a plastic kettle instead of glass). Use their answer to introduce that manufacturing considerations and wider influences constantly pull design decisions away from a purely ideal solution.

#### R038-2.2 Manufacturing considerations
Manufacturing considerations that shape a design include scale (one-off, batch or mass production, which affects what processes are economical), materials (availability, cost, properties needed), manufacturing processes grouped into wasting (removing material, e.g. cutting, drilling), shaping/forming (e.g. moulding, bending), joining (e.g. welding, fastening) and finishing (e.g. painting, coating), and overall cost. A design that ignores manufacturing considerations may be impossible or uneconomical to actually produce at the intended scale. Learners should link a specific consideration to a specific design decision, not list the categories generically. Activity: explain why a mass-produced plastic product would typically use moulding rather than hand-cutting each piece.

#### R038-2.3 Influences on engineering product design
Wider influences on design include market pull (designing in response to demonstrated customer demand) versus technology push (a new technology enabling a product nobody asked for yet), standards (agreed technical requirements products must meet), legislation (legal requirements, e.g. safety law), planned/perceived obsolescence, and sustainable design summarised by the 6Rs (reduce, reuse, recycle, repair, refuse, rethink) within a circular economy that keeps materials in use rather than discarded. Learners should be able to identify which influence is driving a decision in a given scenario and explain its effect. Activity: decide whether a new folding electric scooter is more market pull or technology push, and justify the choice.

#### R038-3.1 Types of engineering drawing
Types include freehand sketching (quick, unmeasured ideas), isometric and oblique drawing (3D-look drawings using set angles, useful for presenting ideas), orthographic drawing (separate, accurate 2D views: front, plan, side), exploded views (showing how parts fit together), assembly drawings (showing a finished assembled product with parts labelled), block diagrams (showing system parts and their connections simply) and flowcharts and circuit/wiring diagrams (showing a process or an electrical/electronic system). Each suits a different communication purpose: sketches for quick idea capture, orthographic for accurate manufacture, exploded/assembly for showing construction. Activity: state which drawing type would best communicate how ten parts of a toy fit together, and why.

#### R038-3.2 Working drawings: third-angle orthographic projection and BS 8888 conventions
Working drawings used for manufacture follow third-angle orthographic projection: separate, accurately scaled views (typically front, plan and side) arranged by a fixed convention so a manufacturer can read exact dimensions. British Standard BS 8888 sets the conventions used: a title block (name, scale, date, author), correct units, a stated scale, tolerances (the allowed variation in a dimension), consistent dimensioning, and standard line types (for example continuous lines for visible edges, dashed for hidden detail). A working drawing that omits these conventions cannot be manufactured from reliably. Activity: list the five BS 8888 elements named here that a correct working drawing must include.
