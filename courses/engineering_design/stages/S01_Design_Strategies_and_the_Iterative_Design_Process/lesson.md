# S01_Design_Strategies_and_the_Iterative_Design_Process - Lesson: Design strategies, the iterative design process and research methods

## Goal
The learner names and applies the design strategies, explains the design-make-evaluate cycle, uses primary and secondary research, and analyses a product using ACCESS FM.

## Syllabus items taught here
- R038-1.1 - Design strategies: know linear, iterative, inclusive, user-centred, sustainable and ergonomic design strategies and when each suits a scenario
- R038-1.2a - The iterative design process: design then make-and-evaluate: explain the cyclic design->make->evaluate approach and why iteration improves a product over a single linear attempt
- R038-1.2b - Research methods: primary and secondary: compare primary and secondary research methods and when each is used in the design stage
- R038-1.2c - Product analysis using ACCESS FM: analyse an existing product against each ACCESS FM factor

## How to teach this
Show the learner two products solving the same problem in very different ways (for example a reusable water bottle and a disposable one). Ask: 'Which one do you think was designed by testing lots of versions with real users, and which by one team working to a fixed brief?' Use the answer to introduce that engineers choose a design strategy deliberately, and that R038 is the exam unit underpinning everything practical in R039 and R040.

#### R038-1.1 Design strategies
Six design strategies are named in the specification: linear (a single planned sequence of stages, start to finish, with no return to earlier stages), iterative (a repeating cycle of design, make and evaluate, refining the product each time), inclusive (designing so the widest range of people can use the product), user-centred (designing around the needs and feedback of real users throughout), sustainable (minimising environmental impact across the product's life), and ergonomic (designing around how the human body and senses interact with the product). A given scenario usually suits one strategy best: a simple, well-understood product with a fixed deadline suits linear; a novel product benefiting from user testing suits iterative or user-centred. Learners must justify their chosen strategy against the specific scenario, not just define it. Activity: for a new kind of school pencil case, state which strategy fits best and give two reasons.

#### R038-1.2a The iterative design process: design then make-and-evaluate
The iterative process is a repeating two-stage cycle. The design stage covers analysing the brief, carrying out research, analysing existing products with ACCESS FM, writing a specification and generating ideas. The make-and-evaluate stage covers modelling and prototyping, and comparing the outcome against the specification's requirements, feeding findings back into a further design stage if needed. Each pass through the cycle should improve the product, which is why iterative design is normally described as producing a better-refined outcome than a single linear pass, at the cost of extra time. Activity: draw the cycle as a simple loop diagram and label what happens at each of its two stages.

#### R038-1.2b Research methods: primary and secondary
Primary research is data the designer collects directly - user interviews, surveys, observation, focus groups, testing existing products themselves. Secondary research uses data already collected by others - published reports, manufacturer specifications, reviews, existing standards. Primary research gives specific, current, first-hand evidence but takes longer and costs more; secondary research is quicker and often free but may be out of date or not exactly relevant to the brief. A well-researched design specification usually draws on both. Activity: for a "new school bike-storage system" brief, suggest one primary and one secondary research method and state what each would reveal.

#### R038-1.2c Product analysis using ACCESS FM
ACCESS FM is a structured way to analyse an existing product against eight factors: Aesthetics (how it looks/feels), Cost, Customer (who it is for), Environment (its environmental impact), Size, Safety, Function (what it does), and Materials. Analysing a product this way surfaces both good features to keep and weaknesses to improve on in a new design, and each factor should be assessed with specific evidence from the product, not a generic comment. This same technique reappears as R040 Task 1's set-assignment method, so fluency here directly supports that NEA task. Activity: analyse a school stapler against all eight ACCESS FM factors, one sentence each.
