# S16_Limited_Company_Accounts_Extended - Lesson: Limited company accounts: revaluation, share issues and reporting

## Goal
The learner accounts for the revaluation of non-current assets, distinguishes a rights issue from a bonus issue of shares, and explains the requirement to publish accounts, its stakeholder use, and the purpose of the international accounting standards framework.

## Syllabus items taught here
- 3.16a - Revaluation of non-current assets
- 3.16b - Rights issue of shares
- 3.16c - Bonus issue of shares
- 3.16d - Published accounts, stakeholders and the IASB framework

## How to teach this
Ask: a rights issue and a bonus issue both increase the number of shares in issue. Why does only one of them actually raise new cash for the company? Teach each topic with a worked numerical example wherever the content is calculation-based (ledger entries, ratios, variances, investment appraisal): have the learner attempt the calculation before seeing the worked answer. For evaluative content (S17-S21), always pair a calculated figure with the qualitative/contextual factors that should be weighed against it - AQA's Section C marking specifically rewards this. All numerical worked examples were computed with Python when the course was built.

#### 3.16a Revaluation of non-current assets
**Revaluation of non-current assets**: where an asset (typically property) is revalued upward from its carrying amount to a higher, more current value, the increase is credited to a **revaluation reserve** within equity (not to the statement of profit or loss, since it is an unrealised gain, applying prudence) and the asset's carrying amount in the statement of financial position is increased to the new valuation; future depreciation is then based on the revalued amount. *Worked example*: a property with a carrying amount of £200,000 is revalued to £260,000: revaluation surplus = 260,000 - 200,000 = £60,000, credited to a revaluation reserve; the property is now shown in the statement of financial position at £260,000.

#### 3.16b Rights issue of shares
**Rights issue**: existing shareholders are offered new shares, in proportion to their current holding, usually below the current market price but at or above nominal value - it **raises new cash** for the company. *Worked example*: a company with 100,000 £1 ordinary shares in issue makes a 1-for-4 rights issue at £1.20: new shares = 100,000/4 = 25,000; cash raised = 25,000 x £1.20 = £30,000; share capital increases by 25,000 x £1 = £25,000 (nominal value); share premium increases by 25,000 x (1.20-1) = £5,000.

#### 3.16c Bonus issue of shares
**Bonus (scrip) issue**: new shares are given to existing shareholders free, in proportion to their holding, funded by **capitalising reserves** (transferring an amount from reserves, e.g. share premium or retained earnings, to share capital) - it **raises no new cash**; total equity is unchanged, only its split between share capital and reserves. *Worked example*: a company with 125,000 £1 ordinary shares makes a 1-for-5 bonus issue: new shares = 125,000/5 = 25,000; share capital increases by 25,000 x £1 = £25,000 (with the same amount transferred out of reserves, e.g. retained earnings, and no cash changing hands).

#### 3.16d Published accounts, stakeholders and the IASB framework
**Published accounts, stakeholders, and the international accounting standards framework**: companies (especially larger and listed ones) are legally required to prepare and file accounts to a prescribed format, audited where required, so that outside stakeholders - shareholders (assessing their investment and any dividend), lenders (assessing creditworthiness), suppliers (deciding whether to offer credit), employees (job security), HMRC (tax), and the wider public - can rely on comparable, trustworthy information they could not otherwise obtain. The **International Accounting Standards Board (IASB)** issues **IFRS Accounting Standards**, which UK-listed companies must follow for their published (consolidated) accounts; a common framework of standards makes financial statements comparable **between** companies and **across borders**, supporting confident investment and lending decisions that would be much harder if every company used entirely different accounting policies.
