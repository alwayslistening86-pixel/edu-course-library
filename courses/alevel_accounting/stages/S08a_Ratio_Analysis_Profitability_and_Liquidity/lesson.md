# S08a_Ratio_Analysis_Profitability_and_Liquidity - Lesson: Ratio analysis I: profitability and liquidity

## Goal
The learner calculates and interprets profitability ratios (gross profit margin, operating profit margin, ROCE) and liquidity ratios (current ratio, quick ratio), and distinguishes cash from profit.

## Syllabus items taught here
- QS1 - Ratios and fractions
- 3.8a - Profitability ratios and ROCE
- QS2 - Percentages and percentage changes
- 3.8b - Liquidity ratios; cash versus profit

## How to teach this
Ask: a business reports a healthy profit for the year but its bank account is overdrawn and getting worse. How can both be true at once? Teach each topic with a worked numerical example wherever the content is calculation-based (ledger entries, ratios, variances, investment appraisal): have the learner attempt the calculation before seeing the worked answer. For evaluative content (S17-S21), always pair a calculated figure with the qualitative/contextual factors that should be weighed against it - AQA's Section C marking specifically rewards this. All numerical worked examples were computed with Python when the course was built.

#### QS1 Ratios and fractions
This course numbers AQA's unnumbered quantitative-skills annex QS1-QS9 in the annex's own order; QS1 is: **calculate, use and understand ratios and fractions** - the basic arithmetic skill (a/b, expressed as a ratio "a:b" or a fraction/percentage) that every ratio in 3.8 depends on.

#### 3.8a Profitability ratios and ROCE
**Profitability ratios**: **gross profit margin** = gross profit / revenue x 100 (how much of every £1 of sales is retained after the direct cost of the goods sold - reflects pricing policy and purchasing costs); **operating profit margin** = profit from operations / revenue x 100 (retained after all operating expenses too - reflects overhead control); **return on capital employed (ROCE)** = profit from operations / capital employed (total equity + non-current liabilities) x 100 (how efficiently the capital invested in the business generates profit - comparable to the return available elsewhere, e.g. a savings account or another business). *Worked example*: revenue £200,000, gross profit £90,000, profit from operations £54,000, capital employed £360,000: gross margin = 45.0%, operating margin = 27.0%, ROCE = 15.0%.

#### QS2 Percentages and percentage changes
QS2: **calculate, use and understand percentages and percentage changes** - every ratio above is conventionally expressed as a percentage, and comparing this year's ratio with last year's requires calculating the percentage-point change (e.g. gross margin moving from 42% to 45% is a rise of 3 percentage points, not "3%").

#### 3.8b Liquidity ratios; cash versus profit
**Liquidity ratios and cash versus profit**: **current ratio** = current assets / current liabilities (can short-term assets cover short-term debts? A ratio well below 1:1 signals a risk of being unable to pay on time; a very high ratio can suggest cash tied up unproductively in inventory or slow-paying customers). **Quick (acid test) ratio** = (current assets - inventory) / current liabilities (a stricter test, excluding inventory, the current asset least easily and quickly turned into cash). *Worked example*: current assets £50,000 (including inventory £18,000), current liabilities £40,000: current ratio = 1.25:1; quick ratio = (32,000)/40,000 = 0.80:1. **Cash is not the same as profit**: profit is an accounting measure of income earned less expenses incurred (accruals basis) over a period, while cash is the actual money received and paid. A profitable business can still run out of cash - e.g. profit tied up in growing inventory or receivables, cash spent on capital expenditure or loan repayments (neither of which is an expense in the statement of profit or loss), or a period of rapid, cash-hungry growth.
