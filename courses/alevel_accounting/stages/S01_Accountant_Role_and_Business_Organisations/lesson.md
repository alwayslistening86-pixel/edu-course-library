# S01_Accountant_Role_and_Business_Organisations - Lesson: The accountant's role and types of business organisation

## Goal
The learner explains the accountant's role, distinguishes financial from management accounting, and compares sole trader, partnership, private and public limited company organisation, including their sources of finance.

## Syllabus items taught here
- 3.1a - Role of the accountant in business
- 3.1b - Financial accounting versus management accounting
- 3.2a - Sole trader organisation and unlimited liability
- 3.2b - Partnership organisation and the Partnership Act 1890
- 3.2c - Private/public limited companies; sources of finance compared

## How to teach this
Ask: a sole trader and a public limited company both need accounts kept - so why does a plc face far more onerous reporting rules than a market-stall sole trader? Teach each topic with a worked numerical example wherever the content is calculation-based (ledger entries, ratios, variances, investment appraisal): have the learner attempt the calculation before seeing the worked answer. For evaluative content (S17-S21), always pair a calculated figure with the qualitative/contextual factors that should be weighed against it - AQA's Section C marking specifically rewards this. All numerical worked examples were computed with Python when the course was built.

#### 3.1a Role of the accountant in business
**The role of the accountant in business**: the accountant designs and oversees the accounting information system that captures every transaction, supervises bookkeepers and ledger clerks who do the day-to-day recording, and turns raw data into information that is accurate, timely and relevant for decision-making - both for people inside the business (management) and people outside it (owners, lenders, HMRC, suppliers).

#### 3.1b Financial accounting versus management accounting
**Financial accounting versus management accounting**: **financial accounting** produces the statutory financial statements (statement of profit or loss, statement of financial position) for external users, following accounting concepts and (for companies) the law and accounting standards, covering the business as a whole for a past period. **Management accounting** produces information for internal use only (budgets, costing, variance analysis) with no legal format requirement, often looking forward and often broken down by department, product or activity, to help managers plan, control and decide.

#### 3.2a Sole trader organisation and unlimited liability
**Sole trader**: one owner who provides all the capital, keeps all the profit, and has **unlimited liability** (personal assets are at risk if the business cannot pay its debts). Simple to set up, few legal formalities, no requirement to publish accounts, but capital is limited to what the owner can raise and the business ends if the owner does.

#### 3.2b Partnership organisation and the Partnership Act 1890
**Partnership**: two or more owners (partners) who share capital, profits and, unless a limited liability partnership, **unlimited liability** jointly and severally for the firm's debts. Governed by a partnership agreement (or, in its absence, the Partnership Act 1890's default rules: profits shared equally, no partner salaries, no interest on capital). More capital and expertise than a sole trader, but shared control and disputes are possible.

#### 3.2c Private/public limited companies; sources of finance compared
**Limited companies**: a **private limited company (Ltd)** and a **public limited company (plc)** are each a separate legal person from their owners (shareholders), who have **limited liability** - they can lose only what they invested. A plc, but not a Ltd, may offer its shares to the public and list on a stock exchange, raising much larger amounts of capital, but faces heavier disclosure and governance requirements. **Sources of finance** compared: owner's/partners' capital and retained profit (no interest, but ties up personal/partner wealth); a bank overdraft (flexible, short-term, but repayable on demand and interest is charged on the fluctuating balance); a bank loan or mortgage (fixed repayment schedule and interest, may require security over an asset); ordinary shares (permanent capital, no repayment obligation, but dividends dilute control and are paid after tax, unlike loan interest which is tax-deductible); debentures (fixed-interest loan capital, often secured on company assets, repayable at a set date - cheaper than shares if the business can service the interest, but committed interest must be paid even in a loss-making year). *Worked example*: a company borrows £50,000 as a 6% debenture. Annual interest = £3,000, payable regardless of profit, before any dividend is considered.
