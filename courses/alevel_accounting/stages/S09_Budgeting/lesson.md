# S09_Budgeting - Lesson: Budgeting

## Goal
The learner explains the purpose of budgeting, prepares a cash budget, compares incremental and zero-based approaches, and calculates and interprets budget variances.

## Syllabus items taught here
- 3.9a - Purpose of budgeting
- 3.9b - Incremental vs zero-based budgeting; cash budgets
- 3.9c - Budget variances (favourable/adverse)

## How to teach this
Ask: why might a profitable, growing business still need a cash budget, when its statement of profit or loss already shows it is making money? Teach each topic with a worked numerical example wherever the content is calculation-based (ledger entries, ratios, variances, investment appraisal): have the learner attempt the calculation before seeing the worked answer. For evaluative content (S17-S21), always pair a calculated figure with the qualitative/contextual factors that should be weighed against it - AQA's Section C marking specifically rewards this. All numerical worked examples were computed with Python when the course was built.

#### 3.9a Purpose of budgeting
**Purpose of budgeting**: a budget is a financial plan for a future period, used for **planning** (deciding what resources are needed and how activity will be funded), **control** (comparing actual results with the budget to spot and act on deviations early), **coordination** (aligning different departments' plans, e.g. production must match the sales budget), **motivation** (a realistic, agreed budget can motivate staff/managers who helped set it) and **responsibility** (holding managers accountable for the areas they control). A cash budget specifically forecasts cash receipts and payments month by month, because a business can be profitable on paper (accruals basis) yet still run out of cash if receipts lag behind payments.

#### 3.9b Incremental vs zero-based budgeting; cash budgets
**Incremental versus zero-based budgeting**: **incremental budgeting** starts from the previous period's budget/actual figures and adjusts them (e.g. +3% for inflation) - quick and simple, but it can build in past inefficiencies indefinitely and gives no incentive to question whether spending is still needed. **Zero-based budgeting** starts from a "zero base" each period - every item of spending must be justified afresh, regardless of what was spent before - closely scrutinises costs and can reveal wasteful spending, but is far more time-consuming and resource-intensive to prepare than simply adjusting last year's figures. *Worked example, cash budget*: opening cash balance £5,000; forecast receipts £42,000; forecast payments £38,000: closing balance = 5,000 + 42,000 - 38,000 = £9,000, which becomes next month's opening balance.

#### 3.9c Budget variances (favourable/adverse)
**Budget variances**: comparing actual results with the budget produces a variance for each line - **favourable (F)** if it improves profit/cash (e.g. actual costs below budget, actual sales above budget) or **adverse (A)** if it worsens it (e.g. actual costs above budget). *Worked example*: budgeted sales revenue £60,000, actual sales revenue £55,000: variance = 55,000 - 60,000 = £-5,000, i.e. £5,000 **adverse** (actual revenue fell short of budget). Budgeted materials cost £18,000, actual materials cost £16,500: variance = £1,500 **favourable** (actual cost was lower than budgeted). A large variance, in either direction, should prompt investigation into its cause before being acted on.
