# S08b_Ratio_Analysis_Efficiency_and_Limitations - Lesson: Ratio analysis II: efficiency and limitations

## Goal
The learner calculates and interprets efficiency ratios (receivables/payables collection periods, inventory holding period) and evaluates the limitations of financial statement and ratio analysis.

## Syllabus items taught here
- 3.8c - Efficiency ratios
- 3.8d - Limitations of financial statement and ratio analysis

## How to teach this
Ask: two businesses have identical profit margins, but one collects cash from customers twice as fast as the other. Why might that matter more than the margin itself? Teach each topic with a worked numerical example wherever the content is calculation-based (ledger entries, ratios, variances, investment appraisal): have the learner attempt the calculation before seeing the worked answer. For evaluative content (S17-S21), always pair a calculated figure with the qualitative/contextual factors that should be weighed against it - AQA's Section C marking specifically rewards this. All numerical worked examples were computed with Python when the course was built.

#### 3.8c Efficiency ratios
**Efficiency ratios**: **receivables (trade receivables) collection period** = trade receivables / revenue x 365 (average days taken to collect cash from credit customers - a rising figure may signal weaker credit control or customers in difficulty); **payables (trade payables) payment period** = trade payables / cost of sales (or credit purchases) x 365 (average days taken to pay suppliers - useful for cash flow, but too high may damage supplier relationships or signal an inability to pay); **inventory holding period** = inventory / cost of sales x 365 (average days inventory is held before sale - a rising figure may signal overstocking or slow-moving/obsolete stock). *Worked example*: trade receivables £24,000, revenue £292,000: collection period = 24,000/292,000 x 365 = 30 days.

#### 3.8d Limitations of financial statement and ratio analysis
**Limitations of financial statement and ratio analysis**: ratios need a benchmark to be meaningful (the prior year, a budget, or a competitor) - a ratio in isolation says little; different businesses may use different accounting policies (e.g. depreciation method, inventory valuation), distorting comparability; the statements are historical, often as at a single year-end date, which may not represent the year as a whole (e.g. a seasonal business); qualitative factors (staff morale, brand strength, management quality, the wider economy) are not captured by ratios at all; and a ratio flags that something has changed without explaining why, so it is a starting point for further investigation, not a conclusion on its own.
