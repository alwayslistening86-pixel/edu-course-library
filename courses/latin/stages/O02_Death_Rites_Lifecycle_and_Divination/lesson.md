# O02_Death_Rites_Lifecycle_and_Divination - Lesson: Parentalia and Lemuria, life-cycle rituals, sacrifice, death and afterlife beliefs, and divination

## Goal
The learner describes the festivals of the dead, the religious rites marking a Roman's life stages, sacrificial procedure, beliefs about death, and the practice of divination.

## Syllabus items taught here
- LAT-C3B-T8-parentalia-lemuria - Parentalia and Lemuria: describe the Parentalia (13-21 February, honouring dead family members with tomb offerings) and the Lemuria (9/11/13 May, driving away evil spirits)
- LAT-C3B-T8-life-cycle - Life-cycle rituals: describe religious rites at birth/naming, the bulla and lunula, coming of age, and marriage
- LAT-C3B-T8-sacrifice - Sacrifice and offerings: describe animal sacrifice procedure, the role of the victimarii, tomb offerings and the mola salsa
- LAT-C3B-T8-death-afterlife - Death and afterlife beliefs: describe Roman burial practices (cremation/inhumation, columbaria), Twelve Tables restrictions, and beliefs about the afterlife
- LAT-C3B-T8-divination - Divination and the religious calendar: explain augury, haruspicy, and the distinction between fasti and nefasti days

## How to teach this
Ask: "Rome had two separate festivals for the dead within a few months of each other, with opposite moods — one calm, one frightened. Why?" Use this to introduce Parentalia (calm, respectful) against Lemuria (fearful, apotropaic), a contrast worth returning to throughout this stage.

#### LAT-C3B-T8-parentalia-lemuria Parentalia and Lemuria
The Parentalia (13-21 February, with the main public rites on the last day, called the Feralia) was a calm, affectionate festival honouring dead family members: families visited ancestral tombs bringing modest offerings of wine, milk, honey, and garlands, and public temples closed for its duration as a mark of respect. The Lemuria (held on the non-consecutive nights of 9, 11 and 13 May) was, by contrast, an anxious, protective rite performed by the head of a household at midnight to drive away lemures, restless or malevolent spirits of the unquiet dead: he walked barefoot through the house throwing black beans over his shoulder while repeatedly saying a fixed formula, then made a loud noise (clashing bronze) to finally banish the spirits — a good example of a private household ritual distinct from the large public festivals studied elsewhere in this topic.

#### LAT-C3B-T8-life-cycle Life-cycle rituals
Roman religion marked every major life stage with ritual. A newborn was formally accepted into the family at the lustration (dies lustricus), on the eighth day for a girl or the ninth for a boy, when the child was named and given protective amulets: a bulla (a round pendant, of gold for wealthier families) for a boy, worn until he came of age, and a lunula (a crescent-shaped amulet) for a girl. Coming of age for a boy involved formally exchanging the purple-bordered toga praetexta of childhood for the plain white toga virilis, usually around age fourteen to sixteen, often timed to the Liberalia festival (17 March) and marked by dedicating his bulla to the household gods. Marriage involved its own religious ceremony, including sacrifice, the bride's ritual transfer from her father's household gods to her husband's, and set formulae and omens consulted beforehand to secure a favourable outcome.

#### LAT-C3B-T8-sacrifice Sacrifice and offerings
Animal sacrifice was Roman religion's central public act: an animal appropriate to the god and occasion (a pig, sheep or ox — the triple sacrifice of all three together, the suovetaurilia, marked especially solemn state occasions) was led to the altar, sprinkled with mola salsa (a mixture of salted, roasted spelt flour prepared under strict ritual conditions by the Vestal Virgins), and killed by a specialist attendant, the victimarius, under the supervision of the presiding priest, who first checked the animal's entrails (the exta, especially the liver) for signs that the sacrifice had been favourably received. Parts of the animal (particularly certain internal organs) were burned on the altar for the god, while the rest was typically cooked and shared among the human participants — sacrifice therefore functioned simultaneously as an act of worship and as a communal, and often the only regular, meat meal for many Romans. Offerings at tombs (wine, food, garlands, as in the Parentalia) followed a related but non-sacrificial logic of maintaining a relationship with the dead.

#### LAT-C3B-T8-death-afterlife Death and afterlife beliefs
Both cremation and inhumation (burial of the intact body) were practised by the Romans at different periods, with cremation more common earlier under the Republic and inhumation becoming increasingly common from the second century AD onward; ashes after cremation were commonly stored in a columbarium, a communal structure with many small niches for urns, especially used by less wealthy Romans and freed slaves' collective burial clubs. The Twelve Tables (Rome's earliest law code, traditionally dated to the mid-fifth century BC) explicitly restricted burial and cremation within the city boundary itself, for practical and religious-pollution reasons, which is why tombs and grave monuments line the roads leading out of Rome, such as the Appian Way. Roman beliefs about the afterlife itself were notably vague and varied rather than fixed doctrine: ideas ranged from a shadowy underworld existence (echoing Greek notions of the underworld) to the dead being cared for at the tomb as a kind of ongoing family presence (the di manes, "spirits of the departed," regularly addressed on tombstones) to outright philosophical scepticism about any afterlife at all.

#### LAT-C3B-T8-divination Divination and the religious calendar
Because Roman religion prized knowing whether the gods approved a course of action in advance, formal divination was built into public life: augury (interpreting the flight, calls and feeding behaviour of birds, performed by trained augurs before major state decisions, including whether to hold an assembly at all) and haruspicy (examining the liver and entrails of a sacrificed animal for abnormalities, a skill originally imported from Etruscan haruspices) were the two main official methods. The religious calendar itself divided every day into fasti (days on which public legal and political business could lawfully be conducted) and nefasti (days reserved for religious observance on which such business was forbidden) — a distinction recorded in the very calendars that survive as one of our key primary sources for the festival dates taught across this topic.
