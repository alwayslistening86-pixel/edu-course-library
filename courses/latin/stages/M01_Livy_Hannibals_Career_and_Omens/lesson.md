## Goal
The learner explains the historical background to the Second Punic War and recounts Livy's account of Hannibal's boyhood oath, his character and the dream that precedes the march into Italy.

## Syllabus items taught here
- LAT-C3A-LIVY-CONTEXT - Historical context: the Second Punic War: explain why Hannibal took the Alpine route, referencing Hamilcar Barca's expansion into Spain and the Rome-Carthage rivalry
- LAT-C3A-LIVY-A - Passage A: Hannibal's early career and oath: recount Livy's account of Hannibal's boyhood oath against Rome and the shaping of his early military career
- LAT-C3A-LIVY-B - Passage B: character sketch of Hannibal: analyse Livy's description of Hannibal's leadership qualities, physical endurance and personal vices
- LAT-C3A-LIVY-C - Passage C: the prophetic dream: recount the dream episode in which a supernatural guide shows Hannibal the way to Italy, and its narrative purpose
- LAT-C3A-LIVY-D - Passage D: crossing the Rhone with the elephants: describe the logistics of crossing the Rhone, including how the elephants were transported

## How to teach this
Start with the question: "Why would a Roman historian, writing over a century after Hannibal's death, want to make him seem larger than life?" Livy is writing for a Roman audience proud of having eventually defeated Carthage's greatest general — so an impressive, almost supernatural Hannibal makes Rome's later victory more glorious. Keep returning to this idea of Livy as a dramatist shaping history, not a neutral reporter, across every passage in this text.

#### LAT-C3A-LIVY-CONTEXT Historical context: the Second Punic War
Carthage lost the First Punic War (264-241 BC) to Rome, surrendering Sicily and paying a huge indemnity. Hamilcar Barca, Hannibal's father and a Carthaginian general who had not himself been defeated in the field, rebuilt Carthaginian power by conquering and exploiting the silver-rich territory of Spain from 237 BC, partly to fund future revenge and partly to compensate for the lost Sicilian revenue. When Hamilcar died, command passed to his son-in-law Hasdrubal, and then, on Hasdrubal's assassination in 221 BC, to the twenty-six-year-old Hannibal. Hannibal's attack on Saguntum, a Spanish town allied to Rome, in 219 BC broke the treaty line dividing Roman and Carthaginian spheres and triggered Rome's declaration of war — the Second Punic War (218-201 BC), the conflict Livy's Book 21 opens.

#### LAT-C3A-LIVY-A Passage A: Hannibal's early career and oath
Livy opens Book 21 by tracing Hannibal's hatred of Rome to childhood. As Hamilcar prepared to leave for Spain, the nine-year-old Hannibal begged to go with him; Hamilcar led the boy to an altar and made him swear, with his hand on the sacrificial offerings, that he would never be a friend to Rome — an oath (later echoed by Hannibal himself, according to the historian Polybius, as sworn against Rome specifically, though Livy's own phrasing emphasises the father's initiative). Livy uses this scene to explain Hannibal's later career as the fulfilment of a vow rooted in family and religion, not mere ambition, and to foreshadow the entire war that follows.

#### LAT-C3A-LIVY-B Passage B: character sketch of Hannibal
Livy's set-piece character sketch (a "character portrait" typical of ancient historiography) lists Hannibal's soldierly virtues in almost superhuman terms: he could endure extremes of heat and cold, needed little food or sleep, and was first into battle and last to leave the field. But Livy balances this admiration with a list of vices — inhuman cruelty, treachery worse than Carthaginian standards even by Roman stereotype, no regard for truth, the sacred, an oath, or religious scruple (Livy's "nihil veri, nihil sancti" catalogue, rendered in English here rather than quoted). Teach this passage as a deliberately double-edged portrait: an admirable general and, in Roman eyes, a dangerous, faithless enemy — both readings are testable.

#### LAT-C3A-LIVY-C Passage C: the prophetic dream
Before setting out from Spain, Hannibal dreams that a young man of divine appearance tells him he has been sent by Jupiter to guide him into Italy, and orders him to follow without looking back. Overcome by curiosity, Hannibal looks back anyway and sees a huge, monstrous serpent moving behind him, crushing trees and bushes, followed by a storm cloud with thunder; when he asks the guide what the portent means, he is told it is the devastation of Italy, and that he should press on and ask no more. Teach this as Livy's device for foreshadowing the destructive but ultimately indecisive nature of Hannibal's invasion — dramatic, ominous, and never literally verified, keeping the reader in suspense.

#### LAT-C3A-LIVY-D Passage D: crossing the Rhone with the elephants
Reaching the river Rhone in southern Gaul, Hannibal's army faces hostile Gauls on the far bank and the logistical problem of ferrying the whole army — including war elephants — across a wide, fast-flowing river. The soldiers cross first on boats and rafts, then draw the Gauls off from the crossing point with a diversionary force sent upstream to cross and attack from the rear. For the elephants, Livy describes rafts covered with earth and turf so the animals were tricked into thinking they were still walking on solid ground before the raft was cut loose and floated or towed across; some panicked and swam, using their raised trunks to breathe.
