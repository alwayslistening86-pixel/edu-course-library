# N02_Virgil_Combat_Cult_and_Future_Rome - Lesson: Virgil — Hercules exposes the cave, the combat, the cult of Hercules, the Salii's hymn and future Rome

## Goal
The learner recounts Hercules' destruction of the cave roof, the fight and death of Cacus, the founding of Hercules' cult and hymn, and Evander's tour of Pallanteum, and identifies Virgil's verse techniques.

## Syllabus items taught here
- LAT-C3A-VIRG-E - Passage E: Hercules exposes the cave: describe Hercules' fury and his tearing away of the cave's rocky roof, noting Virgil's hyperbolic imagery
- LAT-C3A-VIRG-F - Passage F: combat and the death of Cacus: recount the fight in which Hercules corners and kills Cacus
- LAT-C3A-VIRG-G - Passage G: establishing worship of Hercules: explain how Evander institutes an altar and rites honouring Hercules after the victory
- LAT-C3A-VIRG-H - Passage H: the Salii's hymn: describe the priests' hymn recounting Hercules' Labours and other exploits
- LAT-C3A-VIRG-I - Passage I: touring future Rome: describe Evander's tour of Pallanteum and its mythological links to Saturn's Golden Age
- LAT-C3A-VIRG-STYLE - Virgil's verse techniques: identify and explain the effect of emphatic word order, sound effects (onomatopoeia, alliteration) and Homeric epithets in the passage

## How to teach this
Ask: "How do you make a fight between a hero and a monster feel bigger than an ordinary brawl?" Draw out ideas (exaggeration, violent imagery, noise, scale) before showing how Virgil actually does it — by having Hercules physically tear a mountain apart before the fight even starts.

#### LAT-C3A-VIRG-E Passage E: Hercules exposes the cave
Enraged at the theft and the lowing cow's betrayal, Hercules attacks the hillside itself rather than searching for a door: seizing the rocky ridge that forms the cave's roof with his bare hands, he wrenches it loose and hurls it aside, tearing open the cavern to daylight in one act of superhuman strength. Virgil describes the scene with deliberate hyperbole — the cave's exposure compared to the sky being torn open, the ground shaking, Cacus's hidden lair suddenly visible "from its deepest caverns" as if the underworld itself had been thrown open to view — establishing Hercules' power as something closer to a natural catastrophe than an ordinary human effort, before the physical fight has even begun.

#### LAT-C3A-VIRG-F Passage F: combat and the death of Cacus
Trapped and exposed, Cacus resorts to his one remaining weapon, vomiting smoke and fire from his mouth to fill the cave with choking darkness and cover his position, but Hercules, undeterred, leaps down through the flames to grapple with him directly. The fight ends not with a weapon but with Hercules' bare hands: he seizes Cacus by the throat and throttles him until his eyes bulge from his head and his throat runs dry of blood, a graphically physical death that closes the monster's reign of terror. Teach this as the climax the whole episode has been building towards since passage C, and note that Hercules wins through raw strength rather than cunning, contrasting with Cacus's earlier trickery over the cattle.

#### LAT-C3A-VIRG-G Passage G: establishing worship of Hercules
Once Cacus is dead, Evander tells how the local people, grateful for their deliverance, institute lasting worship of Hercules: an altar (which Evander names as the very one, the Ara Maxima, at which the present feast Aeneas is attending takes place) is founded and named "Greatest," and the cult is established with fixed rites that, Evander stresses, have been kept up ever since, unbroken, down to Evander's own day. This is the episode's core aetiological move: Virgil is explaining, for his contemporary Roman audience, the real origin of a real, still-functioning religious cult and altar in the Forum Boarium in Rome.

#### LAT-C3A-VIRG-H Passage H: the Salii's hymn
As part of the rite, the priests (Virgil names them the Salii, more usually associated with the war-god Mars in actual Roman practice, a detail worth flagging as Virgil's own poetic construction rather than strict historical accuracy) sing a hymn recounting Hercules' great deeds: his precocious infant strength in strangling two serpents sent by the jealous goddess Juno while still in his cradle, his destruction of the Greek cities of Troy and Oechalia, and his many Labours performed at the command of King Eurystheus, undertaken because of "the harsh commands of unjust Juno." The hymn functions as a compressed catalogue of Hercules' heroic biography, reinforcing his status as a demigod worthy of cult worship.

#### LAT-C3A-VIRG-I Passage I: touring future Rome
After the rites conclude, Evander leads Aeneas on a walking tour of Pallanteum, pointing out sites that Virgil's readers would recognise as later Roman landmarks-in-waiting: the future Capitoline hill (then wild and overgrown, but already regarded as sacred and the haunt of a god), the Tarpeian Rock, and the Carmental Gate. Evander also tells of the god Saturn, who fled here from Jupiter and gathered the scattered local people into ordered society, giving his name to the "Golden Age," a time of justice and peace before it decayed into war and greed. The tour lets Virgil overlay legendary prehistory directly onto Rome's actual topography, so that every stone Aeneas sees is already, secretly, Rome.

#### LAT-C3A-VIRG-STYLE Virgil's verse techniques
Examinable stylistic features include emphatic word order (Virgil places a key word first or last in the line, or separates an adjective from its noun across several words, to give it weight — e.g. holding back the verb of violent action to the very end of a sentence for dramatic effect), sound effects such as onomatopoeia (word-sounds that imitate what they describe, e.g. heavy spondaic rhythms slowing a line to suggest effort or weight) and alliteration (repeated initial consonants binding words together for emphasis or to mimic a physical action, such as crashing or hissing sounds), and Homeric epithets (fixed, formulaic descriptive phrases inherited from the Greek epic tradition, such as "pater Aeneas," "father Aeneas," reused to mark a character's essential nature every time they are named). Teach learners to name the specific technique, quote a few words as evidence, and explain the precise effect, since GCSE mark schemes reward this three-part structure over unsupported description.
