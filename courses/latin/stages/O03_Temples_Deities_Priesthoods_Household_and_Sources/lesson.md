# O03_Temples_Deities_Priesthoods_Household_and_Sources - Lesson: Temples, deities, priesthoods, household religion, and working with ancient sources

## Goal
The learner identifies major Roman temples and deities and their functions, distinguishes the main priesthoods, describes household worship, and evaluates what different kinds of ancient evidence can and cannot show about Roman religious practice.

## Syllabus items taught here
- LAT-C3B-T8-temples - Temples and religious buildings: identify major Roman temples (Saturn, Vesta, Hercules Victor, Portunus, the Pantheon, the Ara Pacis) and their religious function
- LAT-C3B-T8-deities - Deities and divine figures: identify the deities associated with festivals and household worship (Saturn, Vesta, Bona Dea, Lares, Penates and others) and their spheres
- LAT-C3B-T8-priesthoods - Priests and religious officials: distinguish the roles of the pontifices, flamines, augures, haruspices, Vestal Virgins and victimarii
- LAT-C3B-T8-household-religion - Private religion and household worship: describe the lararium, daily prayers to Lares and Penates, and worship at the domestic hearth
- LAT-C3B-T8-sources - Working with ancient sources on Roman religion: evaluate literary sources (e.g. Ovid's Fasti), inscriptions and archaeological evidence for what they can and cannot tell us about practice

## How to teach this
Close the topic by asking: "If all you had was a single surviving inscription, or a single poem, could you fully reconstruct what Roman religion was actually like?" Use the answer (no single source type is sufficient alone) to frame this final stage's emphasis on combining evidence types critically.

#### LAT-C3B-T8-temples Temples and religious buildings
Major Roman temples examinable in this topic include the Temple of Saturn (at the foot of the Capitoline in the Forum, which also housed the Roman state treasury, the aerarium, linking Saturn's festival to public finance), the round Temple of Vesta (housing the sacred hearth-fire, distinctively circular rather than the standard rectangular Roman temple plan, echoing an early round hut), the Ara Maxima or "Greatest Altar" of Hercules Victor/Invictus in the Forum Boarium (the altar whose mythical founding is narrated in the Virgil set text studied earlier in this course), the small round Temple of Portunus (god of harbours and river-crossings, on the Tiber bank), and the Pantheon (rebuilt under Hadrian in the second century AD as a temple to "all the gods," famous for its coffered concrete dome) — plus the Ara Pacis ("Altar of Peace"), an Augustan-period sculpted altar rather than a roofed temple, commemorating peace after Augustus's campaigns and richly decorated with religious and family imagery useful as visual source evidence.

#### LAT-C3B-T8-deities Deities and divine figures
Beyond the major Greek-derived Olympian gods, this topic requires the deities tied specifically to the festivals and household worship already studied: Saturn (agriculture, the mythical Golden Age, honoured at the Saturnalia), Vesta (the hearth, both domestic and civic, tended by the Vestal Virgins), Bona Dea (a goddess of women, fertility and healing whose true identity and rites were deliberately kept partly secret even in antiquity), and the household spirits proper to Roman private religion: the Lares (guardian spirits of the household and its land, sometimes identified with ancestral or crossroads spirits) and the Penates (guardians specifically of the household's stores and larder). Each deity's "sphere" (area of responsibility) explains which festival or domestic practice belongs to them, so learners should always be able to link a named deity to a specific rite already studied.

#### LAT-C3B-T8-priesthoods Priests and religious officials
Roman priesthoods were distinct, specialised offices, not a single unified clergy. The pontifices (headed by the pontifex maximus, a title later absorbed by the emperor) oversaw religious law and the calendar generally, advising on correct procedure across the whole state cult. The flamines were individual priests each dedicated permanently to one specific god (the three senior "major" flamines served Jupiter, Mars and Quirinus), bound by unique personal taboos particular to their god. Augures performed augury (bird-divination, covered in the previous stage); haruspices, originally Etruscan specialists, read sacrificial entrails. The Vestal Virgins, six in number, were uniquely both priestesses and a lifelong (thirty-year minimum) religious institution, chosen as young girls, required to remain celibate on pain of death, and responsible for Vesta's fire and the mola salsa. Victimarii were the specialist attendants (not priests in the strict sense) who physically restrained and killed the sacrificial animal under a priest's direction.

#### LAT-C3B-T8-household-religion Private religion and household worship
Alongside the great public festivals, every Roman household maintained its own daily religious routine, centred on the lararium, a small shrine (often a decorated niche or miniature temple-shaped cupboard) usually near the hearth or entrance, holding statuettes of the Lares and Penates. The head of the household (paterfamilias) led short daily prayers and small offerings — a pinch of food from the table, a little wine — at the lararium, particularly at mealtimes and on set family occasions such as birthdays, and the household hearth itself functioned as a small-scale version of Vesta's public flame, needing to be kept properly tended. This constant, unofficial, largely undocumented household practice is one reason archaeological evidence (surviving lararia at sites like Pompeii) is especially valuable: it shows a layer of everyday religious life that literary sources, mostly interested in public state religion, rarely describe in detail.

#### LAT-C3B-T8-sources Working with ancient sources on Roman religion
Different evidence types have different strengths and limits, and a good Component 3B answer weighs them explicitly rather than treating any one as simply "true." Literary sources such as Ovid's Fasti (a poem working systematically through the Roman religious calendar month by month) give vivid narrative detail and explanation of festival origins, but are literary artefacts shaped for a reading audience, sometimes centuries after a custom began, and may embellish, moralise or genuinely misunderstand older practice. Inscriptions (dedications, calendars carved in stone, tombstones) offer directly contemporary, unmediated evidence of names, dates and formulae, but are typically brief, formulaic and give little explanation of what a rite meant to participants. Archaeological evidence (temple remains, altars, lararia, grave goods) shows the physical reality of where and how worship happened, but cannot by itself reveal the words spoken or the beliefs held. Teach learners to answer source questions by naming the evidence type, stating one thing it reliably shows, and one thing it cannot show — the three-part structure Eduqas's own assessment objectives for this component reward.
