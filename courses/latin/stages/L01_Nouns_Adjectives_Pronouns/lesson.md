# L01_Nouns_Adjectives_Pronouns - Lesson: Nouns, adjectives and pronouns — the five declensions and agreement

## Goal
The learner declines nouns of all five declensions, agrees adjectives correctly and forms comparatives/superlatives, and recognises the required pronouns.

## Syllabus items taught here
- LAT-GR-decl1 - First declension nouns: decline first-declension nouns (mostly feminine) across all cases, singular and plural
- LAT-GR-decl2 - Second declension nouns: decline second-declension nouns (masculine -us/-er and neuter -um) across all cases, singular and plural
- LAT-GR-decl3 - Third declension nouns: decline third-declension nouns (mixed gender, consonant and i-stems) across all cases, singular and plural
- LAT-GR-decl4-5 - Fourth and fifth declension nouns: recognise and decline fourth-declension (-us/-u) and fifth-declension (-es) nouns
- LAT-GR-adj - Adjectives, agreement, comparative and superlative: decline regular adjectives, agree them with nouns in gender/number/case, and form comparatives and superlatives
- LAT-GR-pron - Pronouns and pronominal adjectives: recognise and decline the personal, demonstrative and relative pronouns from the defined vocabulary list

## How to teach this
Ask: "Puella agricolam videt." Now swap the word order to "Agricolam puella videt." Does the meaning change? It does not — the girl still sees the farmer. In English, word order carries meaning; in Latin, the *endings* do. This single fact underlies almost everything taught in this course, so return to it whenever a learner tries to translate by word position instead of by case.

#### LAT-GR-decl1 First declension nouns
Latin nouns decline (change their ending) to show their case — their grammatical role in the sentence. The five core cases: nominative (subject), vocative (address), accusative (direct object), genitive (possession, "of"), dative (indirect object, "to/for"), ablative (by/with/from, and other uses). The first declension (mostly feminine, e.g. puella, "girl") runs: puella (nom/voc), puellam (acc), puellae (gen), puellae (dat), puellā (abl) in the singular; puellae, puellās, puellārum, puellīs, puellīs in the plural. Learn this one pattern completely and hundreds of first-declension nouns follow it exactly — including some masculine exceptions by meaning, such as agricola (farmer) and nauta (sailor), which still decline like puella despite being masculine.

#### LAT-GR-decl2 Second declension nouns
The second declension covers masculine nouns in -us (dominus, "master": dominus, domine [vocative], dominum, dominī, dominō, dominō; plural dominī, dominōs, dominōrum, dominīs, dominīs), masculine nouns in -er (puer, "boy" — some, like puer itself, keep the -er throughout; others, like ager, "field," drop the e: agrī, agrō...), and neuter nouns in -um (bellum, "war": bellum, bellum, bellī, bellō, bellō; plural bella, bella, bellōrum, bellīs, bellīs). The defining neuter rule, true at every declension: nominative and accusative are always identical, and the neuter plural nominative/accusative always ends in -a — a rule worth teaching as a standalone fact since it recurs constantly in unseen translation.

#### LAT-GR-decl3 Third declension nouns
The third declension is the largest and most mixed group, covering all three genders with varied nominative forms but a single, learnable genitive singular ending in -is that reveals the stem: rex, regis (king, m.) — stem reg-; rēgīna is first declension, not to be confused. Typical pattern for a consonant-stem masculine/feminine noun (rex): rex, rex, rēgem, rēgis, rēgī, rēge; plural rēgēs, rēgēs, rēgum, rēgibus, rēgibus. Third-declension neuters (e.g. corpus, corporis, "body") follow the same neuter rule: nominative = accusative, and the plural nominative/accusative ends in -a (corpora). Because the nominative singular varies so much (rex, corpus, mīles, urbs), always learn a third-declension noun from its genitive singular, not its nominative alone.

#### LAT-GR-decl4-5 Fourth and fifth declension nouns
Less frequent but still assessed: the fourth declension, mostly masculine, ending in -us with genitive -ūs (exercitus, exercitūs, "army"), plus the neuter exception cornū, cornūs ("horn"); and the fifth declension, ending in -ēs with genitive -ēī or -eī (diēs, diēī, "day" — one of very few masculine fifth-declension nouns; rēs, reī, "thing," is feminine). Recognise these by their distinctive genitive singular endings (-ūs and -ēī/-eī) rather than expecting them to behave like the first three declensions.

#### LAT-GR-adj Adjectives, agreement, comparative and superlative
Adjectives must agree with the noun they describe in gender, number and case — not necessarily using the same declension pattern as that noun, but matching its grammatical category exactly. Most GCSE adjectives use first/second declension endings for feminine/masculine/neuter (bonus, bona, bonum, "good") even when describing a third-declension noun (rex bonus, "a good king" — bonus still uses second-declension endings because rex is masculine, even though rex itself declines differently). Comparatives add -ior/-ius to the stem (fortis → fortior, "braver"); superlatives add -issimus/-a/-um (fortissimus, "bravest"), with irregulars to learn by heart: bonus → melior → optimus; malus → peior → pessimus; magnus → maior → maximus.

#### LAT-GR-pron Pronouns and pronominal adjectives
The defined vocabulary list requires recognition of the personal pronouns (ego, tū, is/ea/id — he/she/it, and the reflexive sē), the demonstratives (hic, "this"; ille, "that"; ipse, "himself/the very"), and the relative pronoun qui/quae/quod ("who/which," essential for relative clauses, taught later in Syntax). These decline irregularly compared with ordinary nouns and adjectives and must simply be learned as set patterns, e.g. is, ea, id (nom.) → eum, eam, id (acc.) → eius, eius, eius (gen., "of him/her/it" — note eius is identical across all three genders, a frequent source of confusion).
