# L03_Verbs_Perfect_System_Passive_Deponent - Lesson: Verbs — perfect system, passive voice and deponent verbs

## Goal
The learner forms and translates the perfect and pluperfect indicative active, the passive voice across all required tenses, and deponent verbs.

## Syllabus items taught here
- LAT-GR-perf-ind - Perfect indicative active: form the perfect indicative active from the third principal part and translate it as a completed past action
- LAT-GR-plup-ind - Pluperfect indicative active: form and translate the pluperfect indicative active for an action completed before another past action
- LAT-GR-passive - Passive voice, all required tenses: form and translate the passive voice in the present, future, imperfect, perfect and pluperfect
- LAT-GR-deponent - Deponent verbs: recognise deponent verbs (passive form, active meaning) and translate them correctly across tenses

## How to teach this
Ask: "Puella ā agricolā vidētur" and "Puella agricolam videt" look almost identical but mean nearly opposite things: the first is "the girl is seen by the farmer" (passive), the second is "the girl sees the farmer" (active). Confusing active and passive is flagged by real Eduqas mark schemes as one of the most common serious errors — this stage exists to stop that happening.

#### LAT-GR-perf-ind Perfect indicative active
The perfect is built from the verb's third principal part (the perfect stem), not the present stem, so it must be learned per verb: amō, amāvī (I loved/have loved); regō, rexī; audiō, audīvī. Perfect endings are the same across all conjugations once the stem is known: -ī, -istī, -it, -imus, -istis, -ērunt (amāvī, amāvistī, amāvit, amāvimus, amāvistis, amāvērunt). Translate as a single completed past action ("I loved" or "I have loved"), contrasted sharply with the imperfect's ongoing sense from the previous stage.

#### LAT-GR-plup-ind Pluperfect indicative active
The pluperfect ("had done") describes an action completed before another past action, built from the perfect stem plus -eram, -erās, -erat, -erāmus, -erātis, -erant: amāveram (I had loved), rexerat (he had ruled). It regularly appears in a cum-clause or alongside a main perfect-tense verb to show the correct sequence of events: "cum pater vēnerat, puerī iam discesserant" (when the father had arrived, the boys had already left) — note both pluperfects here, one for each prior action.

#### LAT-GR-passive Passive voice, all required tenses
In the passive, the subject receives the action rather than performing it, and the doer (if named) appears in the ablative with ā/ab (by): puella ā agricolā vidētur (the girl is seen by the farmer). Present passive endings: -or/-r, -ris, -tur, -mur, -minī, -ntur (amor, amāris, amātur...). The imperfect and future passive follow the same active stems (-bā-, -bi-/-ē-) with passive endings (amābar, amābitur). The perfect and pluperfect passive are periphrastic: perfect participle + sum/eram (amātus est, "he was loved/has been loved"; amātus erat, "he had been loved") — and the participle agrees with the subject in gender and number, exactly like an adjective.

#### LAT-GR-deponent Deponent verbs
Deponent verbs are passive in form but active in meaning — a genuine trap for anyone translating mechanically. Common examples: loquor, loquī, locūtus sum (to speak); sequor (to follow); ūtor (to use, + ablative); conor (to try); proficiscor (to set out). "Puella loquitur" does not mean "the girl is spoken" — it means "the girl speaks," because loquor is deponent. Recognise deponents by learning them as vocabulary items with their principal parts, since their passive-looking forms give no reliable clue on their own.
