## Goal
The learner explains the episode's place within the Aeneid, recounts Aeneas's alliance with Evander, the banquet honouring Hercules, and Livy-neighbouring monster Cacus's cave and reign of terror, and recounts the discovery of the cattle theft.

## Syllabus items taught here
- LAT-C3A-VIRG-CONTEXT - Context: the Aeneid and Aeneas's visit to Pallanteum: explain how the Hercules and Cacus episode sits within Aeneas's visit to Evander and the poem's project of grounding Rome's future greatness
- LAT-C3A-VIRG-A - Passage A: Aeneas seeks alliance: explain how Aeneas establishes kinship with Evander to secure military support
- LAT-C3A-VIRG-B - Passage B: banquet in Hercules' honour: describe Evander's feast welcoming Aeneas and its link to Hercules
- LAT-C3A-VIRG-C - Passage C: the monster's lair: describe Evander's account of Cacus's cave and his reign of terror by fire and murder
- LAT-C3A-VIRG-D - Passage D: the cattle theft: recount how Cacus steals Hercules' cattle by dragging them backwards into his cave, and how the theft is discovered

## How to teach this
Ask: "The Aeneid is a poem about Aeneas founding the Roman people — so why does Virgil pause the plot to tell a story about Hercules fighting a monster?" Guide the learner to the answer: everything Aeneas sees at Pallanteum is a preview of Rome's future greatness, and the Hercules story is itself a myth already tied to a real Roman landmark (the Ara Maxima, still standing and in use in Virgil's own day) — Virgil is welding legendary and religious history onto the literal ground Rome would be built on.

#### LAT-C3A-VIRG-CONTEXT Context: the Aeneid and Aeneas's visit to Pallanteum
By Aeneid Book 8, the Trojan refugee Aeneas has reached Italy and is gathering allies for the coming war against the Latins and their ally Turnus. Advised in a dream to seek out Evander, a Greek king who has settled at Pallanteum — a small town on the future site of Rome itself, on the Palatine hill — Aeneas sails up the Tiber and arrives during Evander's own annual festival in honour of Hercules. The whole episode serves the Aeneid's larger project of grounding Rome's future greatness in a deep, sacred past: the ground Aeneas walks on will become the Roman Forum and Capitol, and the religious rite he witnesses (honouring Hercules) is one Virgil's own Roman readers still observed at the real Ara Maxima altar in the Forum Boarium.

#### LAT-C3A-VIRG-A Passage A: Aeneas seeks alliance
Aeneas approaches Evander peacefully, unarmed, and explains his mission by appealing to a shared ancestry: both Trojans and Arcadians (Evander's people) trace descent from the god Mercury's father Atlas, making Aeneas and Evander kinsmen rather than strangers. This appeal to blood-kinship, alongside Aeneas's own reputation and evident good faith, persuades Evander to receive him as a guest and, crucially, as a future ally against the Latins — Evander himself is too old to fight but promises troops and, eventually, introduces Aeneas to the Etruscans, whose support proves decisive later in the poem.

#### LAT-C3A-VIRG-B Passage B: banquet in Hercules' honour
Evander welcomes Aeneas into the middle of his people's annual sacrifice and feast in Hercules' honour, held at a great altar in a grove. Wine and roasted meat from the sacrificed ox are shared out, and Evander explains to his Trojan guest exactly why this rite exists before telling the myth itself — establishing the frame Virgil uses to deliver the whole Cacus story as an internal narration by Evander, a technique that lets Virgil present the myth as living local religious tradition rather than mere background colour, and prepares the audience for the aetiology (myth explaining the origin of a real custom) that follows.

#### LAT-C3A-VIRG-C Passage C: the monster's lair
Evander tells how the half-human monster Cacus, a son of the god Vulcan and so linked to fire, once terrorised the region from a vast cave on the Aventine hill, breathing fire from his mouth and covering the cave's entrance and doorposts with the heads and reeking gore of his human victims, displayed as trophies. Virgil piles up grotesque, Gothic detail — darkness, blood, half-animal appearance — to build Cacus into a genuinely monstrous threat, deliberately recalling the Homeric Cyclops Polyphemus (a "type" of monster Virgil's educated readers would instantly recognise) so that Hercules' coming victory reads as a civilising, heroic act that makes the land safe for human settlement.

#### LAT-C3A-VIRG-D Passage D: the cattle theft
Hercules, resting nearby with the cattle he has won from the triple-bodied monster Geryon (his Tenth Labour), unwisely lets his guard down, and Cacus seizes the opportunity to steal four of the finest bulls and four heifers, dragging them by their tails backwards into his cave so their hoofprints point the wrong way and disguise the theft. The trick very nearly works — Hercules, preparing to move the rest of the herd on, is on the point of leaving deceived, until one of the stolen cows lows from inside the hidden cave and is answered by the herd outside, giving away the theft and provoking Hercules' fury, the turning point that opens the next stage's combat.
