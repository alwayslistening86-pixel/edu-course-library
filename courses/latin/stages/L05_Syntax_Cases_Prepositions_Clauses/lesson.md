# L05_Syntax_Cases_Prepositions_Clauses - Lesson: Syntax — case usage, prepositions, relative clauses and time/cause/concession

## Goal
The learner applies the core uses of each case, translates prepositional phrases, and translates relative, temporal, causal and concessive clauses.

## Syllabus items taught here
- LAT-GR-case-usage - Case usage and time expressions: identify the core use of each case (nominative, vocative, accusative, genitive, dative, ablative) and the accusative/ablative of time and duration
- LAT-GR-prepositions - Prepositions and the cases they govern: translate the listed prepositions correctly with the case each one governs
- LAT-GR-relative-clauses - Relative clauses with the indicative: translate relative clauses introduced by qui/quae/quod with an indicative verb
- LAT-GR-temporal-causal-concessive-clauses - Temporal, causal and concessive clauses: recognise and translate clauses of time (cum, postquam), cause (quod, cum) and concession (quamquam)

## How to teach this
Ask: "Why is word order in Latin free to do something other than show who does what to whom?" Answer: because the endings already show it — so word order is free for emphasis or style, exactly as you will see in Livy and Virgil later in this course.

#### LAT-GR-case-usage Case usage and time expressions
Beyond simple subject (nominative) and object (accusative), the exam expects fluent recognition of: genitive for possession (liber puellae, "the girl's book"); dative for the indirect object (puerō librum dedit, "he gave the book to the boy"); ablative for by/with/from and manner (gladiō pugnāvit, "he fought with a sword"). Time expressions use specific cases without a preposition: accusative for duration ("for how long" — trēs hōrās manēbat, "he stayed for three hours"); ablative for time when/within which ("at/in" — tertiā hōrā vēnit, "he came at the third hour"). Confusing these two is a frequently tested error, since both can superficially look like "a time phrase with no preposition."

#### LAT-GR-prepositions Prepositions and the cases they govern
Prepositions govern a fixed case that must be learned together with the word, not guessed: in, ad, per, trans + accusative typically show motion towards/through (in urbem, "into the city"); in, sub, cum, ab/ā, dē, ē/ex + ablative typically show static location, separation or "with" (in urbe, "in the city"; cum amīcō, "with a friend"). "In" is the classic trap: in + accusative = motion into; in + ablative = static position in. Always check the case ending, not just the preposition, before translating.

#### LAT-GR-relative-clauses Relative clauses with the indicative
A relative clause is introduced by quī/quae/quod (who/which) and adds information about a noun already named (the antecedent), with the relative pronoun agreeing with its antecedent in gender and number but taking its own case from its role within its own clause: "puella quam videō" (the girl whom I see — quam is accusative because she is the object of videō, even though puella, her antecedent, is nominative). This case-shifting is worth drilling explicitly, since learners often wrongly copy the antecedent's case onto the relative pronoun.

#### LAT-GR-temporal-causal-concessive-clauses Temporal, causal and concessive clauses
Cum can introduce a temporal clause ("when," often with the imperfect/pluperfect subjunctive for circumstance, though a simple indicative cum-clause is also assessed) or, distinctly, a causal clause ("since/because," also frequently with the subjunctive). Postquam ("after") and ubi ("when") introduce temporal clauses with the indicative. Quod ("because") introduces a straightforward causal clause with the indicative. Quamquam ("although") introduces a concessive clause, again normally with the indicative at this level. Distinguishing these connectives by meaning, not just recognising "cum" as one fixed word, is essential since cum's meaning depends on the mood of the verb that follows it.
