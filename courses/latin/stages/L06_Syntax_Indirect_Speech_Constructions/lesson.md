# L06_Syntax_Indirect_Speech_Constructions - Lesson: Syntax — indirect statement, question, command and other required constructions

## Goal
The learner translates indirect statements, questions and commands, participial constructions short of the ablative absolute, open conditionals, and purpose/result clauses.

## Syllabus items taught here
- LAT-GR-indirect-statement - Indirect statement: recognise and translate accusative-and-infinitive indirect statements after verbs of saying/thinking/knowing
- LAT-GR-indirect-question - Indirect question: recognise and translate indirect questions with the subjunctive
- LAT-GR-indirect-command - Indirect command: recognise and translate indirect commands introduced by ut/ne with the subjunctive, distinguishing them from result clauses
- LAT-GR-participial-constr - Participial constructions (excluding ablative absolute): translate participial phrases agreeing with a noun already in the sentence, correctly distinguishing this from the ablative absolute (a literary feature outside Component 1's syntax list)
- LAT-GR-conditionals - Conditional sentences (present and past open): translate open conditional sentences in the present and past ("if X, then Y" with no unreality implied)
- LAT-GR-purpose-result-clauses - Purpose and result clauses: distinguish and translate purpose clauses (ut/ne + subjunctive, "in order to") from result clauses (tam...ut, "so...that")

## How to teach this
An exam passage reads: "imperāvit ut mīlitēs fugerent." A beginner might translate this literally as "he ordered so that the soldiers fled" — but this loses marks. The real construction being tested is an indirect command, and real GCSE examiners specifically flag this exact mistranslation as a common error worth teaching against directly.

#### LAT-GR-indirect-statement Indirect statement
After a verb of saying, thinking, knowing or perceiving (dīcit, putat, scit, videt), Latin reports what is said/thought using the accusative-and-infinitive construction rather than a "that" clause: "dīcit puellam cantāre" (he says that the girl is singing — literally "he says the girl to be singing"). The subject of the reported statement goes into the accusative, and its verb becomes an infinitive (present infinitive for the same time as the main verb, perfect infinitive for earlier time: dīcit puellam cantāvisse, "he says that the girl sang/has sung").

#### LAT-GR-indirect-question Indirect question
An indirect question reports a question rather than asking it directly, and its verb goes into the subjunctive: "rogat cūr puella cantet" (he asks why the girl is singing). The introductory question word (cūr, quis, ubi, quid) stays the same as in a direct question, but the mood of the verb changes from indicative (in a direct question) to subjunctive (in the indirect form) — this mood shift is the grammatical signal that marks the construction, and translating it back into natural English simply removes the subjunctive's own trace ("why the girl is singing," not "why the girl may be singing").

#### LAT-GR-indirect-command Indirect command
"Imperāvit ut mīlitēs fugerent" is an indirect command: "he ordered that the soldiers flee / he ordered the soldiers to flee," introduced by ut (or nē if negative) with a subjunctive verb — not a literal "so that" result clause. Distinguishing this from a genuine result clause (tam...ut, "so...that," taught below) is one of the most commonly tested and commonly mistranslated constructions at GCSE, precisely because both use ut + subjunctive on the surface.

#### LAT-GR-participial-constr Participial constructions (excluding ablative absolute)
A participle can describe a noun already present in the sentence, agreeing with it in gender, number and case, functioning like an adjective with its own action attached: "puella flēns discessit" (the girl left, crying / the weeping girl left). This differs from the ablative absolute (a participle + noun both in the ablative, grammatically independent of the rest of the sentence, e.g. "hostibus fugientibus," "with the enemy fleeing") — the ablative absolute is explicitly excluded from Component 1's assessed syntax list, though it appears as a genuine literary feature in the Component 3A set texts (see Livy's stylistic fingerprints, taught in the literature stages). At Component 1, only participles agreeing with an existing sentence element are tested.

#### LAT-GR-conditionals Conditional sentences (present and past open)
Only "open" conditionals (no implication of unreality) are required: present open ("if X happens/is happening, Y happens" — sī id facis, errās, "if you do that, you are wrong") and past open ("if X happened, Y happened" — sī id fēcistī, erravistī, "if you did that, you were wrong"), both using the indicative in both halves. Unfulfilled/unreal conditionals (using the subjunctive, "if X had happened, Y would have happened") are not part of Component 1's required syntax, though they appear as a literary feature in Livy (see the literature stages).

#### LAT-GR-purpose-result-clauses Purpose and result clauses
Purpose clauses (ut/nē + subjunctive, "in order to/so that... not") state the reason for an action: "vēnit ut mīlitēs vidēret" (he came in order to see the soldiers). Result clauses (tam...ut + subjunctive, "so...that") state a consequence, usually flagged by a preceding tam/tantus/tot: "tam fortis erat ut omnēs eum laudārent" (he was so brave that everyone praised him). Both use ut + subjunctive, so the surrounding signal word (a purpose clause has no "tam"; a result clause is triggered by one) is the reliable way to tell them apart, alongside indirect command (also ut + subjunctive but following a verb of ordering).
