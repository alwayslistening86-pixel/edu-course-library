# M02_Livy_Alps_Ascent_Descent_and_Style - Lesson: Livy — the Alpine ascent, the landslide, the fire-and-vinegar solution, and Livy's style

> **Set-text notice (tell the learner at the start of this stage):** Livy (Hannibal crosses the Alps) and Virgil (Hercules and Cacus) are Eduqas's prescribed Component 3A texts for the **2026 and 2027 exams only**. From 2028 Eduqas will prescribe different authors, and this course will be updated when it does. ## Goal
The learner recounts the army's Alpine crossing from the foothills to arrival in Italy and identifies Livy's characteristic stylistic features across the text.

## Syllabus items taught here
- LAT-C3A-LIVY-E - Passage E: arrival at the Alpine foothills: describe the army's arrival at the Alps and the harsh winter conditions of the initial ascent
- LAT-C3A-LIVY-F - Passage F: the nine-day ascent: recount the difficulties of the ascent, unreliable guides, and Hannibal's speech motivating his troops
- LAT-C3A-LIVY-G - Passage G: the landslide on the descent: describe the treacherous descent and the landslide that blocks the army's path
- LAT-C3A-LIVY-H - Passage H: fire, vinegar and arrival in Italy: explain the fire-and-vinegar engineering solution to the rockfall and the army's arrival in more hospitable land
- LAT-C3A-LIVY-STYLE - Livy's stylistic fingerprints: identify and explain the effect of ablative absolutes, gerunds/gerundives, neuter plural adjectives as nouns, result clauses and unfulfilled conditions in Livy's narrative

## How to teach this
Ask: "An army that has already crossed a major river with elephants still has to cross a mountain range in late autumn. What could possibly go wrong?" Use the list the learner generates (cold, hostile locals, narrow paths, exhausted animals, unreliable information) to preview the sequence of disasters Livy narrates, then teach the passages in order as an escalating crisis resolved only by Hannibal's improvisation.

#### LAT-C3A-LIVY-E Passage E: arrival at the Alpine foothills
On reaching the foothills, Hannibal's army meets the native Allobroges and other Gaulish tribes, some hostile, harassing the column from higher ground and rolling rocks down onto the marching soldiers. Livy stresses both the terror the mountains themselves inspire in men who have never seen snow-capped peaks before, and the onset of harsh late-season weather, with snow already visible on the heights — establishing that the army faces a double enemy: the local tribes and the terrain and climate itself. Livy's narrative slows here deliberately, building dread before the ascent proper begins, and setting up Hannibal's need for both diplomacy and force to buy the army safe passage through the lower passes.

#### LAT-C3A-LIVY-F Passage F: the nine-day ascent
The climb to the highest pass takes nine days of exhausting, dangerous marching, worsened by treacherous local guides and paths that repeatedly prove impassable for the baggage train and elephants, forcing costly detours. Reaching the summit, Hannibal halts the exhausted, demoralised army and, according to Livy, points out Italy spread below them, telling the men they are climbing not merely into Italy but to the walls of Rome itself, and that what remains is downhill, easy marching by comparison. Teach this speech as a set-piece of Livy's dramatic history-writing: whether or not the actual words were ever spoken, Livy uses direct or reported speech to characterise Hannibal as an inspiring leader at the story's lowest emotional point.

#### LAT-C3A-LIVY-G Passage G: the landslide on the descent
The descent proves even harder than the ascent: the path is narrower, icier and more exposed, and a landslide (in one part of Livy's account, an earlier avalanche or rockfall has already narrowed the track) has swept away a stretch of the route entirely, leaving a sheer drop with no way around it for the loaded pack animals and elephants. The army is effectively trapped for a day while Hannibal, camped on a ridge, waits for the panic to subside and assesses the ground, since simply forcing exhausted men and animals across a narrow icy ledge above a precipice would cause more losses than the enemy has managed so far.

#### LAT-C3A-LIVY-H Passage H: fire, vinegar and arrival in Italy
Hannibal's engineering solution is to have his soldiers build huge fires against the obstructing rock face, heat the stone thoroughly, then pour acidic wine (Livy's acetum, often rendered "vinegar") onto the heated rock, causing it to crack and crumble so it can be hacked away with iron tools to widen a usable path — a passage often set for translation because it shows Hannibal's resourcefulness under pressure rather than only his military force. Once a way is cleared, the army, animals and elephants (which had survived on short rations through the crossing) descend into the fertile plain of the Po valley, the territory of the Insubres, arriving in Italy roughly five months after leaving Spain and considerably reduced in numbers from the losses of the crossing.

#### LAT-C3A-LIVY-STYLE Livy's stylistic fingerprints
Across Book 21, Livy uses recurring devices worth naming precisely in an exam answer. Ablative absolutes (a noun-participle pair in the ablative, grammatically detached from the main sentence, e.g. "his rebus gestis," "these things having been done") let Livy compress background action economically and vary sentence rhythm. Gerunds and gerundives (verbal nouns/adjectives expressing purpose or necessity, e.g. "ad urbem oppugnandam," "for attacking the city") often mark military objectives. Livy frequently uses neuter plural adjectives as abstract nouns (e.g. "aspera," "the rough parts/difficulties"; "ardua," "the steep places") to describe terrain vividly and economically. Result clauses (tam...ut) build dramatic climax, and unfulfilled/unreal conditions ("if X had not happened, Y would have happened") let Livy speculate about how close disaster came — a distinctly literary, non-Component-1 use of the subjunctive that this course flags explicitly as excluded from the Language paper's own required syntax.
