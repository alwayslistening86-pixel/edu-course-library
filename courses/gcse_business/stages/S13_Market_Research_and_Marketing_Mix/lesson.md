# S13_Market_Research_and_Marketing_Mix - Lesson: Market research and the marketing mix (4Ps)

## Goal
The learner explains the purpose and methods (primary and secondary) of market research, distinguishes qualitative from quantitative data, and explains all four elements of the marketing mix (price, product, promotion, place).

## Syllabus items taught here
- 3.5.3 - The purpose and methods of market research: primary research (questionnaires, surveys, interviews, focus groups) and secondary research (internet, printed press), qualitative vs. quantitative data, market size and market share
- 3.5.4 - The marketing mix (4Ps): price (skimming, penetration, competitive, loss leader, cost-plus), product (USP, brand image, product life cycle, extension strategies, product portfolio), promotion (advertising, PR, sales promotion, sponsorship, social media) and place (retailers, wholesalers, telesales, e-commerce/m-commerce)

## How to teach this
Ask the learner: before a business spends thousands launching a new product, how could it find out in advance whether people will actually buy it? That question is exactly what market research answers. GCSE Business has real quantitative content throughout (at least 10% of all marks are quantitative skills): break-even (identifying break-even output and margin of safety from a chart -- AQA does not require the break-even formula or chart-drawing, but understanding the underlying contribution logic is how a learner reads a chart correctly), cash-flow forecasts (net cash flow, opening/closing balance), gross and net profit margins, and average rate of return on an investment. AQA does not give formulae in the exam for gross/net profit margin or average rate of return, so the learner must know these by heart, not just how to plug numbers in. Every numerical worked example in this course was computed and checked when this course was built, and the learner should always be made to show full working. AQA GCSE Business explicitly does NOT examine named motivational theories (e.g. Maslow, Herzberg, Taylor) by name -- only financial and non-financial motivation methods in general terms -- so this course does not teach or test theorist names either.

#### 3.5.3 The purpose and methods of market research: primary research (questionnaires, surveys, interviews, focus groups) and secondary research (internet, printed press), qualitative vs. quantitative data, market size and market share
**Market research** is gathering information about customers, demand and competitors, to reduce the risk of costly mistakes and support better business decisions. **Primary research** collects new, original data directly from potential customers -- methods include **questionnaires/surveys** (structured questions given to many people, often producing mostly quantitative data), **interviews** (one-to-one, often producing richer, qualitative detail), and **focus groups** (a small group discussing a product/idea together, useful for exploring opinions/reactions in depth) -- primary research is tailored exactly to the business's needs but is time-consuming and can be expensive. **Secondary research** uses data that already exists, collected by someone else for another purpose -- e.g. **internet research** (published reports, competitor websites, government statistics) and the **printed press** (newspapers, trade publications) -- secondary research is quicker and cheaper than primary research but may not exactly fit the business's specific question and could be out of date. Data gathered can be **quantitative** (numerical, e.g. '62% of respondents said yes' -- easy to compare/analyse statistically) or **qualitative** (opinions/attitudes/reasons, e.g. why a customer prefers one brand -- richer detail but harder to compare and can be more subjective). Findings help a business estimate **market size** (the total value/volume of sales in a market) and its own **market share** (the business's sales as a proportion of the total market) to support decisions.

#### 3.5.4 The marketing mix (4Ps): price (skimming, penetration, competitive, loss leader, cost-plus), product (USP, brand image, product life cycle, extension strategies, product portfolio), promotion (advertising, PR, sales promotion, sponsorship, social media) and place (retailers, wholesalers, telesales, e-commerce/m-commerce)
The **marketing mix (the 4Ps)** is the combination of decisions a business makes to market a product effectively. **Price** strategies include: **price skimming** (a high initial price for a new/innovative product, lowered over time, to maximise revenue from early adopters before competitors enter); **price penetration** (a low initial price to build market share quickly in a competitive market, often raised later); **competitive pricing** (setting price in line with rivals' prices); **loss leader** (pricing a product below cost to draw customers into a shop, hoping they buy other, profitable items too); and **cost-plus pricing** (adding a fixed percentage profit margin on top of the cost of making the product). **Product** decisions include giving a product a clear **unique selling point (USP)** (what makes it different from rivals) and a strong **brand image**; every product moves through a **product life cycle** -- research & development, introduction, growth, maturity and decline -- and businesses use **extension strategies** (e.g. updated packaging, new features, targeting a new market, fresh advertising, or a price reduction) to prolong a product's life before it declines; larger firms manage a **product portfolio** (a range of products at different life-cycle stages, sometimes analysed with the Boston Matrix, to balance products bringing in cash today against ones being developed for the future). **Promotion** methods include **advertising** (newspapers, magazines, television, internet, billboards), **PR** (public relations -- managing a business's public image/reputation), **sales promotion** (point-of-sale displays, special offers, free samples, coupons, competitions -- short-term incentives to buy now), **sponsorship**, and **social media**. **Place** covers how a product reaches the customer: **retailers** (shops selling directly to the public), **wholesalers** (buying in bulk from producers and selling smaller quantities on to retailers), **telesales** (selling by phone), and **e-commerce/m-commerce** (selling via websites/apps, including on mobile devices).
