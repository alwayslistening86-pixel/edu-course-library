# S02_Aims_Objectives_Stakeholders - Lesson: Business aims/objectives and stakeholders

## Goal
The learner explains why businesses set aims and objectives, how these can change over time, and identifies the main stakeholder groups and their (sometimes conflicting) objectives.

## Syllabus items taught here
- 3.1.3 - Setting business aims and objectives: what aims and objectives are, why businesses set them, how they can change, and using them to judge success
- 3.1.4 - Stakeholders: owners, employees, customers, local community and suppliers -- their objectives and how they influence and are affected by a business

## How to teach this
Ask the learner: does a brand-new café want the same thing as a huge supermarket chain? A new business often just wants to survive; a big one might chase growth or market share -- objectives change with circumstances. GCSE Business has real quantitative content throughout (at least 10% of all marks are quantitative skills): break-even (identifying break-even output and margin of safety from a chart -- AQA does not require the break-even formula or chart-drawing, but understanding the underlying contribution logic is how a learner reads a chart correctly), cash-flow forecasts (net cash flow, opening/closing balance), gross and net profit margins, and average rate of return on an investment. AQA does not give formulae in the exam for gross/net profit margin or average rate of return, so the learner must know these by heart, not just how to plug numbers in. Every numerical worked example in this course was computed and checked when this course was built, and the learner should always be made to show full working. AQA GCSE Business explicitly does NOT examine named motivational theories (e.g. Maslow, Herzberg, Taylor) by name -- only financial and non-financial motivation methods in general terms -- so this course does not teach or test theorist names either.

#### 3.1.3 Setting business aims and objectives: what aims and objectives are, why businesses set them, how they can change, and using them to judge success
A business **aim** is a long-term overall goal (e.g. 'to become the leading coffee shop chain in the region'); an **objective** is a more specific, often measurable, shorter-term target that helps achieve the aim (e.g. 'to open five new branches within two years'). Common business objectives include **survival** (especially for new/struggling businesses), **profit maximisation**, **growth** (in sales, market share or size), **market share**, and increasingly **social/ethical objectives** (e.g. being seen as environmentally responsible). Objectives are set to give a business **direction and focus**, to help staff understand what they are working towards, and to provide a clear **benchmark against which success can be judged** -- did the business achieve what it set out to do? Objectives commonly **change over time**: a new business may start by focusing purely on survival, then shift to growth once established, and a mature, dominant business might add wider social/ethical objectives once profit and survival are secure.

#### 3.1.4 Stakeholders: owners, employees, customers, local community and suppliers -- their objectives and how they influence and are affected by a business
**Stakeholders** are any individuals or groups with an interest in, or affected by, a business's activities and decisions. The main groups are: **owners** (want profit/a return on their investment, and the business's long-term success), **employees** (want fair pay, job security and good working conditions), **customers** (want good-quality products/services at a fair price, and reliable service), the **local community** (wants local jobs, minimal pollution/disruption e.g. from traffic or noise, and community investment), and **suppliers** (want to be paid promptly and in full, and want a business that keeps ordering from them regularly). Stakeholder objectives can easily **conflict**: e.g. owners wanting higher profit might push for lower wages, which conflicts with what employees want; or a business expanding a factory might create local jobs (good for the community) but also add traffic and noise (bad for the community) -- a business must weigh and balance these competing interests when making decisions.
