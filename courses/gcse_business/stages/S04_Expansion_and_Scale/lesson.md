# S04_Expansion_and_Scale - Lesson: Expanding a business, and economies/diseconomies of scale

## Goal
The learner explains methods of business expansion with their benefits and drawbacks, and distinguishes economies of scale from diseconomies of scale.

## Syllabus items taught here
- 3.1.7 - Expanding a business: methods of expansion, benefits and drawbacks, and economies and diseconomies of scale

## How to teach this
Ask the learner: could a corner shop simply decide overnight to become a supermarket chain? Growing a business takes deliberate methods -- and growing bigger brings both real cost savings and real new problems. GCSE Business has real quantitative content throughout (at least 10% of all marks are quantitative skills): break-even (identifying break-even output and margin of safety from a chart -- AQA does not require the break-even formula or chart-drawing, but understanding the underlying contribution logic is how a learner reads a chart correctly), cash-flow forecasts (net cash flow, opening/closing balance), gross and net profit margins, and average rate of return on an investment. AQA does not give formulae in the exam for gross/net profit margin or average rate of return, so the learner must know these by heart, not just how to plug numbers in. Every numerical worked example in this course was computed and checked when this course was built, and the learner should always be made to show full working. AQA GCSE Business explicitly does NOT examine named motivational theories (e.g. Maslow, Herzberg, Taylor) by name -- only financial and non-financial motivation methods in general terms -- so this course does not teach or test theorist names either.

#### 3.1.7 Expanding a business: methods of expansion, benefits and drawbacks, and economies and diseconomies of scale
Businesses can **expand** in different ways: **internal (organic) growth** -- opening new branches/outlets, expanding online, or launching new products, using the business's own profits/resources, which is slower but lower-risk and keeps the owners in full control; and **external growth** -- **merging** with another business (two firms agreeing to join as one) or **taking over** another business (buying a controlling stake in an existing firm), which is faster but can be expensive, carries integration risk (clashing cultures/systems), and, for a merger, can mean a loss of full control. *Benefits* of expansion generally include higher potential profit, increased market share/power, and (as covered below) economies of scale; *drawbacks* include the finance needed to fund growth, the risk of losing control/direction as the business gets larger and harder to manage, and the risk of diseconomies of scale. **Economies of scale** are falls in a firm's average cost of production as it grows larger -- e.g. buying raw materials in bulk at a discount (purchasing economies), affording large specialised machinery (technical economies), or borrowing more cheaply as a larger, lower-risk firm (financial economies). Beyond a certain size, however, a firm can suffer **diseconomies of scale** -- rising average costs as it grows further, typically from communication problems in a very large organisation and workers/managers feeling like a small, unmotivated part of a huge business, making coordination harder.
