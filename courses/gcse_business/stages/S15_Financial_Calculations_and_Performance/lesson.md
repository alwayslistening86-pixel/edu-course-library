# S15_Financial_Calculations_and_Performance - Lesson: Financial calculations and analysing financial performance

## Goal
The learner distinguishes fixed, variable and total costs, revenue, profit and loss, understands break-even output and margin of safety from a chart and calculates average rate of return, and calculates gross and net profit margin to analyse financial performance.

## How to teach this
Ask the learner: how many units of a product must a business sell before it stops making a loss and starts making a profit? That's break-even -- one of the most useful numbers in business. GCSE Business has real quantitative content throughout (at least 10% of all marks are quantitative skills): break-even (identifying break-even output and margin of safety from a chart -- AQA does not require the break-even formula or chart-drawing, but understanding the underlying contribution logic is how a learner reads a chart correctly), cash-flow forecasts (net cash flow, opening/closing balance), gross and net profit margins, and average rate of return on an investment. AQA does not give formulae in the exam for gross/net profit margin or average rate of return, so the learner must know these by heart, not just how to plug numbers in. Every numerical worked example in this course was computed and checked when this course was built, and the learner should always be made to show full working. AQA GCSE Business explicitly does NOT examine named motivational theories (e.g. Maslow, Herzberg, Taylor) by name -- only financial and non-financial motivation methods in general terms -- so this course does not teach or test theorist names either.

#### 3.6.3 Financial terms and calculations: fixed/variable/total costs, revenue, costs, profit and loss, break-even output and margin of safety (read from a chart), and average rate of return
**Fixed costs** do not change with the level of output (e.g. rent, insurance, salaried staff); **variable costs** change directly with output (e.g. raw materials, piece-rate wages); **total cost = fixed costs + variable costs**. **Revenue = selling price × quantity sold**; **profit = revenue − total cost** (a **loss** occurs when total cost exceeds revenue). The **break-even output** is the level of output at which total revenue exactly equals total cost -- the business is making neither a profit nor a loss. On a **break-even chart**, total cost and total revenue are both plotted against output; the break-even point is where the two lines cross, and the **margin of safety** is the gap between a business's actual (or planned) output and the break-even output -- read straight off the chart as the horizontal distance between the two -- showing how far output could fall before the business tips into a loss. AQA does **not** require the break-even formula or drawing a chart from scratch in the exam -- only identifying break-even output and margin of safety from a chart already given -- but understanding the underlying logic (**break-even output = fixed costs ÷ contribution per unit**, where **contribution per unit = selling price − variable cost per unit**) is exactly what makes a chart's break-even point and shape make sense. *Worked example (for understanding, not an exam formula):* a business has fixed costs of £5,000, sells at £50 and has a variable cost of £30 per unit: contribution per unit = £50 − £30 = £20, so break-even output = £5,000 ÷ £20 = **250 units**; if the business plans to make 400 units, its margin of safety = 400 − 250 = **150 units**. The **average rate of return (ARR)** measures the profitability of an investment project (e.g. new machinery, a new building or vehicles), and -- unlike break-even -- its formula is examinable, though not given in the exam: **ARR = (average annual profit ÷ initial cost of the investment) × 100**. *Worked example:* a business invests £60,000 in machinery, expecting total profit of £27,000 over 3 years: average annual profit = £27,000 ÷ 3 = £9,000; ARR = (£9,000 ÷ £60,000) × 100 = **15%** -- the higher the ARR, the more attractive the investment (all else equal), and it can be used to compare two different possible investment projects.

#### 3.6.4 Analysing the financial performance of a business: the income statement and statement of financial position, assets vs. liabilities, and gross/net profit margin
A business's financial performance can be analysed from two main financial statements. The **income statement** (sometimes still called the profit and loss account) shows revenue, costs and the resulting profit or loss over a period. The **statement of financial position** (sometimes still called the balance sheet) shows what a business owns and owes at a single point in time: **assets** (what the business owns or is owed, e.g. cash, stock, buildings, equipment, money owed by customers) and **liabilities** (what the business owes to others, e.g. loans, overdrafts, money owed to suppliers). From the income statement, two ratios are used (formulae not given in the exam, so must be known): **gross profit margin = (gross profit ÷ revenue) × 100**, where **gross profit = revenue − cost of sales** (the direct cost of making/buying what was sold) -- it shows how efficiently a business turns sales into profit before overheads/other expenses are deducted; and **net profit margin = (net profit ÷ revenue) × 100**, where **net profit = gross profit − expenses** (all other operating costs, e.g. rent, wages, marketing) -- it shows overall profitability after all costs are accounted for. *Worked example:* revenue is £120,000 and cost of sales is £72,000: gross profit = £120,000 − £72,000 = £48,000; gross profit margin = (£48,000 ÷ £120,000) × 100 = **40%**; if expenses are £30,000, net profit = £48,000 − £30,000 = £18,000; net profit margin = (£18,000 ÷ £120,000) × 100 = **15%**. These margins are used to compare performance: against the business's **own past performance** (is the margin improving or worsening year on year?), against **competitors** (is this business more or less profitable per pound of sales than similar rivals?), and from different **stakeholders' perspectives** (e.g. owners/investors care about profitability directly, while a bank considering a loan cares about it as a sign of ability to repay).
