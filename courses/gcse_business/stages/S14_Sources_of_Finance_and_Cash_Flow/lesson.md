# S14_Sources_of_Finance_and_Cash_Flow - Lesson: Sources of finance and cash flow

## Goal
The learner explains internal and external sources of finance and their suitability, and explains why cash flow matters, how cash-flow forecasts are built, and solutions to cash-flow problems.

## Syllabus items taught here
- 3.6.1 - Sources of finance: internal (retained profit, sale of assets) and external (family/friends, share issue, loans/mortgages, overdraft, trade credit, hire purchase, government grants), and their suitability
- 3.6.2 - Cash flow: why cash matters, the consequences of cash-flow problems, how and why cash-flow forecasts are built, net cash flow and opening/closing balances, and solutions to cash-flow problems

## How to teach this
Ask the learner: can a genuinely profitable business still run out of cash and be forced to close? Yes -- cash flow (money actually in the bank, when it's needed) is different from profit, and this stage explains why that distinction matters so much. GCSE Business has real quantitative content throughout (at least 10% of all marks are quantitative skills): break-even (identifying break-even output and margin of safety from a chart -- AQA does not require the break-even formula or chart-drawing, but understanding the underlying contribution logic is how a learner reads a chart correctly), cash-flow forecasts (net cash flow, opening/closing balance), gross and net profit margins, and average rate of return on an investment. AQA does not give formulae in the exam for gross/net profit margin or average rate of return, so the learner must know these by heart, not just how to plug numbers in. Every numerical worked example in this course was computed and checked when this course was built, and the learner should always be made to show full working. AQA GCSE Business explicitly does NOT examine named motivational theories (e.g. Maslow, Herzberg, Taylor) by name -- only financial and non-financial motivation methods in general terms -- so this course does not teach or test theorist names either.

#### 3.6.1 Sources of finance: internal (retained profit, sale of assets) and external (family/friends, share issue, loans/mortgages, overdraft, trade credit, hire purchase, government grants), and their suitability
A business needs finance to start up, run day-to-day, and expand. **Internal sources** come from within the business itself: **retained profit** (profit kept back from previous periods rather than paid out, and reinvested) -- free (no interest/repayment, no loss of control) but only available to an already-profitable, established business, and using it means it cannot be used for anything else; and **selling unwanted assets** (e.g. old equipment or unused property) -- raises cash without borrowing, but only a one-off amount and only if suitable unwanted assets exist. **External sources** come from outside the business: **family and friends** (often flexible/informal terms, but can strain personal relationships if things go wrong, and amounts are usually small); a **new share issue** (selling more shares -- only available to limited companies -- raises capital with no obligation to repay, but dilutes existing owners' share of control/profit); **loans/mortgages** (borrowed from a bank, repaid with interest over an agreed period -- predictable repayments, but interest adds to cost and security/collateral, e.g. property, is often required); **overdrafts** (a flexible, short-term arrangement to spend more than is in a bank account, up to an agreed limit -- flexible and only used (and charged) when actually needed, but interest rates are typically high and the bank can demand repayment at short notice); **trade credit** (buying goods/materials from a supplier now but paying later, e.g. in 30 or 60 days -- interest-free and helps short-term cash flow, but suppliers may charge more or refuse further credit if payments are late); and **hire purchase** (paying for an asset, e.g. machinery or a vehicle, in instalments while using it, owning it outright only once fully paid -- spreads the cost, but total cost is higher than paying upfront due to interest/charges); and **government grants** (money that, unlike a loan, does not need to be repaid, sometimes targeted at specific industries/regions -- but is often competitive to obtain and may have conditions attached). Suitability depends on the business: a **new business** with no track record and few assets often relies more on external sources (friends/family, loans, government grants), since it has no retained profit yet and may struggle to prove its reliability to lenders; an **established business** can more easily use retained profit or sell assets, and can usually borrow more easily/cheaply, having built up a track record.

#### 3.6.2 Cash flow: why cash matters, the consequences of cash-flow problems, how and why cash-flow forecasts are built, net cash flow and opening/closing balances, and solutions to cash-flow problems
**Cash** (money immediately available, e.g. in a business bank account) is vital because a business needs it to pay its day-to-day bills -- wages, suppliers, rent -- as they fall due; crucially, a business can be **profitable on paper but still run out of cash** (e.g. if customers are slow to pay, or too much cash is tied up in stock), and running out of cash, even briefly, can force a business to close even while it remains profitable overall. Consequences of **cash-flow problems** include being unable to pay staff or suppliers on time (damaging staff morale and supplier relationships/credit terms), and, if severe and unresolved, business failure; **positive cash flow** (more cash coming in than going out) lets a business pay its bills on time, take advantage of new opportunities, and build a reserve for future difficulties. A **cash-flow forecast** predicts a business's future cash inflows and outflows, usually month by month, and is built and used to plan ahead, spot future cash shortages before they happen, and support a business plan/loan application. Each month: **net cash flow = total cash inflows − total cash outflows**; the **closing balance = opening balance + net cash flow**, and each month's closing balance becomes the **next month's opening balance**. *Worked example:* in January, opening balance is £2,000, cash inflows are £8,000 and cash outflows are £7,000: net cash flow = £8,000 − £7,000 = **£1,000**, so the closing balance = £2,000 + £1,000 = **£3,000**. February then opens with £3,000: if inflows are £6,000 and outflows are £7,500, net cash flow = £6,000 − £7,500 = **−£1,500** (a net outflow), so the closing balance = £3,000 − £1,500 = **£1,500**. If a forecast shows a cash-flow problem ahead, **solutions** include: rescheduling payments (e.g. agreeing to pay a supplier later, or asking customers to pay sooner), arranging an overdraft to cover a short-term gap, reducing outflows (e.g. cutting costs, delaying non-essential spending), increasing inflows (e.g. a short-term sales promotion, or chasing overdue customer payments), or arranging new finance.
