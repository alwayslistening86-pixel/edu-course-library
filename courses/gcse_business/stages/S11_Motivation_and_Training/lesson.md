# S11_Motivation_and_Training - Lesson: Motivating and training employees

## Goal
The learner explains financial and non-financial methods of motivating employees and their benefits, and explains induction, on-the-job and off-the-job training and their effects on the business.

## Syllabus items taught here
- 3.4.3 - Motivating employees: financial methods (salary, wage, commission, profit sharing) and non-financial methods (management style, training, responsibility, fringe benefits)
- 3.4.4 - Training: induction, on-the-job and off-the-job training, and their effect on productivity, quality, technology adoption and retention

## How to teach this
Ask the learner: would they work harder purely for more money, or does interesting work/recognition matter too? Real businesses use both financial and non-financial methods, because people are motivated by more than pay alone. GCSE Business has real quantitative content throughout (at least 10% of all marks are quantitative skills): break-even (identifying break-even output and margin of safety from a chart -- AQA does not require the break-even formula or chart-drawing, but understanding the underlying contribution logic is how a learner reads a chart correctly), cash-flow forecasts (net cash flow, opening/closing balance), gross and net profit margins, and average rate of return on an investment. AQA does not give formulae in the exam for gross/net profit margin or average rate of return, so the learner must know these by heart, not just how to plug numbers in. Every numerical worked example in this course was computed and checked when this course was built, and the learner should always be made to show full working. AQA GCSE Business explicitly does NOT examine named motivational theories (e.g. Maslow, Herzberg, Taylor) by name -- only financial and non-financial motivation methods in general terms -- so this course does not teach or test theorist names either.

#### 3.4.3 Motivating employees: financial methods (salary, wage, commission, profit sharing) and non-financial methods (management style, training, responsibility, fringe benefits)
Businesses use both **financial** and **non-financial** methods to motivate employees (AQA does not examine named motivation theories -- only the methods themselves and their effects). **Financial methods** include a fixed **salary** (an annual amount, usually paid monthly, regardless of hours worked) or hourly **wage**, **commission** (extra pay linked directly to sales made, common in sales roles, incentivising more effort/sales), and **profit sharing** (employees receive a share of the business's profit, giving them a direct stake in the business doing well). **Non-financial methods** include the **management style** used (e.g. involving/consulting staff in decisions rather than simply issuing orders can improve motivation), **training** (developing skills can make staff feel more capable and valued), giving staff more **responsibility** (interesting, meaningful work rather than repetitive tasks), and **fringe benefits** (non-cash perks, e.g. a company car, private healthcare, or extra holiday). Well-motivated staff tend to be more productive, produce higher-quality work, and are more likely to stay with the business, benefiting the business directly, while poorly motivated staff can be less productive and more likely to leave (raising recruitment/training costs to replace them).

#### 3.4.4 Training: induction, on-the-job and off-the-job training, and their effect on productivity, quality, technology adoption and retention
**Training** develops employees' skills and knowledge. **Induction training** introduces a new employee to the business -- its policies, procedures, layout and their specific role -- helping them settle in and become productive faster. **On-the-job training** happens while the employee continues working (e.g. shadowing an experienced colleague, or being coached at their desk) -- relatively cheap and directly relevant to the actual role, but can disrupt normal work and the trainer's own productivity, and mistakes made while learning can affect real customers/output. **Off-the-job training** happens away from the employee's normal workstation (e.g. an external course, a college day-release, or a structured training session elsewhere in the business) -- often higher quality/more specialist instruction and no disruption to ongoing production, but is more expensive and means the employee is unproductive (or absent) during the training. Well-designed training raises **productivity** (staff work more efficiently) and **quality** (fewer errors), helps staff adapt to **new technology**, and improves **staff retention** (employees value the investment in their development and are less likely to leave).
