# S08_Production_and_Procurement - Lesson: Production processes and procurement

## Goal
The learner distinguishes job, flow and lean production, explains just in time (JIT) production, and explains the role of procurement including JIT/JIC stock management and supplier selection.

## Syllabus items taught here
- 3.3.1 - Production processes: job and flow production, lean production and just in time (JIT)
- 3.3.2 - The role of procurement: JIT and just in case (JIC) stock management, supplier selection, and supply-chain management

## How to teach this
Ask the learner to compare a bespoke wedding cake (made one at a time, to order) with a factory-made loaf of sliced bread (identical, made continuously by the thousand) -- that contrast is job versus flow production. GCSE Business has real quantitative content throughout (at least 10% of all marks are quantitative skills): break-even (identifying break-even output and margin of safety from a chart -- AQA does not require the break-even formula or chart-drawing, but understanding the underlying contribution logic is how a learner reads a chart correctly), cash-flow forecasts (net cash flow, opening/closing balance), gross and net profit margins, and average rate of return on an investment. AQA does not give formulae in the exam for gross/net profit margin or average rate of return, so the learner must know these by heart, not just how to plug numbers in. Every numerical worked example in this course was computed and checked when this course was built, and the learner should always be made to show full working. AQA GCSE Business explicitly does NOT examine named motivational theories (e.g. Maslow, Herzberg, Taylor) by name -- only financial and non-financial motivation methods in general terms -- so this course does not teach or test theorist names either.

#### 3.3.1 Production processes: job and flow production, lean production and just in time (JIT)
**Job production** makes a single, often unique/bespoke product at a time, tailored to a specific customer's requirements (e.g. a wedding cake, a hand-built yacht, a bridge) -- allows high customisation and often commands a premium price, but is slow and has high unit costs (little benefit from specialisation/economies of scale). **Flow production** makes a continuous, standardised stream of identical products on a production line (e.g. cars, canned drinks, sliced bread) -- allows specialisation, automation and much lower unit costs at high volume, but is inflexible (hard to customise individual units) and requires a large, consistent level of demand to be worthwhile. **Lean production** is a set of techniques aimed at cutting waste (of time, materials, effort or stock) throughout the production process, to reduce costs and improve efficiency without sacrificing quality. **Just in time (JIT)** is a lean approach where materials/stock arrive exactly when needed for production, rather than being stored in advance -- this cuts the cost of holding/storing stock and reduces waste from unsold or perished stock, but leaves a business vulnerable to running out of stock if a delivery is delayed or demand suddenly spikes.

#### 3.3.2 The role of procurement: JIT and just in case (JIC) stock management, supplier selection, and supply-chain management
**Procurement** is the process of sourcing and purchasing the materials, parts and supplies a business needs. Stock can be managed under **just in time (JIT)** (as above -- stock arrives exactly when needed, minimising storage costs but risking stock-outs) or **just in case (JIC)** (the business holds a buffer/reserve of stock in case of unexpected demand or delivery problems -- safer against running out, but ties up cash in stock and incurs storage costs, with a risk of unsold stock becoming obsolete or wasted). Selecting suppliers involves weighing **price** (lower cost improves the business's own margins), **quality** (poor-quality supplies can mean poor-quality output and reputational damage), and **reliability** (a supplier that delivers late or inconsistently can disrupt production, especially under JIT). Good **supply-chain management** -- coordinating effectively with suppliers -- improves efficiency and can lower unit costs, e.g. through reliable, well-timed deliveries and strong supplier relationships that can secure better prices or priority in a shortage.
