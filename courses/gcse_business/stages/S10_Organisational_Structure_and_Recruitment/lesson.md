# S10_Organisational_Structure_and_Recruitment - Lesson: Organisational structures and recruitment/selection

## Goal
The learner explains span of control, chain of command, delayering and delegation and how tall/flat structures affect communication, and explains internal/external recruitment, job analysis/description/person specification, selection methods and contract types.

## Syllabus items taught here
- 3.4.1 - Organisational structures: span of control, chain of command, delayering and delegation, tall vs. flat structures and their effect on communication
- 3.4.2 - Recruitment and selection: internal and external recruitment, job analysis/description/person specification, selection methods, and full-time/part-time/job-share/zero-hours contracts

## How to teach this
Ask the learner to picture an army (many layers of rank from general down to private) versus a small start-up (everyone reports straight to the owner) -- that's the difference between a tall and a flat organisational structure. GCSE Business has real quantitative content throughout (at least 10% of all marks are quantitative skills): break-even (identifying break-even output and margin of safety from a chart -- AQA does not require the break-even formula or chart-drawing, but understanding the underlying contribution logic is how a learner reads a chart correctly), cash-flow forecasts (net cash flow, opening/closing balance), gross and net profit margins, and average rate of return on an investment. AQA does not give formulae in the exam for gross/net profit margin or average rate of return, so the learner must know these by heart, not just how to plug numbers in. Every numerical worked example in this course was computed and checked when this course was built, and the learner should always be made to show full working. AQA GCSE Business explicitly does NOT examine named motivational theories (e.g. Maslow, Herzberg, Taylor) by name -- only financial and non-financial motivation methods in general terms -- so this course does not teach or test theorist names either.

#### 3.4.1 Organisational structures: span of control, chain of command, delayering and delegation, tall vs. flat structures and their effect on communication
An **organisational structure** shows the formal layers of management and reporting relationships within a business. The **chain of command** is the line of authority running from the top (e.g. the managing director) down through each level to the most junior staff -- it shows who reports to whom. The **span of control** is the number of subordinates a manager directly supervises. A **tall structure** has many layers/levels of management, typically meaning a narrow span of control at each level and a long chain of command (messages take longer to travel top to bottom, and there is more scope for miscommunication, but each manager can supervise their smaller team closely). A **flat structure** has few layers, typically a wide span of control, and a short chain of command (faster, more direct communication and quicker decision-making, but each manager has more staff to directly oversee, which can be harder to manage closely). **Delayering** is removing a layer of management from the hierarchy (e.g. to cut costs or speed up communication), which widens the span of control for the managers who remain. **Delegation** is a manager passing authority for a specific task down to a subordinate -- this can free up the manager's time for more important work and can motivate/develop the subordinate, but the manager remains ultimately responsible for the outcome.

#### 3.4.2 Recruitment and selection: internal and external recruitment, job analysis/description/person specification, selection methods, and full-time/part-time/job-share/zero-hours contracts
**Recruitment** can be **internal** (filling a vacancy from within the existing workforce, e.g. promoting a current employee -- faster, cheaper, and the candidate is already known and trained in the business's ways, but brings in no genuinely new skills/ideas and creates a new vacancy elsewhere) or **external** (recruiting from outside the business -- brings fresh skills/ideas and a wider pool of candidates, but is slower, more expensive, and the new hire needs induction/training). The recruitment process typically involves **job analysis** (identifying exactly what a role involves), from which a **job description** (the specific duties and responsibilities of the role) and a **person specification** (the skills, qualifications and experience an ideal candidate should have) are written, used to attract and shortlist suitable applicants. **Selection methods** (choosing between shortlisted candidates) commonly include interviews, tests, and reviewing references/CVs. Businesses also choose between different **contract types**: **full-time** and **part-time** contracts (fewer hours), **job share** (two people sharing one full-time role, splitting the hours/responsibilities between them), and **zero-hours contracts** (the employer offers no guaranteed minimum hours, and the employee is not obliged to accept any offered shift) -- each contract type suits different staffing needs and costs.
