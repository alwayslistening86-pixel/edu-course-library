# S01_Purpose_and_Ownership - Lesson: The purpose of business and business ownership

## Goal
The learner explains why businesses exist and why people start them, and distinguishes the main forms of business ownership in the UK, with the advantages and disadvantages of each.

## Syllabus items taught here
- 3.1.1 - The purpose and nature of businesses: why businesses exist, reasons for starting a business, enterprise and entrepreneurship, the dynamic (constantly changing) nature of business
- 3.1.2 - Business ownership: sole traders, partnerships, private limited companies (ltd), public limited companies (plc), not-for-profit organisations/social enterprises

## How to teach this
Ask the learner to name a local business they use -- is it run by one person, a family, or is it a big company with shareholders? That choice of ownership shapes almost everything else about how it runs. GCSE Business has real quantitative content throughout (at least 10% of all marks are quantitative skills): break-even (identifying break-even output and margin of safety from a chart -- AQA does not require the break-even formula or chart-drawing, but understanding the underlying contribution logic is how a learner reads a chart correctly), cash-flow forecasts (net cash flow, opening/closing balance), gross and net profit margins, and average rate of return on an investment. AQA does not give formulae in the exam for gross/net profit margin or average rate of return, so the learner must know these by heart, not just how to plug numbers in. Every numerical worked example in this course was computed and checked when this course was built, and the learner should always be made to show full working. AQA GCSE Business explicitly does NOT examine named motivational theories (e.g. Maslow, Herzberg, Taylor) by name -- only financial and non-financial motivation methods in general terms -- so this course does not teach or test theorist names either.

#### 3.1.1 The purpose and nature of businesses: why businesses exist, reasons for starting a business, enterprise and entrepreneurship, the dynamic (constantly changing) nature of business
Businesses exist to satisfy the needs and wants of customers by producing goods and/or services, most commonly with the aim of making a **profit** (though not always -- see not-for-profits below). People start businesses for many reasons: to be their own boss (**independence**), to pursue a passion or hobby, to exploit a gap they have spotted in the market, or simply for financial reward. **Enterprise** is the willingness and ability to take the risk of organising resources to start and run a business; an **entrepreneur** is the person who does this, bearing the financial risk of the venture (if it fails, they can lose the money they invested). Business is **dynamic** -- constantly changing -- because of competition, changing customer tastes, new technology, and the wider economic, legal and social environment, so what makes a business successful today may not work tomorrow.

#### 3.1.2 Business ownership: sole traders, partnerships, private limited companies (ltd), public limited companies (plc), not-for-profit organisations/social enterprises
The UK's main forms of **business ownership**: a **sole trader** is a business owned and run by one person (though they may employ staff) -- easy and cheap to set up, the owner keeps all the profit and has full control, but has **unlimited liability** (their personal assets, e.g. their house, are at risk if the business cannot pay its debts) and can find it harder to raise finance. A **partnership** is owned by two or more people who share the capital, work, profit and (usually) unlimited liability -- more capital and shared skills/workload than a sole trader, but profit is shared and partners can disagree. A **private limited company (ltd)** has **limited liability** (owners/shareholders can only lose what they invested, not their personal assets) and sells shares privately (not on a public stock exchange, and only with the agreement of existing shareholders) -- easier to raise finance than a sole trader/partnership, but must file annual accounts publicly at Companies House and faces more regulation. A **public limited company (plc)** also has limited liability, but can sell shares to the general public on a stock exchange -- able to raise very large amounts of capital this way, but faces the risk of an unwanted takeover (anyone can buy enough shares to gain control) and even more regulation/public scrutiny. A **not-for-profit organisation** (e.g. a charity or social enterprise) exists mainly to achieve a social, charitable or environmental purpose rather than to maximise profit for owners -- any surplus is normally reinvested in that purpose rather than paid out.
