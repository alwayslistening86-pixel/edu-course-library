# S03_ADV_Core - Lesson: Advocacy: submission structure, examination-in-chief and cross-examination basics, and court etiquette

## Goal
The learner structures a persuasive oral submission, asks proper examination-in-chief and cross-examination questions, and conducts themselves correctly in a court or tribunal setting.

## Syllabus items taught here
- SQE2-ADV-1 - Submission structure: learn and apply: a clear, logical, persuasive structure for an oral submission
- SQE2-ADV-2 - Examination-in-chief basics: learn and apply: open, non-leading questions that let a witness give their own account
- SQE2-ADV-3 - Cross-examination basics: learn and apply: controlled leading questions that test and challenge a witness's evidence
- SQE2-ADV-4 - Court etiquette and procedure: learn and apply: forms of address, conduct, bundles and courtroom procedure

## How to teach this
Ask the learner to stand up (literally, if practicable) and give a 60-second submission on "why the court should allow a two-week extension of time" with no preparation. Then show how 45 minutes' real preparation time in the SQE2 assessment should be spent: read the brief once for the gist, again for detail, plan structure, note the two or three strongest points, and rehearse the opening sentence, so the candidate is never starting cold.

#### SQE2-ADV-1 Submission structure
Open by identifying yourself, who you appear for, and the application or issue before the court in one sentence. State your proposition up front ("I submit that permission should be granted because...") rather than building up to it. Give your best point first, then supporting points in descending order of strength, each supported by a fact or authority. Anticipate and address the strongest point against you rather than ignoring it. Close by stating precisely what order you are asking the court to make. A clear roadmap ("I will make three submissions...") helps the tribunal follow you and signals structure, which is itself assessed.

#### SQE2-ADV-2 Examination-in-chief basics
When questioning your own witness, ask open, non-leading questions ("What happened next?", "Can you describe what you saw?") so the account comes from the witness, not from you. Leading questions (questions suggesting their own answer, "You saw the red car speed off, didn't you?") are generally not permitted in chief except on undisputed background (name, address, formal matters). Build the account chronologically and let the witness's own words carry the weight; pause after answers rather than rushing to the next question, and avoid comment or argument - that belongs in submissions, not in the witness's evidence.

#### SQE2-ADV-3 Cross-examination basics
Cross-examination uses controlled, leading, closed questions that put your case and test the other side's evidence ("You didn't actually see the driver's face, did you?"). Keep questions short, one fact per question, and know the answer you expect before you ask; never ask an open question you cannot control. Put your own case to the witness where required by the "rule in Browne v Dunn" convention (challenge evidence you intend to contradict). Stay controlled and courteous even if the witness is evasive - visible frustration undermines credibility with the tribunal more than an evasive witness does.

#### SQE2-ADV-4 Court etiquette and procedure
Address the tribunal correctly for its level ("Your Honour," "Sir/Madam," "Madam/Master" as directed) and refer to yourself and opponents formally rather than by first name. Stand when addressing the court, stay for the ruling, and do not interrupt the bench. Have your bundle paginated and know where documents are before you stand up; when referring to a page, guide the tribunal to it explicitly ("page 12 of the bundle, please"). Time-manage strictly - if given 15 minutes, structure to finish within it, since running over signals poor preparation and can be cut off mid-submission.
