# S10_WRI_Worked_Scenarios - Lesson: Legal writing across the five SQE2 practice-area contexts

## Goal
The learner writes an accurate, correctly structured document (typically a letter of advice) in each of the five SQE2 practice-area contexts.

## Syllabus items taught here
- SQE2-WRI-CRI-1 - Worked scenario: letter of advice on plea options: apply writing skill to a Criminal Practice document
- SQE2-WRI-DR-1 - Worked scenario: letter before claim: apply writing skill to a Dispute Resolution document
- SQE2-WRI-PROP-1 - Worked scenario: report on title letter: apply writing skill to a Property Practice document
- SQE2-WRI-WILLS-1 - Worked scenario: letter of advice on executor duties: apply writing skill to a Wills and Probate document
- SQE2-WRI-BUS-1 - Worked scenario: letter of advice on company formation: apply writing skill to a Business Organisations document

## How to teach this
Ask: "If the reader is a frightened client rather than a confident business director, does your letter change even though the legal point is the same?" Use this to draw out that tone and what is explained versus assumed should shift by both context and by who the letter is actually addressed to, even though the writing method itself is constant.

#### SQE2-WRI-CRI-1 Worked scenario: letter of advice on plea options
Scenario: write to Mr Douglas (from the interviewing stage) advising on plea options given the evidence against him. Lead with a clear statement of the realistic options (guilty plea with credit for timing, or contest), not a lecture on procedure. Explain briefly and without jargon what "credit for an early guilty plea" means in practical terms, address his likely worry (a criminal record, sentence severity) directly, and end with a clear recommendation and next steps, including any deadline for entering a plea.

#### SQE2-WRI-DR-1 Worked scenario: letter before claim
Scenario: write a letter before claim to a supplier on behalf of Ms Whitfield, alleging breach of contract for late delivery. Structure: state the claim clearly, summarise the relevant facts and the breach alleged, state what is required (payment, performance, or another remedy) and by when, and state the consequence of non-compliance (proceedings) without being needlessly aggressive. This is a formal, third-party-facing document: precise, factual, and litigation-aware, avoiding any statement that could later look like an unsupported admission or overreach.

#### SQE2-WRI-PROP-1 Worked scenario: report on title letter
Scenario: write a report on title to Mr and Mrs Kaur (the buyers) summarising what the title search and enquiries revealed. Structure: confirm what was checked, state clearly whether title is in order or flag specific issues (a restrictive covenant, a missing consent), explain in plain English what any issue means practically for them, and set out next steps and any outstanding matters before completion. Buyers are usually anxious about delay, so be precise about what remains outstanding and by when it should resolve.

#### SQE2-WRI-WILLS-1 Worked scenario: letter of advice on executor duties
Scenario: write to Mrs Dabrowska (named executor, facing a sibling's challenge) explaining her duties and options. Lead with reassurance that her role and the process are manageable, then explain clearly what being an executor requires (protecting the estate, dealing with the challenge properly rather than personally), and address the practical question she actually asked (can she still distribute assets, should she wait). Sensitivity of tone matters here as much as legal accuracy, given the bereavement and family conflict context.

#### SQE2-WRI-BUS-1 Worked scenario: letter of advice on company formation
Scenario: write to Mr Patel and Ms Byrne (the founders) advising on share structure and the need for a shareholders' agreement. Lead with the practical recommendation (proceed with formation, but put a shareholders' agreement in place addressing X, Y, Z) rather than a neutral menu of options with no view given. Explain technical terms (e.g. "drag-along," "tag-along," if used) briefly in plain English, since business clients are not always company-law literate even when they are commercially sophisticated.
