# S09_WRI_Core - Lesson: Legal writing: conventions, attendance notes, letters of advice and other conventional documents

## Goal
The learner writes clear, precise, audience-appropriate legal documents including attendance notes, letters of advice, file notes and formal correspondence.

## Syllabus items taught here
- SQE2-WRI-1 - Writing conventions: learn and apply: clear, precise, concise and audience-appropriate written language
- SQE2-WRI-2 - The attendance note as a document: learn and apply: the standard structure and content of an attendance note as a writing genre
- SQE2-WRI-3 - Letters of advice: learn and apply: structuring a client letter of advice
- SQE2-WRI-4 - Other conventional documents: learn and apply: file notes, emails and formal correspondence to third parties

## How to teach this
Give the learner one set of facts and ask them to write the SAME core message twice: once as a note to a colleague, once as a letter to the client. Compare the results to show that legal writing skill is not about knowing more law, it is about matching register, structure and content to the reader - which is exactly what the real 30-minute SQE2 writing task tests, using facts already established elsewhere in the assessment.

#### SQE2-WRI-1 Writing conventions
Write in clear, precise, concise language appropriate to the reader: plain English for clients, more technical language is acceptable for another lawyer, but jargon should never replace a clear point either way. Use short sentences and paragraphs, active voice, and one idea per paragraph. Avoid hedging ("it would appear that possibly...") where you have reached a clear conclusion, and avoid false certainty where the position is genuinely uncertain - say so and explain why. Proofread for tone: legal writing should be professional and measured, never casual or, at the other extreme, needlessly aggressive.

#### SQE2-WRI-2 The attendance note as a document
Beyond recording client interviews (see Client Interviewing), attendance notes record any significant event on a file: a phone call, an internal discussion, research undertaken. Use the standard shape: file reference, date/time, who was involved, purpose, what was said or done, and what happens next. Write in the third person and past tense, be factual rather than evaluative, and record enough detail that someone with no other knowledge of the file could understand what happened and why it mattered, without recording so much detail that the key point is buried.

#### SQE2-WRI-3 Letters of advice
Structure a letter of advice as: purpose of the letter (why you are writing), a summary of the position/facts as understood, the advice itself (clearly stated, not just a list of options with no recommendation), the reasoning briefly, and next steps with who does what by when. Open in a way the client can act on even if they read no further ("I am writing to confirm that, in my view, you are entitled to..."), then support it. Avoid burying the actual advice on page two; a client should never have to hunt for what you think they should do.

#### SQE2-WRI-4 Other conventional documents
File notes are brief, informal internal records (shorter and less formal than an attendance note) used to flag a point for the file. Emails to colleagues should be direct and structured like a mini-memo (issue, answer, reasoning) rather than a stream of consciousness. Formal correspondence to third parties (opposing solicitors, other professionals) should be measured, precise, and litigation-aware - avoid admissions, keep to the point, and use "without prejudice" markings correctly where the letter is genuinely part of settlement negotiations, not as a habitual label on everything.
