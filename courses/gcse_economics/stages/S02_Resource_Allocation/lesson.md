# S02_Resource_Allocation - Lesson: Resource allocation: markets, economic sectors, specialisation

## Goal
The learner explains how markets allocate resources, distinguishes factor markets from product markets, classifies economic activity into primary/secondary/tertiary sectors, distinguishes goods from services, and explains the costs and benefits of specialisation, division of labour and exchange.

## Syllabus items taught here
- 2.1 - Markets and the allocation of resources, including factor and product markets
- 2.2 - Economic sectors: primary, secondary and tertiary; goods and services
- 2.3 - Specialisation, division of labour and exchange: benefits and costs

## How to teach this
Ask the learner: if nobody planned it centrally, how does the country end up with roughly the right number of bakers, dentists and phone shops? The answer is the price mechanism in markets. Diagrams (demand/supply curves, shifts, externality diagrams) are described precisely in words, including axis labels and the direction and cause of every shift, since this course cannot draw them -- have the learner sketch each one by hand as it is described, then check their sketch against the description. AQA GCSE Economics requires quantitative skills throughout (at least 10% of all marks): every numerical worked example in this course (elasticities, costs/revenue/profit, economies of scale, gross/net pay, GDP/CPI/unemployment-rate calculations, balance of payments, exchange rates, interest) was computed and checked when this course was built, and the learner should always be made to show full working, not just a final answer, since AQA awards method marks. Real UK institutions (the Bank of England and its Monetary Policy Committee, HMRC, the Office for National Statistics) and real current UK tax/NI thresholds and rates are used for illustrative calculations, clearly labelled as such -- these change periodically, so flag to the learner that the method matters more than memorising the exact current figures, which examiners provide in any real question that needs them.

#### 2.1 Markets and the allocation of resources, including factor and product markets
A **market** is any arrangement that brings buyers and sellers of a good, service or resource together, whether physically (a market stall) or not (an online marketplace). Markets **allocate resources**: prices act as signals and incentives that determine what is produced and by whom, without any central planner -- if consumers want more of something, prices rise, which signals producers to supply more (and reallocates land, labour and capital towards producing it); if demand falls, prices fall and resources are reallocated elsewhere. Economists distinguish **factor markets** (where the factors of production themselves are bought and sold, e.g. the labour market, where firms buy workers' labour) from **product markets** (where the finished goods and services that factors combine to produce are bought and sold, e.g. the market for smartphones).

#### 2.2 Economic sectors: primary, secondary and tertiary; goods and services
Economic activity is classified into three **sectors**: the **primary sector** (extracting raw materials directly from nature -- farming, fishing, mining, forestry), the **secondary sector** (manufacturing and construction -- turning raw materials into finished or semi-finished goods, e.g. car assembly, house-building), and the **tertiary sector** (providing services rather than physical goods -- retail, banking, education, healthcare, tourism). In the modern UK economy the **tertiary sector is by far the largest** in terms of both output and employment, followed by secondary, with primary now the smallest share (the UK has moved from a manufacturing-dominated to a service-dominated economy over recent decades). A **good** is a physical, tangible item that can be touched and stored (a car, a loaf of bread); a **service** is an intangible act or benefit provided, which cannot be stored and is usually consumed at the point of production (a haircut, a doctor's consultation).

#### 2.3 Specialisation, division of labour and exchange: benefits and costs
**Specialisation** occurs when a person, firm or country concentrates on producing a narrow range of goods or services rather than trying to be self-sufficient; **division of labour** is a specific form of specialisation where the production process itself is broken into separate, repetitive tasks, each performed by a different worker (e.g. on a factory production line). Specialisation only works alongside **exchange** (trading the specialised output for the other things needed). *Benefits*: workers become more skilled and faster at a narrow task (raising productivity and output), firms can invest in task-specific machinery, and countries can produce what they are relatively best at and trade for the rest, raising overall living standards. *Costs*: work can become repetitive, monotonous and demotivating for workers doing the same narrow task all day; workers and regions can become **overdependent** on one skill or industry, so if demand for it collapses they may struggle to find alternative work (structural unemployment, covered in Topic 8); and quality can suffer if no single worker takes responsibility for the whole finished product.
