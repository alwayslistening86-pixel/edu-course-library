# S18_Money_and_Financial_Sector - Lesson: The role of money and the financial sector

## Goal
The learner explains the functions and a broad definition of money, and the role and importance of the financial sector for the economy, including the role of the Bank of England and of other financial institutions.

## Syllabus items taught here
- 11.1 - The role of money: functions and definition
- 11.2 - The role and importance of the financial sector, including the Bank of England

## How to teach this
Ask the learner: why is a £20 note actually worth anything -- it's just paper. What it is doing (letting them buy things, store value, and compare prices) is the real definition of money. Diagrams (demand/supply curves, shifts, externality diagrams) are described precisely in words, including axis labels and the direction and cause of every shift, since this course cannot draw them -- have the learner sketch each one by hand as it is described, then check their sketch against the description. AQA GCSE Economics requires quantitative skills throughout (at least 10% of all marks): every numerical worked example in this course (elasticities, costs/revenue/profit, economies of scale, gross/net pay, GDP/CPI/unemployment-rate calculations, balance of payments, exchange rates, interest) was computed and checked when this course was built, and the learner should always be made to show full working, not just a final answer, since AQA awards method marks. Real UK institutions (the Bank of England and its Monetary Policy Committee, HMRC, the Office for National Statistics) and real current UK tax/NI thresholds and rates are used for illustrative calculations, clearly labelled as such -- these change periodically, so flag to the learner that the method matters more than memorising the exact current figures, which examiners provide in any real question that needs them.

#### 11.1 The role of money: functions and definition
**Money** is defined by the **functions** it performs, not by any particular physical form: it acts as a **medium of exchange** (something universally accepted in payment for goods/services, avoiding the need for barter/a 'double coincidence of wants'), a **unit of account** (a common measure that lets the value of very different goods/services be compared, e.g. pricing both a coffee and a car in pounds), a **store of value** (it can be held and used to buy things later, retaining value over time -- though inflation erodes this, S12), and a **means of deferred payment** (debts/future payments can be specified in terms of money, e.g. a loan to be repaid next year). Because money is defined by these functions, the definition of money is **broader than just banknotes and coins (cash)**: it also includes money held in bank current and savings accounts (which can be spent electronically, by card or transfer), since these balances perform the same functions.

#### 11.2 The role and importance of the financial sector, including the Bank of England
The **financial sector** is the part of the economy made up of institutions that channel funds between savers and borrowers, and provide related services; its key agents include the **Bank of England** (the UK's central bank), **commercial (high street) banks**, and **building societies**. The **Bank of England** plays several roles: it sets the base interest rate through its Monetary Policy Committee (S14) to influence inflation and the wider economy, acts as the 'lender of last resort' to the banking system in a crisis, oversees financial stability (regulating banks to reduce the risk of another financial crisis), and issues banknotes. **Commercial banks and building societies** provide the everyday services that connect savers and borrowers: taking deposits (savings) from households and firms and paying interest on them, and lending that money out (as mortgages, personal loans and business loans) at a higher rate of interest, so that funds are channelled from those with a surplus (savers) to those wanting to borrow to spend or invest -- this role is essential to the wider economy, since without it, saving and borrowing/investment would be far harder to coordinate, holding back consumer spending, business investment, and so economic growth.
