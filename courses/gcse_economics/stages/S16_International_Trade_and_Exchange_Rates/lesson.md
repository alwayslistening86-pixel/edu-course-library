# S16_International_Trade_and_Exchange_Rates - Lesson: International trade and exchange rates

## Goal
The learner explains why countries trade and the importance of international trade to the UK, and explains how exchange rates are determined and their effects on consumers and producers.

## Syllabus items taught here
- 10.1 - Why countries trade and the importance of international trade to the UK
- 10.2 - Exchange rates: determination and effects on consumers and producers

## How to teach this
Ask the learner why the UK doesn't try to grow all its own bananas -- and how that same idea (each country doing what it does relatively best, and trading for the rest) applies across the whole economy. Diagrams (demand/supply curves, shifts, externality diagrams) are described precisely in words, including axis labels and the direction and cause of every shift, since this course cannot draw them -- have the learner sketch each one by hand as it is described, then check their sketch against the description. AQA GCSE Economics requires quantitative skills throughout (at least 10% of all marks): every numerical worked example in this course (elasticities, costs/revenue/profit, economies of scale, gross/net pay, GDP/CPI/unemployment-rate calculations, balance of payments, exchange rates, interest) was computed and checked when this course was built, and the learner should always be made to show full working, not just a final answer, since AQA awards method marks. Real UK institutions (the Bank of England and its Monetary Policy Committee, HMRC, the Office for National Statistics) and real current UK tax/NI thresholds and rates are used for illustrative calculations, clearly labelled as such -- these change periodically, so flag to the learner that the method matters more than memorising the exact current figures, which examiners provide in any real question that needs them.

#### 10.1 Why countries trade and the importance of international trade to the UK
Countries **trade** because no country can efficiently produce everything its citizens want: trade allows countries to specialise in producing what they are relatively good at (using their available resources/climate/skills most efficiently) and import the rest, raising the total output and choice available to everyone compared with each country trying to be self-sufficient. **Benefits of trade** include: access to a wider range of goods and services than a country could produce alone, lower prices through greater competition and economies of scale (producing for a bigger, global market), and higher living standards from the extra output specialisation and trade make possible. Trade is very important to the UK, a relatively **open economy**: the UK's main **exports** include services (financial services, business/professional services are a particular UK strength), machinery and transport equipment, and pharmaceuticals; main **imports** include machinery and vehicles, fuels, and food/consumer goods. The UK typically runs a surplus on trade in **services** but a deficit on trade in **goods** (covered together in the current account, S13).

#### 10.2 Exchange rates: determination and effects on consumers and producers
An **exchange rate** is the price of one currency expressed in terms of another (e.g. £1 = $1.25). Like any price, a **floating** exchange rate (the system most major currencies, including sterling, use) is determined by the **supply and demand** for a currency in the foreign exchange market: higher demand for sterling (e.g. from foreigners buying UK exports, or investing in the UK) pushes the pound's value **up** (appreciation); a rise in the supply of sterling being sold (e.g. UK residents buying more imports/investing abroad) pushes it **down** (depreciation). *Effects of a stronger pound (appreciation)* on consumers: imports become **cheaper** in pound terms (good for UK consumers buying foreign goods, and for firms importing raw materials); on producers: UK exports become **more expensive** in foreign-currency terms, potentially reducing overseas demand for them and harming export competitiveness. *Effects of a weaker pound (depreciation)* are the reverse: exports become cheaper abroad (boosting export competitiveness) but imports become more expensive (raising costs for UK consumers and firms reliant on imported goods/materials, and adding inflationary pressure). *Worked example:* at £1 = $1.30, an item priced at 800 in UK pounds costs foreign buyers 800 × 1.30 = **$1,040**; if the pound strengthens to £1 = $1.40, the same item now costs 800 × 1.40 = **$1,120** to foreign buyers -- more expensive, and so potentially less competitive, purely because of the exchange rate move, even though the UK price in pounds has not changed.
