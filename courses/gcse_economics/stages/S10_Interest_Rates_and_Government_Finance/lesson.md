# S10_Interest_Rates_and_Government_Finance - Lesson: Interest rates and government income and expenditure

## Goal
The learner explains how interest rates affect consumers' saving, borrowing and spending decisions and producers' saving, borrowing and investment decisions, calculates interest, and explains UK government revenue and spending, direct versus indirect taxation, and progressive taxation.

## Syllabus items taught here
- 7.1 - Interest rates, saving, borrowing, spending and investment
- 7.2 - Government income and expenditure, including direct/indirect and progressive taxation

## How to teach this
Ask the learner what they would do with £1,000 in savings if the interest rate offered suddenly jumped from 1% to 6% -- would they be more or less tempted to spend it now? Diagrams (demand/supply curves, shifts, externality diagrams) are described precisely in words, including axis labels and the direction and cause of every shift, since this course cannot draw them -- have the learner sketch each one by hand as it is described, then check their sketch against the description. AQA GCSE Economics requires quantitative skills throughout (at least 10% of all marks): every numerical worked example in this course (elasticities, costs/revenue/profit, economies of scale, gross/net pay, GDP/CPI/unemployment-rate calculations, balance of payments, exchange rates, interest) was computed and checked when this course was built, and the learner should always be made to show full working, not just a final answer, since AQA awards method marks. Real UK institutions (the Bank of England and its Monetary Policy Committee, HMRC, the Office for National Statistics) and real current UK tax/NI thresholds and rates are used for illustrative calculations, clearly labelled as such -- these change periodically, so flag to the learner that the method matters more than memorising the exact current figures, which examiners provide in any real question that needs them.

#### 7.1 Interest rates, saving, borrowing, spending and investment
The **interest rate** is the cost of borrowing money, or the reward for saving/lending it, usually expressed as a percentage per year. When interest rates **rise**: saving becomes more rewarding, so consumers tend to **save more** and spend less; borrowing becomes more expensive (higher monthly repayments on mortgages, loans and credit cards), so consumers tend to **borrow less and spend less**, reducing overall consumer spending in the economy. Producers respond similarly: higher interest rates make **borrowing to invest** in new machinery, premises or expansion more expensive, discouraging investment, while making it more attractive to simply **save** any spare cash rather than invest it in the business. When interest rates **fall**, the opposite happens: saving is less rewarding (discouraging saving, encouraging spending), and borrowing is cheaper (encouraging both consumer spending and business investment). *Worked example (simple interest):* £5,000 saved at 3% per year for 2 years earns interest of £5,000 × 3% × 2 = **£300**, giving a total of **£5,300**.

#### 7.2 Government income and expenditure, including direct/indirect and progressive taxation
UK **government revenue** comes mainly from taxation: **income tax** (on individuals' earnings), **National Insurance** contributions, **VAT** (Value Added Tax, on most spending), **corporation tax** (on company profits), and smaller sources such as excise duties (on fuel, alcohol, tobacco) and business rates; government also borrows to cover any shortfall. **Government spending** goes chiefly on the welfare state (state pensions and benefits), the NHS/health, education, and smaller amounts on defence, transport infrastructure, and debt interest. Taxes are classified as **direct taxes** (paid directly by the individual or firm on whom they are levied, straight to the government, e.g. income tax, corporation tax, National Insurance -- cannot easily be passed on to someone else) or **indirect taxes** (levied on spending on goods and services, e.g. VAT, fuel duty, tobacco duty -- collected by the seller but ultimately (at least partly) passed on to the buyer through a higher price). **Progressive taxation** means the proportion of income paid in tax rises as income rises (those who earn more pay a higher percentage, not just a higher amount) -- UK income tax is progressive, with a tax-free personal allowance and rising tax-rate bands as income increases, so it also helps to reduce income inequality (covered further in S13).
