# S14_Fiscal_and_Monetary_Policy - Lesson: Fiscal policy and monetary policy

## Goal
The learner explains fiscal policy and the government budget (balanced, surplus and deficit), and explains monetary policy, including how it is used to control inflation and pursue other government objectives.

## Syllabus items taught here
- 9.1 - Fiscal policy and the government budget
- 9.2 - Monetary policy

## How to teach this
Ask the learner: what two 'levers' does the government actually have to influence the economy -- how much it taxes and spends (fiscal policy), and (via the Bank of England) the cost of borrowing (monetary policy)? Diagrams (demand/supply curves, shifts, externality diagrams) are described precisely in words, including axis labels and the direction and cause of every shift, since this course cannot draw them -- have the learner sketch each one by hand as it is described, then check their sketch against the description. AQA GCSE Economics requires quantitative skills throughout (at least 10% of all marks): every numerical worked example in this course (elasticities, costs/revenue/profit, economies of scale, gross/net pay, GDP/CPI/unemployment-rate calculations, balance of payments, exchange rates, interest) was computed and checked when this course was built, and the learner should always be made to show full working, not just a final answer, since AQA awards method marks. Real UK institutions (the Bank of England and its Monetary Policy Committee, HMRC, the Office for National Statistics) and real current UK tax/NI thresholds and rates are used for illustrative calculations, clearly labelled as such -- these change periodically, so flag to the learner that the method matters more than memorising the exact current figures, which examiners provide in any real question that needs them.

#### 9.1 Fiscal policy and the government budget
**Fiscal policy** is the government's use of taxation and public spending to influence the level of economic activity and help achieve its objectives (e.g. cutting taxes or raising spending to boost growth and cut unemployment in a downturn -- 'expansionary' fiscal policy; or raising taxes/cutting spending to cool an overheating economy -- 'contractionary' fiscal policy). The **government budget** compares government spending with tax revenue over a period (typically a year): a **balanced budget** is where spending equals revenue; a **budget surplus** is where revenue exceeds spending (the government pays down some of its debt); a **budget deficit** is where spending exceeds revenue (the government must borrow to cover the gap, adding to the national debt -- the accumulated total of all past deficits). Persistent large deficits can raise concerns about the sustainability of government debt (interest must be paid on it, and lenders may demand higher interest rates if they see a government as riskier), but deficit spending (e.g. in a recession) can also help support demand and cushion an economic downturn.

#### 9.2 Monetary policy
**Monetary policy** is the use of interest rates (and related tools) to influence the economy, in the UK set independently by the **Bank of England's Monetary Policy Committee (MPC)**, which meets regularly to decide the base interest rate, targeting the government's inflation target (2% CPI inflation). If inflation is too high, the MPC tends to **raise** interest rates: this makes borrowing more expensive and saving more attractive (S10), reducing consumer spending and business investment, which cools demand in the economy and helps bring inflation back towards target -- though at some cost to growth and employment. If inflation is too low, or the economy needs stimulating (e.g. in a downturn), the MPC can **cut** interest rates to encourage spending and investment, boosting demand, growth and employment -- though this risks pushing inflation up if overdone. Monetary policy can therefore be used to pursue several government objectives at once (price stability directly, growth and employment indirectly), though its objectives can conflict with each other, as with fiscal policy.

Longer-term policies aimed at improving the economy's productive capacity (rather than managing demand) are S15.
