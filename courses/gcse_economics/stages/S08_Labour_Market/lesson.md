# S08_Labour_Market - Lesson: The labour market: wages, wage differentials, gross and net pay

## Goal
The learner explains how the labour market operates and how wages are determined through the supply and demand for labour, explains why wages differ between jobs, and distinguishes gross pay from net pay, including calculating each.

## Syllabus items taught here
- 5.4 - The labour market: wage determination, wage differentials, gross and net pay

## How to teach this
Ask the learner why a brain surgeon typically earns far more than a shop assistant -- get them thinking in terms of the supply of (and demand for) each kind of labour. Diagrams (demand/supply curves, shifts, externality diagrams) are described precisely in words, including axis labels and the direction and cause of every shift, since this course cannot draw them -- have the learner sketch each one by hand as it is described, then check their sketch against the description. AQA GCSE Economics requires quantitative skills throughout (at least 10% of all marks): every numerical worked example in this course (elasticities, costs/revenue/profit, economies of scale, gross/net pay, GDP/CPI/unemployment-rate calculations, balance of payments, exchange rates, interest) was computed and checked when this course was built, and the learner should always be made to show full working, not just a final answer, since AQA awards method marks. Real UK institutions (the Bank of England and its Monetary Policy Committee, HMRC, the Office for National Statistics) and real current UK tax/NI thresholds and rates are used for illustrative calculations, clearly labelled as such -- these change periodically, so flag to the learner that the method matters more than memorising the exact current figures, which examiners provide in any real question that needs them.

#### 5.4 The labour market: wage determination, wage differentials, gross and net pay
The **labour market** is where workers (who supply their labour) and firms (who demand labour) interact; like any market, the **wage rate** (the 'price' of labour) is determined by the interaction of the demand for and supply of a particular type of labour. **Demand for labour** comes from firms and depends on how productive/valuable that labour is to them (and on demand for the good/service that labour helps produce); **supply of labour** depends on the number of people willing and able to do that job, which depends on required qualifications/training, how many people have the necessary skills, and the desirability of the job itself. **Wage differentials** (why some jobs pay more than others) arise mainly from imbalances between labour supply and demand: jobs requiring rare, highly-valued skills or lengthy/expensive training (e.g. surgeons) have a low supply relative to demand, pushing wages up; jobs anyone can do with little training (e.g. many retail roles) have abundant supply relative to demand, keeping wages lower; dangerous, unpleasant or unsociable jobs may need to pay more (a 'compensating differential') to attract enough workers. **Gross pay** is a worker's total earnings before any deductions; **net pay** ('take-home pay') is what is actually received after deductions, chiefly **income tax** (paid on earnings above a tax-free personal allowance) and **National Insurance (NI)** contributions. *Worked example (illustrative UK rates):* gross annual pay of £30,000, tax-free personal allowance £12,570, taxed at 20% on the remainder, with NI (employee) taken as 8% of earnings above the same threshold: taxable amount = £30,000 − £12,570 = **£17,430**; income tax = 20% × £17,430 = **£3,486**; NI = 8% × £17,430 = **£1,394.40**; net pay = £30,000 − £3,486 − £1,394.40 = **£25,119.60**.
