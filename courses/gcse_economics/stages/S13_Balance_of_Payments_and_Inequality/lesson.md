# S13_Balance_of_Payments_and_Inequality - Lesson: Balance of payments and income/wealth inequality

## Goal
The learner distinguishes the balance of trade from the balance of payments, calculates and interprets a current account deficit or surplus, explains government policies to influence it, and explains the causes, consequences and possible government responses to income and wealth inequality.

## Syllabus items taught here
- 8.5 - Balance of payments: balance of trade, the current account, surpluses/deficits, and policies
- 8.6 - Distribution of income: income and wealth inequality, causes, consequences and policies

## How to teach this
Ask the learner: if the UK buys more from other countries than it sells to them, where does the extra money to pay for it come from? That gap is a balance of payments deficit. Diagrams (demand/supply curves, shifts, externality diagrams) are described precisely in words, including axis labels and the direction and cause of every shift, since this course cannot draw them -- have the learner sketch each one by hand as it is described, then check their sketch against the description. AQA GCSE Economics requires quantitative skills throughout (at least 10% of all marks): every numerical worked example in this course (elasticities, costs/revenue/profit, economies of scale, gross/net pay, GDP/CPI/unemployment-rate calculations, balance of payments, exchange rates, interest) was computed and checked when this course was built, and the learner should always be made to show full working, not just a final answer, since AQA awards method marks. Real UK institutions (the Bank of England and its Monetary Policy Committee, HMRC, the Office for National Statistics) and real current UK tax/NI thresholds and rates are used for illustrative calculations, clearly labelled as such -- these change periodically, so flag to the learner that the method matters more than memorising the exact current figures, which examiners provide in any real question that needs them.

#### 8.5 Balance of payments: balance of trade, the current account, surpluses/deficits, and policies
The **balance of trade** is the difference between the value of a country's exports of goods and its imports of goods (a subset of the wider balance of payments). The **balance of payments** is a fuller record of all economic transactions between a country and the rest of the world; its **current account** covers trade in goods, trade in services, and other income/transfers. A **current account deficit** exists when the value of imports (goods and services bought from abroad, plus other outflows) exceeds the value of exports (and other inflows) over a period; a **current account surplus** is the reverse. *Worked example:* if UK exports of goods and services total £450bn and imports total £480bn over a period, the current account balance = £450bn − £480bn = **−£30bn**, i.e. a £30bn deficit. Persistent large deficits can be a concern (the country is, in effect, financing the shortfall by borrowing from or selling assets to the rest of the world), though a deficit can also simply reflect strong domestic demand pulling in imports, which is not necessarily harmful in the short run. *Government policies* to influence the balance of payments include supply-side policies to make domestically-produced goods more competitive (raising export potential and reducing reliance on imports), and (less directly, and with side-effects) monetary or exchange-rate policy, since a weaker currency makes exports cheaper and imports dearer.

#### 8.6 Distribution of income: income and wealth inequality, causes, consequences and policies
**Income inequality** describes how unevenly income (what people earn, e.g. wages, profit, rent, interest) is distributed across a population; **wealth inequality** describes how unevenly wealth (the stock of assets someone owns, e.g. property, savings, shares, minus debts) is distributed -- wealth is typically distributed even more unequally than income, partly because wealth compounds over time (existing assets can generate further income and be passed on through inheritance). *Causes* of inequality include differences in wages (reflecting differences in skills, education and bargaining power -- see the labour market, S08), differences in wealth/inheritance, unequal access to education and opportunity, and unemployment. *Consequences* of high inequality can include: for individuals at the bottom, poverty and reduced access to opportunities (a possible cycle where poverty limits education/opportunity, perpetuating inequality); for society, potential social/political instability and lost economic potential if talent among the less well-off goes undeveloped; some argue a degree of inequality can also incentivise effort, education and entrepreneurship, so views differ on the ideal level. *Government policies to redistribute income and wealth* include progressive taxation (S10) which taxes higher earners at a higher rate, welfare benefits/the state pension (transferring income to those with lower incomes), and public spending on services such as free state education and the NHS which benefit lower-income households proportionally more; such redistribution can, however, have consequences of its own, e.g. very high tax rates could in principle reduce the incentive to work or invest.
