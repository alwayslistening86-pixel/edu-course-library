# S07_Market_Structures - Lesson: Market structures: competitive and non-competitive markets

## Goal
The learner identifies the factors that distinguish market structures, and explains the characteristics and consumer/producer impacts of competitive markets and of non-competitive markets (monopoly and oligopoly).

## Syllabus items taught here
- 5.1 - The importance of market structures on producers and consumers
- 5.2 - Competitive markets: characteristics and economic impact
- 5.3 - Non-competitive markets: characteristics, monopoly and oligopoly

## How to teach this
Ask the learner to compare buying a coffee (dozens of nearby cafes to choose from) with getting their household's water supply (one company, no real choice) -- that contrast is market structure. Diagrams (demand/supply curves, shifts, externality diagrams) are described precisely in words, including axis labels and the direction and cause of every shift, since this course cannot draw them -- have the learner sketch each one by hand as it is described, then check their sketch against the description. AQA GCSE Economics requires quantitative skills throughout (at least 10% of all marks): every numerical worked example in this course (elasticities, costs/revenue/profit, economies of scale, gross/net pay, GDP/CPI/unemployment-rate calculations, balance of payments, exchange rates, interest) was computed and checked when this course was built, and the learner should always be made to show full working, not just a final answer, since AQA awards method marks. Real UK institutions (the Bank of England and its Monetary Policy Committee, HMRC, the Office for National Statistics) and real current UK tax/NI thresholds and rates are used for illustrative calculations, clearly labelled as such -- these change periodically, so flag to the learner that the method matters more than memorising the exact current figures, which examiners provide in any real question that needs them.

#### 5.1 The importance of market structures on producers and consumers
A **market structure** describes how competitive or concentrated a market is, and is distinguished chiefly by three factors: the **number of producers/firms** in the market (many, few, or one), the degree of **product differentiation** (whether firms' products are identical/homogeneous, e.g. wheat, or branded and differentiated, e.g. trainers), and the **ease of entry** for new firms (low barriers, e.g. a market stall, versus high barriers, e.g. needing a huge factory or a government licence). These factors together determine how much market power (control over price) individual firms have, which in turn affects the prices and choices available to consumers.

#### 5.2 Competitive markets: characteristics and economic impact
A **competitive market** has many buyers and sellers, broadly similar (homogeneous or close-to-homogeneous) products, and low barriers to entry, so no single firm can significantly influence the market price -- firms are largely **price takers**. Because competition is intense, firms compete on price and quality to attract customers, which tends to keep **prices lower** and give consumers **greater choice**; firms in competitive markets typically earn **lower profits** in the long run (competition and easy entry mean high profits attract new entrants, pushing prices/profits back down), which benefits consumers but can squeeze producers and, in a highly competitive labour market, keep wages down for workers with easily substitutable skills.

#### 5.3 Non-competitive markets: characteristics, monopoly and oligopoly
A **non-competitive market** has few sellers and/or significant barriers to entry, giving remaining firms greater power over price -- they are **price makers** to some degree, which tends to mean **higher prices** and **less choice** for consumers than in a competitive market. A **monopoly** exists where a single firm dominates a market (in UK competition law, a firm with 25% or more of a market can be investigated as having significant market power); a true monopoly can set its own price largely without fear of a rival undercutting it, and can restrict output to keep prices high. An **oligopoly** is a market dominated by a small number of large firms (e.g. UK supermarkets, mobile networks) which are **interdependent** -- each firm's pricing and output decisions must account for how rivals are likely to react, often leading to non-price competition (advertising, loyalty schemes, product features) rather than price wars, since a price cut is easily and quickly matched by rivals.
