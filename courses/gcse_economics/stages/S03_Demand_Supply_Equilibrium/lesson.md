# S03_Demand_Supply_Equilibrium - Lesson: Demand, supply and equilibrium price

## Goal
The learner constructs and reads demand and supply curves, distinguishes a shift of a curve from a movement along it, identifies the factors causing each, and explains how equilibrium price and quantity are determined, including the effects of excess demand and excess supply.

## Syllabus items taught here
- 3.1 - Demand for goods and services: factors, causes of changes, and the demand curve
- 3.2 - Supply of goods and services: factors, causes of changes, and the supply curve
- 3.3 - Equilibrium price

## How to teach this
Ask the learner what happens to the queue outside a shop the day a hyped new phone launches at a price everyone is willing to pay -- that queue (excess demand) is the market signalling that price is too low. Diagrams (demand/supply curves, shifts, externality diagrams) are described precisely in words, including axis labels and the direction and cause of every shift, since this course cannot draw them -- have the learner sketch each one by hand as it is described, then check their sketch against the description. AQA GCSE Economics requires quantitative skills throughout (at least 10% of all marks): every numerical worked example in this course (elasticities, costs/revenue/profit, economies of scale, gross/net pay, GDP/CPI/unemployment-rate calculations, balance of payments, exchange rates, interest) was computed and checked when this course was built, and the learner should always be made to show full working, not just a final answer, since AQA awards method marks. Real UK institutions (the Bank of England and its Monetary Policy Committee, HMRC, the Office for National Statistics) and real current UK tax/NI thresholds and rates are used for illustrative calculations, clearly labelled as such -- these change periodically, so flag to the learner that the method matters more than memorising the exact current figures, which examiners provide in any real question that needs them.

#### 3.1 Demand for goods and services: factors, causes of changes, and the demand curve
**Demand** is the quantity of a good or service that consumers are willing and able to buy at a given price over a given time period. The **demand curve** plots price (vertical axis) against quantity demanded (horizontal axis) and slopes **downwards** from left to right, because as price falls, quantity demanded rises (the **law of demand**). A change in price causes a **movement along** the existing demand curve (an 'extension' as price falls and quantity demanded rises; a 'contraction' as price rises and quantity demanded falls). Non-price factors cause the **whole curve to shift**: income (a rise in income shifts demand for a normal good right/outward, i.e. more is demanded at every price), tastes and fashion, the price of substitute goods (a rise in the price of a substitute, e.g. Pepsi, shifts demand for Coca-Cola right), the price of complementary goods (a rise in the price of a complement, e.g. printers, shifts demand for printer ink left), advertising, and the size/age structure of the population. A rise in demand (from any of these) shifts the curve **right**; a fall shifts it **left**.

#### 3.2 Supply of goods and services: factors, causes of changes, and the supply curve
**Supply** is the quantity of a good or service that producers are willing and able to sell at a given price over a given time period. The **supply curve** plots price against quantity supplied and slopes **upwards** from left to right, because a higher price makes production more profitable, giving firms an incentive to supply more (the **law of supply**). A change in price causes a **movement along** the existing supply curve. Non-price factors cause the **whole curve to shift**: the costs of production (a rise in raw material, wage or energy costs shifts supply left, as production becomes less profitable at every price), the state of technology (an improvement shifts supply right, e.g. automation cutting production costs), the number of producers in the market (more producers shift supply right), taxes and subsidies (a tax on a good shifts its supply left; a subsidy shifts it right), and, for some goods (notably agricultural), the weather. A rise in supply shifts the curve **right**; a fall shifts it **left**.

#### 3.3 Equilibrium price
The **equilibrium price** is the price at which the quantity consumers wish to buy exactly equals the quantity producers wish to sell -- on a supply-and-demand diagram, it is the price (and corresponding quantity) at which the demand and supply curves **intersect**. At any price above equilibrium, quantity supplied exceeds quantity demanded (**excess supply**), so unsold stock builds up and firms cut price until equilibrium is restored; at any price below equilibrium, quantity demanded exceeds quantity supplied (**excess demand**), so firms find they can raise price (as consumers compete for the limited stock) until equilibrium is restored -- the market is self-correcting. *Example:* if a music festival prices tickets below equilibrium, tickets sell out instantly and touts resell them at a higher price (excess demand); if a shop overprices a new product, unsold stock piles up and it eventually runs a sale (excess supply). Total revenue for a producer at the equilibrium point can be shown on the same diagram as the rectangle equilibrium price × equilibrium quantity.
