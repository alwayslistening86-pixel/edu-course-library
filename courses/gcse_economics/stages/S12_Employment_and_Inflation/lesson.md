# S12_Employment_and_Inflation - Lesson: Employment, unemployment, inflation and the CPI

## Goal
The learner explains how employment and unemployment are measured and calculated, identifies types, causes and consequences of unemployment, and government policies to reduce it; explains inflation, how it is measured using the CPI, its causes and consequences, and government policies to manage it.

## Syllabus items taught here
- 8.3 - Employment and unemployment: measurement, types, causes, consequences and policies
- 8.4 - Inflation and price stability: measurement (CPI), causes, consequences and policies

## How to teach this
Ask the learner: if a shipyard closes down because ships are no longer built in that town, is that the same kind of unemployment as a graduate taking a few weeks to find their first job? (No -- structural versus frictional.) Diagrams (demand/supply curves, shifts, externality diagrams) are described precisely in words, including axis labels and the direction and cause of every shift, since this course cannot draw them -- have the learner sketch each one by hand as it is described, then check their sketch against the description. AQA GCSE Economics requires quantitative skills throughout (at least 10% of all marks): every numerical worked example in this course (elasticities, costs/revenue/profit, economies of scale, gross/net pay, GDP/CPI/unemployment-rate calculations, balance of payments, exchange rates, interest) was computed and checked when this course was built, and the learner should always be made to show full working, not just a final answer, since AQA awards method marks. Real UK institutions (the Bank of England and its Monetary Policy Committee, HMRC, the Office for National Statistics) and real current UK tax/NI thresholds and rates are used for illustrative calculations, clearly labelled as such -- these change periodically, so flag to the learner that the method matters more than memorising the exact current figures, which examiners provide in any real question that needs them.

#### 8.3 Employment and unemployment: measurement, types, causes, consequences and policies
A person is counted as **employed** if they are in paid work; **unemployed** if they are of working age, without a job, but actively seeking and available for work (this excludes, e.g., full-time students not seeking work). The **unemployment rate** = (number unemployed ÷ size of the labour force, i.e. employed + unemployed) × 100. *Worked example:* a labour force of 32,000,000 with 1,280,000 unemployed gives an unemployment rate of (1,280,000 ÷ 32,000,000) × 100 = **4%**. Types of unemployment: **structural** (caused by a long-term decline in a particular industry/region, leaving workers' skills no longer in demand, e.g. former coal-mining areas), **seasonal** (caused by the time of year, e.g. ski instructors or agricultural workers out of work off-season), **frictional** (short-term unemployment while people move between jobs, e.g. a graduate taking a few weeks to find their first role), and **cyclical** (caused by a downturn in the overall economic cycle/a recession, when demand for goods and services -- and so for labour -- falls economy-wide). *Consequences* of unemployment include lost income and lower living standards for the unemployed, lost output for the economy, higher government spending on benefits alongside lower tax revenue, and wider social costs (e.g. poorer health, higher crime in badly affected areas). *Policies to reduce unemployment* include: for cyclical unemployment, boosting demand (e.g. lower interest rates, higher government spending); for structural unemployment, retraining schemes and support to help workers gain new, in-demand skills or relocate.

#### 8.4 Inflation and price stability: measurement (CPI), causes, consequences and policies
**Inflation** is a sustained rise in the general (average) level of prices in an economy over time, reducing the purchasing power of a given amount of money; the **rate of inflation** is the percentage change in the general price level, usually measured over a year. The UK's main measure is the **Consumer Price Index (CPI)**, which tracks the changing cost of a representative 'basket' of goods and services typically bought by households, reweighted periodically to reflect actual spending patterns. *Worked example:* if the same basket of goods cost £250 in the base year and £262.50 this year, the CPI this year = (£262.50 ÷ £250) × 100 = **105**, meaning the inflation rate over that period = ((£262.50 − £250) ÷ £250) × 100 = **5%**. Inflation has two broad causes: **demand-pull inflation** (aggregate demand in the economy grows faster than the economy's ability to supply, pulling prices up) and **cost-push inflation** (rising costs of production, e.g. higher wages, energy or raw material prices, push firms to raise prices to protect their margins). *Consequences* of inflation include reduced purchasing power (hardest on those on fixed incomes, e.g. many pensioners), uncertainty that can discourage business investment, a loss of international competitiveness if a country's inflation is higher than its trading partners', and (for some, e.g. borrowers with fixed-rate debts, or those with fast-rising wages) some winners alongside the losers. Government (and the Bank of England, see S14) manage inflation chiefly through **monetary policy** (raising interest rates to cool demand) and can also use fiscal policy (cutting government spending/raising taxes to reduce demand).
