# S01_Economic_Foundations - Lesson: Economic foundations: needs, wants, factors of production and choice

## Goal
The learner distinguishes needs from wants, explains the purpose of economic activity and the three key economic decisions, names the main economic groups, identifies the four factors of production and their rewards, and explains the basic economic problem and opportunity cost.

## Syllabus items taught here
- 1.1 - Economic activity: needs and wants, the central purpose of economic activity, the key economic decisions, the main economic groups
- 1.2 - The factors of production
- 1.3 - Making choices: the basic economic problem, costs and benefits of economic choices including opportunity cost

## How to teach this
Ask the learner to list everything they 'need' to survive a week, then everything they 'want' -- the gap between the two lists is where economics starts. Diagrams (demand/supply curves, shifts, externality diagrams) are described precisely in words, including axis labels and the direction and cause of every shift, since this course cannot draw them -- have the learner sketch each one by hand as it is described, then check their sketch against the description. AQA GCSE Economics requires quantitative skills throughout (at least 10% of all marks): every numerical worked example in this course (elasticities, costs/revenue/profit, economies of scale, gross/net pay, GDP/CPI/unemployment-rate calculations, balance of payments, exchange rates, interest) was computed and checked when this course was built, and the learner should always be made to show full working, not just a final answer, since AQA awards method marks. Real UK institutions (the Bank of England and its Monetary Policy Committee, HMRC, the Office for National Statistics) and real current UK tax/NI thresholds and rates are used for illustrative calculations, clearly labelled as such -- these change periodically, so flag to the learner that the method matters more than memorising the exact current figures, which examiners provide in any real question that needs them.

#### 1.1 Economic activity: needs and wants, the central purpose of economic activity, the key economic decisions, the main economic groups
A **need** is something essential for survival (food, water, shelter, clothing); a **want** is something that improves quality of life but is not essential (a games console, a holiday). Economic activity exists because **wants are unlimited but resources are limited** -- its central purpose is to use scarce resources to produce goods and services that satisfy needs and wants as fully as possible. Every economy must answer three key economic decisions: **what to produce** (which goods/services, given limited resources), **how to produce it** (which combination of land, labour, capital and enterprise), and **for whom to produce** (who receives the goods and services produced, i.e. how output is distributed). The three main economic groups involved are **consumers** (individuals/households who buy and use goods and services), **producers** (firms that organise resources to make goods and services, aiming chiefly at profit), and **government** (which taxes, spends, regulates markets and provides some goods/services itself, e.g. the NHS, state education, defence).

#### 1.2 The factors of production
The **factors of production** are the four categories of resource used to produce goods and services, each earning a distinct reward: **land** (natural resources -- farmland, minerals, oil, water -- earning **rent**), **labour** (the physical and mental human effort used in production -- earning **wages**), **capital** (man-made resources used to produce other goods, e.g. machinery, factories, tools, computers -- earning **interest**, the return on the finance used to buy capital), and **enterprise** (the entrepreneur who takes the risk of combining the other three factors to produce a good or service, organising the business and bearing the risk of loss -- earning **profit**, the reward for that risk-taking). *Example:* a bakery combines land (the shop premises), labour (the bakers), capital (ovens, mixers) and enterprise (the owner who decided to start the business and risks their own money).

#### 1.3 Making choices: the basic economic problem, costs and benefits of economic choices including opportunity cost
The **basic economic problem** is that human wants are unlimited but the resources (factors of production) available to satisfy them are finite (scarce) -- so choices must always be made about what to produce and consume, and some wants will always go unsatisfied. Every choice has a cost beyond its money price: the **opportunity cost** of a decision is the value of the next best alternative given up when a choice is made. *Example:* a government with a fixed budget that spends an extra £2bn on hospitals has an opportunity cost of what that £2bn could instead have bought -- e.g. new schools; a student who spends an evening revising economics has an opportunity cost of the leisure time (e.g. seeing friends) they gave up. Costs and benefits should be weighed against each other before any economic choice: a choice is worthwhile if its benefit exceeds its opportunity cost.
