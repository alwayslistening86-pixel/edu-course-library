# S17_Trade_Agreements_and_Globalisation - Lesson: Free-trade agreements and globalisation

## Goal
The learner explains free trade and free-trade agreements (including the EU), and explains the main features of globalisation and its benefits and drawbacks for producers, workers and consumers in developed and less developed countries.

## Syllabus items taught here
- 10.3 - Free trade and free-trade agreements, including the European Union
- 10.4 - Globalisation: benefits and drawbacks, including moral/ethical/sustainability considerations

## How to teach this
Ask the learner: why might a group of neighbouring countries agree to remove all tariffs between themselves, but still charge tariffs on goods from everywhere else? Diagrams (demand/supply curves, shifts, externality diagrams) are described precisely in words, including axis labels and the direction and cause of every shift, since this course cannot draw them -- have the learner sketch each one by hand as it is described, then check their sketch against the description. AQA GCSE Economics requires quantitative skills throughout (at least 10% of all marks): every numerical worked example in this course (elasticities, costs/revenue/profit, economies of scale, gross/net pay, GDP/CPI/unemployment-rate calculations, balance of payments, exchange rates, interest) was computed and checked when this course was built, and the learner should always be made to show full working, not just a final answer, since AQA awards method marks. Real UK institutions (the Bank of England and its Monetary Policy Committee, HMRC, the Office for National Statistics) and real current UK tax/NI thresholds and rates are used for illustrative calculations, clearly labelled as such -- these change periodically, so flag to the learner that the method matters more than memorising the exact current figures, which examiners provide in any real question that needs them.

#### 10.3 Free trade and free-trade agreements, including the European Union
**Free trade** is trade between countries without artificial barriers such as **tariffs** (taxes on imports), **quotas** (limits on the quantity that can be imported) or other restrictions -- economists generally argue it raises global efficiency and living standards by letting countries fully exploit specialisation, though it can create losers as well as winners within a country (e.g. domestic firms/workers who cannot compete with cheaper imports). A **free-trade agreement** is a formal agreement between two or more countries to reduce or remove trade barriers between themselves (while, often, keeping barriers against non-members) -- the **European Union (EU)** is a major example, historically operating as a **single market** with free movement of goods, services, capital and people between member states, and a **customs union** with a common external tariff on goods from outside the EU. The significance of such agreements is that member countries can trade with each other more cheaply and easily than with non-members, boosting trade between them, though the UK's own relationship with the EU changed significantly after its 2020 withdrawal (Brexit), and the UK has since negotiated separate trade agreements with the EU and other countries.

#### 10.4 Globalisation: benefits and drawbacks, including moral/ethical/sustainability considerations
**Globalisation** is the increasing economic integration and interdependence of countries around the world, driven mainly by improving **technology** (faster, cheaper transport and communication, e.g. shipping, the internet) and the growth of **multinational companies** (firms operating and investing across many countries) that shift production to wherever costs are lowest and coordinate global supply chains. *Benefits/drawbacks for developed countries (e.g. the UK)*: consumers benefit from a wider choice of goods and generally lower prices (goods often made more cheaply abroad); but some domestic workers/industries that cannot compete with lower-cost overseas production can lose jobs (structural unemployment, S12), and producers face more intense global competition. *Benefits/drawbacks for less developed countries*: globalisation can bring foreign investment, new jobs and technology transfer, potentially raising incomes and reducing poverty; but drawbacks can include worker exploitation (low wages, poor working conditions in some cases), environmental damage from rapidly industrialising, and a risk that the benefits are captured mainly by multinational companies rather than local workers/communities. These impacts raise **moral, ethical and sustainability considerations** -- e.g. whether governments and multinational companies have a responsibility to ensure globalisation's gains are shared fairly and its environmental costs are minimised, not simply to maximise efficiency/profit.
