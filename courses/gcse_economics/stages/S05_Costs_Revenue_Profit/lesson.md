# S05_Costs_Revenue_Profit - Lesson: Cost, revenue and profit for producers

## Goal
The learner explains firms' business objectives, distinguishes fixed from variable costs and calculates total and average costs, calculates total revenue and profit, and considers moral and ethical implications of pricing and cost decisions.

## Syllabus items taught here
- 4.1 - The importance of cost, revenue and profit for producers, including business objectives and moral/ethical considerations

## How to teach this
Ask the learner: does a business always simply try to make as much profit as possible? Sometimes survival, growing market share, or being seen as ethical matter more -- ask them why. Diagrams (demand/supply curves, shifts, externality diagrams) are described precisely in words, including axis labels and the direction and cause of every shift, since this course cannot draw them -- have the learner sketch each one by hand as it is described, then check their sketch against the description. AQA GCSE Economics requires quantitative skills throughout (at least 10% of all marks): every numerical worked example in this course (elasticities, costs/revenue/profit, economies of scale, gross/net pay, GDP/CPI/unemployment-rate calculations, balance of payments, exchange rates, interest) was computed and checked when this course was built, and the learner should always be made to show full working, not just a final answer, since AQA awards method marks. Real UK institutions (the Bank of England and its Monetary Policy Committee, HMRC, the Office for National Statistics) and real current UK tax/NI thresholds and rates are used for illustrative calculations, clearly labelled as such -- these change periodically, so flag to the learner that the method matters more than memorising the exact current figures, which examiners provide in any real question that needs them.

#### 4.1 The importance of cost, revenue and profit for producers, including business objectives and moral/ethical considerations
Firms can pursue different **business objectives**: **profit maximisation** (the traditional assumption -- making the largest possible profit), **growth** (increasing market share or size, sometimes even at the expense of short-term profit), and **survival** (especially for new or struggling firms, simply staying in business, e.g. in a recession or against intense competition). **Costs** are split into **fixed costs** (do not change with the level of output in the short run, e.g. rent, business rates, insurance, management salaries -- must be paid even if output is zero) and **variable costs** (change directly with the level of output, e.g. raw materials, piece-rate wages, packaging -- rise as more is produced). **Total cost (TC) = total fixed costs (TFC) + total variable costs (TVC)**; **average cost (AC) = total cost ÷ quantity produced**. **Total revenue (TR) = price × quantity sold**. **Profit = total revenue − total cost**; a firm makes a loss if TC exceeds TR. A higher selling price, all else equal, gives firms an incentive to expand production (more profit per unit, assuming demand allows the extra output to be sold). *Worked example:* a firm sells 500 units at £8 each (TR = 500 × £8 = **£4,000**); fixed costs are £1,500 and variable costs are £3.60 per unit (TVC = 500 × £3.60 = £1,800, so TC = £1,500 + £1,800 = **£3,300**); average cost = £3,300 ÷ 500 = **£6.60**; profit = £4,000 − £3,300 = **£700**. Pricing and cost decisions also raise **moral and ethical considerations**: e.g. a firm could cut costs by underpaying workers or using cheaper but more polluting materials, or could exploit inelastic demand (e.g. for a life-saving drug) by charging very high prices -- firms increasingly weigh reputational and ethical concerns, not profit alone, when setting prices and controlling costs.
