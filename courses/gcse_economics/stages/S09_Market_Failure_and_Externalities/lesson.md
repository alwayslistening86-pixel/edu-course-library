# S09_Market_Failure_and_Externalities - Lesson: Market failure and externalities

## Goal
The learner explains what market failure is, the implications of a misallocation of resources, and how government can intervene; distinguishes private from social costs and benefits, and positive from negative externalities in production and consumption.

## Syllabus items taught here
- 6.1 - Misallocation of resources: market failure and government intervention
- 6.2 - Externalities: defining externalities, positive and negative

## How to teach this
Ask the learner why a factory that pollutes a river doesn't pay for the damage it causes to people downstream -- that gap between what the factory pays and the true cost to society is market failure in action. Diagrams (demand/supply curves, shifts, externality diagrams) are described precisely in words, including axis labels and the direction and cause of every shift, since this course cannot draw them -- have the learner sketch each one by hand as it is described, then check their sketch against the description. AQA GCSE Economics requires quantitative skills throughout (at least 10% of all marks): every numerical worked example in this course (elasticities, costs/revenue/profit, economies of scale, gross/net pay, GDP/CPI/unemployment-rate calculations, balance of payments, exchange rates, interest) was computed and checked when this course was built, and the learner should always be made to show full working, not just a final answer, since AQA awards method marks. Real UK institutions (the Bank of England and its Monetary Policy Committee, HMRC, the Office for National Statistics) and real current UK tax/NI thresholds and rates are used for illustrative calculations, clearly labelled as such -- these change periodically, so flag to the learner that the method matters more than memorising the exact current figures, which examiners provide in any real question that needs them.

#### 6.1 Misallocation of resources: market failure and government intervention
**Market failure** is the inability of the free market to allocate resources efficiently -- either too much or too little of a good is produced/consumed relative to what would be best for society as a whole. **Implications** of this misallocation include: goods with harmful side-effects (e.g. pollution) being **over-produced/over-consumed** relative to the socially optimal level, and goods with beneficial side-effects (e.g. education, vaccination) being **under-produced/under-consumed**, because the free market only reflects the costs and benefits to the buyer and seller directly involved, ignoring effects on third parties. Because of this, **government intervention** is often used to correct market failure: taxes on goods that cause harm (to raise their price and discourage consumption, e.g. tobacco duty), subsidies for goods that bring wider benefit (to lower their price and encourage consumption, e.g. subsidised public transport or renewable energy), regulation (laws limiting or banning harmful activity, e.g. emissions limits), and direct government provision of some goods and services (e.g. state education, the NHS) where the market alone would under-provide.

#### 6.2 Externalities: defining externalities, positive and negative
An **externality** is a cost or benefit of an economic activity that falls on a third party who was not directly involved in the transaction -- i.e. the **difference between the social cost/benefit** (the total cost/benefit to society as a whole) **and the private cost/benefit** (the cost/benefit only to the buyer and seller directly involved). A **negative externality** exists where social cost exceeds private cost -- a third party bears a cost that the market price does not reflect. *Negative production externality:* a factory's air or water pollution harms nearby residents' health, but the factory does not pay for that harm, so the good is priced too low and over-produced relative to the social optimum. *Negative consumption externality:* second-hand smoke from a smoker harms bystanders' health, a cost the smoker does not pay for. A **positive externality** exists where social benefit exceeds private benefit -- a third party gains a benefit the market price does not reward. *Positive production externality:* a firm's staff training raises the wider skill level of the local workforce (other future employers benefit too), beyond the benefit to the firm itself. *Positive consumption externality:* an individual getting vaccinated also reduces the risk of others around them catching the disease (herd immunity), a benefit beyond the person vaccinated.
