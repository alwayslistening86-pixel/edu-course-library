# S04_Elasticity_and_Intermarket - Lesson: Intermarket relationships and price elasticity of demand and supply

## Goal
The learner explains complementary and substitute goods and how a change in one market affects another, calculates and interprets price elasticity of demand and of supply, and explains the factors that make demand or supply more or less elastic.

## Syllabus items taught here
- 3.4 - Intermarket relationships: complements and substitutes
- 3.5 - Price elasticity of demand: meaning, factors and measurement
- 3.6 - Price elasticity of supply: meaning, factors and measurement

## How to teach this
Ask the learner: if the price of coffee doubled overnight, would they buy roughly the same amount, or a lot less? Their answer is exactly what price elasticity of demand measures. Diagrams (demand/supply curves, shifts, externality diagrams) are described precisely in words, including axis labels and the direction and cause of every shift, since this course cannot draw them -- have the learner sketch each one by hand as it is described, then check their sketch against the description. AQA GCSE Economics requires quantitative skills throughout (at least 10% of all marks): every numerical worked example in this course (elasticities, costs/revenue/profit, economies of scale, gross/net pay, GDP/CPI/unemployment-rate calculations, balance of payments, exchange rates, interest) was computed and checked when this course was built, and the learner should always be made to show full working, not just a final answer, since AQA awards method marks. Real UK institutions (the Bank of England and its Monetary Policy Committee, HMRC, the Office for National Statistics) and real current UK tax/NI thresholds and rates are used for illustrative calculations, clearly labelled as such -- these change periodically, so flag to the learner that the method matters more than memorising the exact current figures, which examiners provide in any real question that needs them.

#### 3.4 Intermarket relationships: complements and substitutes
**Substitutes** are goods that can replace one another in use (Coca-Cola and Pepsi; butter and margarine): a rise in the price of one causes consumers to switch to the other, so demand for the substitute **rises** (the demand curve for the substitute shifts right). **Complements** are goods usually consumed together (printers and printer ink; cars and petrol): a rise in the price of one makes the pair less attractive together, so demand for the complement **falls** (its demand curve shifts left). Because markets are interconnected, a change in one market can spread to related markets: *example*, a sharp rise in the price of petrol (due to, say, a rise in oil prices) will reduce demand for petrol-guzzling cars (a complement, demand shifts left) while increasing demand for electric cars and public transport (substitutes, demand shifts right).

#### 3.5 Price elasticity of demand: meaning, factors and measurement
**Price elasticity of demand (PED)** measures how responsive quantity demanded is to a change in price: PED = percentage change in quantity demanded ÷ percentage change in price. Because demand curves slope downwards, PED is negative, but it is usually discussed by its size (magnitude), ignoring the sign. If PED's magnitude is greater than 1, demand is **elastic** (quantity demanded changes proportionally more than price -- a price rise sharply cuts quantity demanded, so total revenue falls when price rises); if less than 1, demand is **inelastic** (quantity demanded changes proportionally less than price -- total revenue rises when price rises). Demand tends to be more **elastic** when: close substitutes are easily available, the good is a luxury (not a necessity), the good takes up a large share of consumer income, and there is more time for consumers to find alternatives. Demand tends to be more **inelastic** for necessities, goods with few substitutes (e.g. insulin, petrol in the short run), and goods that take up only a small share of income. *Worked example:* a 10% rise in price causes a 5% fall in quantity demanded: PED = -5% ÷ 10% = -0.5, so demand is price inelastic (magnitude 0.5 < 1) -- because demand is inelastic, this price rise would increase the firm's total revenue.

#### 3.6 Price elasticity of supply: meaning, factors and measurement
**Price elasticity of supply (PES)** measures how responsive quantity supplied is to a change in price: PES = percentage change in quantity supplied ÷ percentage change in price. PES is positive (supply curves slope upward). If PES's magnitude is greater than 1, supply is **elastic** (producers can expand output substantially when price rises); if less than 1, supply is **inelastic** (output cannot easily be increased even if price rises). Supply tends to be more **elastic** when firms have spare capacity, stocks can easily be built up/drawn down, and there is more time to respond (in the long run, firms can build new factories or enter the market); supply tends to be more **inelastic** in the short run, when a good is perishable or cannot be stored, and when production takes a long time to increase (e.g. agricultural goods, limited by the growing season). *Worked example:* a 20% rise in price leads to a 30% rise in quantity supplied: PES = 30% ÷ 20% = 1.5, so supply is elastic. Firms with elastic PED benefit from cutting price to raise revenue; firms facing inelastic PED benefit from raising price, but firms facing elastic PES can respond fastest to any such price change.
