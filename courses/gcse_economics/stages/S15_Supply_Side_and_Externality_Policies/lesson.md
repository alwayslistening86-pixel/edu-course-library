# S15_Supply_Side_and_Externality_Policies - Lesson: Supply-side policies and correcting externalities

## Goal
The learner explains supply-side policies and evaluates their advantages and disadvantages, and explains government policies used to correct positive and negative externalities.

## Syllabus items taught here
- 9.3 - Supply-side policies
- 9.4 - Policies to correct positive and negative externalities

## How to teach this
Ask the learner: instead of just managing how much people spend (demand), could a government instead try to make the whole economy more productive, so it can supply more without prices rising? That is the idea behind supply-side policy. Diagrams (demand/supply curves, shifts, externality diagrams) are described precisely in words, including axis labels and the direction and cause of every shift, since this course cannot draw them -- have the learner sketch each one by hand as it is described, then check their sketch against the description. AQA GCSE Economics requires quantitative skills throughout (at least 10% of all marks): every numerical worked example in this course (elasticities, costs/revenue/profit, economies of scale, gross/net pay, GDP/CPI/unemployment-rate calculations, balance of payments, exchange rates, interest) was computed and checked when this course was built, and the learner should always be made to show full working, not just a final answer, since AQA awards method marks. Real UK institutions (the Bank of England and its Monetary Policy Committee, HMRC, the Office for National Statistics) and real current UK tax/NI thresholds and rates are used for illustrative calculations, clearly labelled as such -- these change periodically, so flag to the learner that the method matters more than memorising the exact current figures, which examiners provide in any real question that needs them.

#### 9.3 Supply-side policies
**Supply-side policies** aim to increase the economy's productive potential (the total amount it is capable of supplying) rather than managing demand directly, so that the economy can grow without generating inflation. Examples include: **investment in education and training** (raising workers' skills and productivity -- advantage: improves long-run competitiveness and growth potential; disadvantage: expensive, and benefits take years to appear); **lower direct taxes** (e.g. income tax, to increase the incentive to work and encourage enterprise -- advantage: can boost effort/investment; disadvantage: reduces government tax revenue in the short term, and evidence on how strongly people respond is mixed); **reducing tax on business profits** (encouraging investment and enterprise -- advantage: can attract investment/business activity; disadvantage: reduces government revenue and could increase inequality if gains mainly benefit business owners); **trade union reform** (limiting unions' power to restrict labour market flexibility -- advantage: can make labour markets more flexible/responsive; disadvantage: can weaken workers' bargaining power/protections); and **privatisation and deregulation** (transferring state-owned businesses to the private sector and removing rules seen as restricting competition -- advantage: increased competition can raise efficiency and lower prices; disadvantage: private firms may prioritise profit over public interest, e.g. under-investing in an essential service, and reduced regulation can allow greater negative externalities).

#### 9.4 Policies to correct positive and negative externalities
Government uses several tools to correct **externalities** (introduced in Topic 6, S09) at a national policy level. For **negative externalities** (where social cost exceeds private cost, e.g. carbon emissions, congestion): **taxation** (e.g. fuel duty, a carbon tax) raises the price of the harmful activity so it better reflects its true social cost, discouraging it towards the socially optimal level; **regulation** (e.g. emissions limits, congestion charges, bans) directly restricts or prohibits the harmful activity. For **positive externalities** (where social benefit exceeds private benefit, e.g. education, vaccination, renewable energy): **subsidies** lower the price/cost of the beneficial activity, encouraging more of it towards the socially optimal level; **direct government provision** (e.g. free state education, NHS vaccinations) ensures a good with wide social benefit is available regardless of ability/willingness to pay the full private cost. Choosing the right size of tax or subsidy is difficult in practice (the true scale of an externality is hard to measure precisely), and policies have their own costs (administration, enforcement, possible unintended consequences), which government must weigh against the benefit of correcting the original market failure.
