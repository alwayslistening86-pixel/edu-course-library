# S11_Growth_and_Government_Objectives - Lesson: Government economic objectives and economic growth

## Goal
The learner identifies the government's main economic objectives, explains conflicts between them and other objectives (inequality, environment), and explains the significance, causes, costs and benefits of economic growth, distinguishing GDP, real GDP and GDP per capita.

## Syllabus items taught here
- 8.1 - Economic objectives of the government, conflicts between them, and other objectives
- 8.2 - Economic growth: significance, causes, costs, benefits and policies, GDP and GDP per capita

## How to teach this
Ask the learner: could a government cutting unemployment to almost zero by hugely overheating the economy also risk causing high inflation? That tension is exactly why government objectives can conflict. Diagrams (demand/supply curves, shifts, externality diagrams) are described precisely in words, including axis labels and the direction and cause of every shift, since this course cannot draw them -- have the learner sketch each one by hand as it is described, then check their sketch against the description. AQA GCSE Economics requires quantitative skills throughout (at least 10% of all marks): every numerical worked example in this course (elasticities, costs/revenue/profit, economies of scale, gross/net pay, GDP/CPI/unemployment-rate calculations, balance of payments, exchange rates, interest) was computed and checked when this course was built, and the learner should always be made to show full working, not just a final answer, since AQA awards method marks. Real UK institutions (the Bank of England and its Monetary Policy Committee, HMRC, the Office for National Statistics) and real current UK tax/NI thresholds and rates are used for illustrative calculations, clearly labelled as such -- these change periodically, so flag to the learner that the method matters more than memorising the exact current figures, which examiners provide in any real question that needs them.

#### 8.1 Economic objectives of the government, conflicts between them, and other objectives
UK governments generally pursue four main **economic objectives**: **full employment** (keeping unemployment as low as practically possible), **price stability** (keeping inflation low and stable, not zero), **economic growth** (a sustained rise in the economy's output over time), and a **healthy balance of payments** (broadly, export earnings not persistently falling far short of import spending). These objectives can **conflict**: e.g. policies that boost growth and cut unemployment (such as low interest rates encouraging spending) can also push up inflation as demand outstrips supply; and policies to control inflation (raising interest rates) can slow growth and raise unemployment. Governments also pursue **other objectives**, including reducing income/wealth **inequality** and protecting the **environment**/pursuing **sustainability** -- which can themselves conflict with growth (e.g. tighter environmental regulation may raise firms' costs and slow growth in the short term), requiring governments to make **moral, ethical and sustainability** judgements about which objectives to prioritise.

#### 8.2 Economic growth: significance, causes, costs, benefits and policies, GDP and GDP per capita
**Economic growth** is a sustained increase in the value of goods and services an economy produces over time, most commonly measured by the percentage change in **Gross Domestic Product (GDP)** (the total value of output produced within a country in a given period). **Real GDP** adjusts nominal (unadjusted) GDP for the effects of inflation, so it measures the true change in the volume of output, not just a rise in prices; **GDP per capita** divides GDP by population, giving a rough measure of average output (and living standards) per person, which can differ from total GDP growth if population is also changing. Growth matters because it tends to raise average living standards, create jobs, and raise government tax revenue (funding more public spending) without needing higher tax rates. *Causes* of growth include rising quantity/quality of factors of production (more workers, more capital investment, improving technology) and rising productivity. *Benefits*: higher incomes/living standards, more jobs, higher tax revenue. *Costs*: growth can bring inflationary pressure, greater inequality if gains are unevenly shared, and environmental damage/resource depletion. Government policies to promote growth include cutting interest rates, tax cuts to boost spending, and supply-side policies (investment in education, infrastructure -- see S15). *Worked example:* nominal GDP rises from £2,000bn to £2,100bn (a nominal rise of 5%), but prices over the same period rose by 3% (inflation); real GDP in year 2 = £2,100bn ÷ 1.03 ≈ **£2,038.8bn**, so real growth ≈ (£2,038.8bn − £2,000bn) ÷ £2,000bn × 100 ≈ **1.9%** -- much lower than the 5% nominal figure suggests, because part of the nominal rise was just higher prices, not more actual output.
