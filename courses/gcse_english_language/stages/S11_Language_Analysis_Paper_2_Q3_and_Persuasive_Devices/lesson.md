# S11_Language_Analysis_Paper_2_Q3_and_Persuasive_Devices - Lesson: Language on Paper 2: Question 3 (12 marks) and rhetorical devices

## Goal
The learner can write a sustained 12-mark language analysis of a non-fiction or literary non-fiction source and recognise persuasive devices.

## Syllabus items taught here
- P2.Q3 - Paper 2 Question 3: language analysis of one source (12 marks, AO2): write a sustained language analysis in twelve marks
- T.9 - Rhetorical and persuasive devices: recognise and evaluate persuasive techniques

## How to teach this
Show a 60-word persuasive appeal (charity advert). Ask: 'Who is this writer trying to move, and which words do the moving?' Point out that the same devices work in fiction and non-fiction; the difference is what they are trying to make the reader do or believe.

#### P2.Q3 Paper 2 Question 3: language analysis of one source (12 marks, AO2)
The question directs you to one source and a line range ('lines 29 to 38'): 'How does the writer use language to describe the rain and the storm?'. It is worth 12 marks, marked in four bands (1-3, 4-6, 7-9, 10-12) using the same skills as Paper 1 Q2 but with a bigger range: Level 4 'detailed and perceptive understanding' with a judicious range of detail and sophisticated terminology. Aim for about 12 to 15 minutes and four or five developed points. Stay in the given lines and about the right source: the marker note says writing about the wrong source or lines is placed at the bottom of the appropriate level. Cover words and phrases, imagery, sentence forms, and be sure to state the effect each time. Non-fiction may make ideas, attitudes and persuasion the focus (for example, how the writer conveys outrage) so link language to viewpoint, not just description.

#### T.9 Rhetorical and persuasive devices
Recognise and evaluate: rhetorical question (a question with no expected answer, drawing the reader into agreement); antithesis (opposites in balanced phrases: 'ask not what... but what...'); parenthesis (extra information in brackets or dashes, adding an aside or personal voice); triple/list of three (rhythm and completeness); repetition (emphasis, obsession); direct address ('you') to involve the reader; emotive language (words chosen to stir feelings); hyperbole; anecdote; statistics and expert opinion for credibility; imperatives that command; inclusive pronouns ('we') to build unity; irony and sarcasm; and shifts in tone. Evaluate rather than list: 'The triple "cold, hungry and forgotten" builds a rhythm of increasing hopelessness, appealing to the reader's compassion.' The same devices are on the list of what you should use in Q5 (stage S22).

## Explicitly not here
Comparing sources (S15), summarising differences (S09), creating your own persuasive writing (S21-S22).
