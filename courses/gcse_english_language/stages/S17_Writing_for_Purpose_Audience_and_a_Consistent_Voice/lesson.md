# S17_Writing_for_Purpose_Audience_and_a_Consistent_Voice - Lesson: Adapting writing to purpose, audience and form; keeping one voice throughout

## Goal
The learner can choose language, form and structure to fit a set purpose and audience, and can sustain one consistent viewpoint and register across a whole response.

## Syllabus items taught here
- 3.1.2.1.1 - Writing for different purposes and audiences: describe, narrate, explain, instruct, inform, argue: adapt writing to the purpose and audience the task sets
- 3.1.2.1.2 - Selecting vocabulary, grammar, form, structure and organisation to reflect audience, purpose and context: make deliberate language and structure choices for a reader
- 3.1.2.1.5 - Maintaining a consistent point of view: keep one voice, perspective and stance throughout a text
- 3.1.2.1.6 - Maintaining coherence and consistency across a text: make ideas, tense, tone and register hold together from start to finish
- AO5a - AO5 (first strand): communicate clearly, effectively and imaginatively, adapting tone, style and register: match tone, style and register to form, purpose and audience
- AO5b - AO5 (second strand): organise information and ideas with structural and grammatical features for coherence and cohesion: organise a text with paragraphs, discourse markers and cohesive devices

## How to teach this
Give the learner one situation (a school car park is being turned into a garden) and ask them to write two openings: one as a letter to a headteacher, one as a diary entry for a friend. Compare word choice, sentence length and tone side by side, then ask: what changed, and what would break the illusion if it changed halfway through?

#### 3.1.2.1.1 Writing for different purposes and audiences: describe, narrate, explain, instruct, inform, argue
Every writing task in the spec asks for one of six purposes: describe (build a picture with the senses), narrate (tell a sequence of events), explain (make something clear and logical), instruct (give steps to follow), inform (state facts neutrally), argue (persuade towards a view). Purpose decides everything else: a description slows down and lingers on detail; an argument moves fast between points and uses rhetorical push. Audience decides tone and vocabulary: a letter to an MP is formal and measured; a blog post for teenagers can be informal and direct. Teach the learner to underline the purpose verb and the named audience in any task before planning a single sentence, because examiners reward writing that visibly fits both.

#### 3.1.2.1.2 Selecting vocabulary, grammar, form, structure and organisation to reflect audience, purpose and context
Beyond picking a purpose, strong writing makes deliberate small choices that consistently reflect it: short simple sentences for urgency or a young audience, subordinate clauses and formal connectives (furthermore, consequently) for a mature persuasive register, technical or field-specific vocabulary for an informed audience, and colloquial contractions only where the context invites them (a friendly email, not a formal report). Form and structure follow the same logic: a leaflet uses headings and bullet points; a speech uses direct address ('you', rhetorical questions) and signposted sections; a narrative uses paragraphing to control pace. Teach the learner to make one deliberate choice per paragraph and be able to justify it in one sentence: 'I used a rhetorical question here because the audience is sceptical and needs to be drawn in.'

#### 3.1.2.1.5 Maintaining a consistent point of view
A point of view is the stance the writer takes and the person/tense it is delivered in: first-person committed argument, third-person report, a narrator's fixed perspective in a story. Once chosen, it must not drift: an argument that starts personal ('I believe') should not slide into a detached 'one might say' unless that is a deliberate rhetorical shift; a narrative told through one character's eyes should not suddenly reveal what another character is secretly thinking. Examiners penalise inconsistency because it breaks the illusion of control. Teach the learner to decide the point of view before drafting and to reread their first line before every new paragraph to check they are still speaking from the same place.

#### 3.1.2.1.6 Maintaining coherence and consistency across a text
Coherence is different from correctness: it is whether the whole text hangs together as one thing. It depends on a stable tense (do not drift from past to present without reason), a stable tone (do not turn suddenly comic in a serious argument), and cohesive links between paragraphs (discourse markers such as however, in addition, as a result; pronouns that clearly refer back; deliberate repetition of a key word or image). A text can be technically accurate sentence by sentence and still fail on coherence if paragraphs do not connect. Teach the learner to read their own draft aloud, or trace the tense and the pronoun 'it'/'they' through it, to catch drift before it is submitted.

#### AO5a AO5 (first strand): communicate clearly, effectively and imaginatively, adapting tone, style and register
This is the 'content' half of the AO5 grid (worth up to 24 marks split with AO5b's organisation strand). It rewards writing that gets its meaning across clearly, holds the reader's interest, and matches tone, style and register precisely to the task's stated purpose, form and audience -- imaginative for a description, urgent and personal for a persuasive letter, measured for a report. Markers are looking for genuine adaptation, not a single default voice reused for every task. Teach the learner that AO5a is judged by asking 'does this sound like the right person writing to the right reader for the right reason?'

#### AO5b AO5 (second strand): organise information and ideas with structural and grammatical features for coherence and cohesion
The other half of the same 24-mark grid: whether the response is planned and organised, not a stream of consciousness. Markers look for paragraphing that reflects a plan (not just length-based breaks), a clear opening and closing, deliberate structural features (a memorable final image, a returning motif, escalating stakes), and grammatical cohesion devices (connectives, pronoun reference, ellipsis) that link sentences and paragraphs together. Teach the learner to spend the first two minutes of any 40-mark writing task on a four-point plan, because a visible plan is what turns AO5b marks from 'some attempt' into 'controlled'.

## Explicitly not here
Imaginative and transformational writing craft, the Paper 1 task and Question 5 (S18); writing for impact and rhetorical devices (S19); technical accuracy under AO6 (S20-S21).
