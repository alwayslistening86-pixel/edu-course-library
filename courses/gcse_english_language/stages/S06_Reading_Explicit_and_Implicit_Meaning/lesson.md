# S06_Reading_Explicit_and_Implicit_Meaning - Lesson: Reading closely: explicit and implicit information, themes and inference

## Goal
The learner can find what a text says, work out what it implies, identify its main ideas and justify each claim with selected evidence.

## Syllabus items taught here
- 3.1.1.1 - Identifying and interpreting themes, ideas and information: find and explain the ideas a text is built around
- 3.1.1.3 - Drawing inferences and justifying them with evidence: infer implicit meaning and prove it with the text
- 3.1.1.4 - Supporting a point of view with evidence from the text: back every claim with selected, relevant textual detail
- AO1a - AO1 (first strand): identify and interpret explicit and implicit information and ideas: separate what is stated from what is implied and interpret both

## How to teach this
Show the learner a single sentence: 'By the third day, the fridge held nothing but a jar of mustard.' Ask: what is stated, and what else do you know? Draw out 'stated: the fridge is nearly empty; implied: money or time is short, the household is struggling, the speaker is perhaps neglected'. Let them own the difference before you name it.

#### 3.1.1.1 Identifying and interpreting themes, ideas and information
A theme is an idea a text keeps returning to (loneliness, ambition, nature versus progress, class, freedom). Information is what the text tells you about people, places, events and attitudes. Identify by asking: what is this text about, and what is it really about? A passage 'about' a walk to school may be really about anxiety. Interpretation means explaining what an idea or piece of information suggests, not just repeating it. Teach a routine: read once for the gist; then jot the two or three ideas that recur; then choose the evidence for each. When asked to identify a theme, name it in a phrase, not a word: 'the pressure to succeed' beats 'pressure'. On Paper 2, themes are the link between the two sources (holiday, education, the countryside and so on), and the writer's attitude to that theme matters most.

#### 3.1.1.3 Drawing inferences and justifying them with evidence
An inference is a reasonable conclusion drawn from clues in the text. Explicit information is stated directly ('She was twelve'); implicit information is suggested through detail, word choice, contrast and silence. Formula for every inference: 'The text says X [short quotation], which suggests Y because Z.' Example: 'The narrator "kept his coat on" in the house, which suggests he feels like a visitor there.' Good inferences are supported by the text (not invented), and can be more than one (if two are plausible, say so). Poor inferences are just paraphrase ('the coat was on') or speculation not anchored in the wording. On Paper 2 Q2, Level 4 requires 'perceptive inferences from both texts'; on Q1 short questions, however, the answers are usually explicit facts, so read carefully and do not over-infer.

#### 3.1.1.4 Supporting a point of view with evidence from the text
Any claim about a text must be supported with reference: a short quotation (best), a paraphrase, or a precise reference to an event. Select evidence that proves your point rather than the first thing you find; the mark scheme repeatedly rewards 'judicious' (well-chosen) detail. Keep quotations short (a word, phrase or clause), embed them in your own sentence ('the phrase "no time to spare" makes the day sound hurried'), and follow every quotation with comment. Errors: copying out whole sentences, quoting without explaining, or using evidence from outside the given lines (this drops a response down a level). Practise by giving the learner a claim ('The narrator is nervous') and asking them to find the best two words in the passage that prove it and to explain why those beat the others.

#### AO1a AO1 (first strand): identify and interpret explicit and implicit information and ideas
This is the first bullet of AO1: 'identify and interpret explicit and implicit information and ideas'. It carries 10% of the whole GCSE together with the second strand (AO1b, stage S09). It is assessed by Q1 on each paper (4 marks each) and, with synthesis, Q2 on Paper 2 (8 marks). It appears in the whole reading process, because everything you analyse begins with understanding what is on the page. Teach the routine: Read the question focus and line range first; read the lines slowly; decide what is stated; decide what is suggested; select evidence; write a clear point. Remind the learner that on Q1 tasks 'interpret' means reading the statement correctly: a statement that is nearly true but changes one detail (a city instead of a village) is false.

## Explicitly not here
