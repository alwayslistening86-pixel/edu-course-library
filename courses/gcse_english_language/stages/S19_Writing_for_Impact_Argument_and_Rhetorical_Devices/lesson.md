# S19_Writing_for_Impact_Argument_and_Rhetorical_Devices - Lesson: Writing for impact: selecting and emphasising ideas, citing evidence, rhetorical devices and argument craft

## Goal
The learner can build a persuasive, structured argument that selects and emphasises its strongest points, cites evidence pertinently, creates emotional impact and uses rhetorical devices accurately and sparingly.

## Syllabus items taught here
- 3.1.2.2.1 - Writing for impact: selecting, organising and emphasising facts, ideas and key points: arrange content so the strongest points land
- 3.1.2.2.2 - Citing evidence and quotation effectively and pertinently: support views with well-chosen facts, quotations and examples
- 3.1.2.2.3 - Creating emotional impact: shape the reader's feelings deliberately
- 3.1.2.2.4 - Using language creatively, imaginatively and persuasively: use persuasive and inventive language for effect
- 3.1.2.2.5 - Rhetorical devices: rhetorical questions, antithesis, parenthesis and more: use rhetorical devices accurately and sparingly for effect
- W.A - Argument and viewpoint writing craft: construct a convincing, structured argument

## How to teach this
Give the learner a weak, flat opinion sentence ('School should start later, I think that would be good') and ask them to rewrite it three ways for maximum impact: as a rhetorical question, as a stark statistic-style claim, and as a short, blunt three-word sentence. Compare the effect of each.

#### 3.1.2.2.1 Writing for impact: selecting, organising and emphasising facts, ideas and key points
Impact is about ordering, not just content: the strongest point usually opens or closes a piece (primacy and recency), weaker or supporting points sit in the middle, and each paragraph should make one point land clearly rather than several points blur together. Teach 'point-emphasis-move on': state the point plainly, give it one vivid example or piece of evidence, then move to the next point rather than over-explaining. Emphasis techniques include short sentences after long ones, a single-sentence paragraph for a key claim, and placing the most shocking or memorable fact last in a list of three.

#### 3.1.2.2.2 Citing evidence and quotation effectively and pertinently
Effective persuasive writing does not invent statistics wholesale but signals authority: 'research suggests', 'a recent survey found', 'as most people who have experienced this know'. Pertinent evidence is specific and directly supports the point being made, not vague or tangential. Teach the pattern: claim, then a concrete supporting detail (a statistic-style figure, a quoted-style example, an anecdote), then the significance ('this shows that...'). Warn against evidence-dumping several facts with no connective analysis -- one well-used piece of evidence, explained, beats three unexplained ones.

#### 3.1.2.2.3 Creating emotional impact
Emotional impact is engineered, not accidental: use a specific, human-scale example rather than an abstract claim (not 'poverty is bad' but a single vivid detail of a child going without); use direct address ('imagine if this were your family') to close the distance between reader and subject; use sentence rhythm (a long build-up followed by a short, blunt sentence) to land a point. Teach the learner to ask, after drafting a persuasive paragraph, 'what feeling do I want the reader to have by the end of this, and does my last sentence deliver it?'

#### 3.1.2.2.4 Using language creatively, imaginatively and persuasively
This bullet asks writers to bring the same imaginative flair used in descriptive writing (fresh comparisons, vivid verbs) into persuasive and viewpoint writing, so that argument does not become dry or list-like. A persuasive point can still use a striking image or metaphor to make an abstract idea concrete (comparing a policy failure to a leaking roof, for example). Teach the learner that the best Paper 2 Question 5 answers read as crafted writing with a view, not a bullet-pointed list of reasons; the same toolkit of imagery, varied sentence length and deliberate word choice applies across both papers' writing sections.

#### 3.1.2.2.5 Rhetorical devices: rhetorical questions, antithesis, parenthesis and more
Core devices to teach with names and effects: rhetorical question (invites agreement without expecting an answer), antithesis (balancing opposite ideas in one sentence: 'not a burden, but an opportunity'), parenthesis (an aside set off by commas, dashes or brackets that adds a persuasive extra), triples/rule of three (three-part lists for rhythm and emphasis), direct address ('you', 'we'), repetition/anaphora (repeating a phrase at the start of successive sentences), hyperbole (deliberate exaggeration for effect), and emotive language (words chosen for feeling, not neutrality). Teach 'use sparingly': one or two devices per paragraph, deployed precisely, mark far higher than a device crammed into every sentence, which reads as mechanical rather than persuasive.

#### W.A Argument and viewpoint writing craft
A convincing argument response needs a clear overall position stated early, three or four well-developed points (not eight thin ones), a considered counter-argument acknowledged and then answered (which shows sophistication), and a memorable closing line that restates the position with new force rather than just repeating it. Teach the shape: hook opening, position statement, point one with evidence and a device, point two, a brief counter-argument conceded and rebutted, point three, a strong closing call to action or final image. Common error: listing reasons without developing any of them -- teach 'fewer points, developed further' over 'more points, thinner'.
