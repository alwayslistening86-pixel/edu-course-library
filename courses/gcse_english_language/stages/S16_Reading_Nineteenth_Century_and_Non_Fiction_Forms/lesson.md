# S16_Reading_Nineteenth_Century_and_Non_Fiction_Forms - Lesson: Reading Paper 2 sources: 19th-century prose and non-fiction forms

## Goal
The learner can read a 19th-century non-fiction source fluently, recognise the form and conventions of the Paper 2 genres and connect period and form to viewpoint.

## Syllabus items taught here
- 4.3.1 - Paper 2 Section A sources: linked non-fiction and literary non-fiction from the 19th and 20th/21st century: know the genres and time-period pairing on Paper 2
- T.10 - Non-fiction forms and their conventions: recognise article, letter, diary, speech, review, essay, travel writing and report conventions
- T.11 - Reading nineteenth-century prose: vocabulary, syntax and attitudes: read older prose fluently and analyse it

## How to teach this
Give the learner a long Victorian-style sentence (write one) and ask them to translate it into modern English, marking each clause. Show that these sentences are built from the same clauses they already know; they are just longer and more formal.

#### 4.3.1 Paper 2 Section A sources: linked non-fiction and literary non-fiction from the 19th and 20th/21st century
The spec says Paper 2 sources are non-fiction and literary non-fiction, one from the 19th century and one from the 20th or 21st, so that viewpoints and perspectives over time can be considered. Genres named include high quality journalism, articles, reports, essays, travel writing, accounts, sketches, letters, diaries, autobiography and biographical passages. Both texts share a theme (a topic such as holidays, schooling, nature, poverty or technology), but the writers may differ strongly in attitude. Practical routine: on first read, write the theme, each writer's attitude in a word, and where each attitude shifts. Look for the writer's purpose: to inform, argue, entertain, reflect, complain, record.

#### T.10 Non-fiction forms and their conventions
Article: headline, standfirst, subheadings, a strong opening, journalistic voice. Letter: address, greeting ('Dear...'), sign-off ('Yours sincerely/faithfully'), a purpose stated early. Diary: first person, past or present, dates, private tone, fragments. Speech: direct address, rhetorical devices, inclusive pronouns, a call to action. Review: judgement, evidence, comparison, star rating or verdict. Essay: thesis, developed argument, reasoning, formal tone. Travel writing: sensory description, personal response, anecdote. Report: factual, formal, headings, impersonal. Autobiography/biography: reflection, hindsight, selected memories. Guide/leaflet: imperatives, headings, bullet lists, direct address. Identify form to understand purpose and audience and to judge effect; the same form knowledge feeds your Q5 writing (stage S20).

#### T.11 Reading nineteenth-century prose: vocabulary, syntax and attitudes
Features to expect: long sentences with several subordinate clauses and semi-colons; formal vocabulary and words with changed meanings; passive voice and impersonal constructions ('it is to be observed that...'); a moralising or authoritative tone; attitudes about class, gender, empire or progress that may appear as assumptions rather than arguments. Reading strategies: find the main clause first (subject and verb) and treat the rest as detail; read aloud to hear the rhythm; guess unfamiliar words from context; identify the writer's view even when it is expressed politely or ironically. For analysis, do not condemn the past; explain the attitude and how the language conveys it: 'the polite, formal tone and the phrase "lower orders" show the writer's assumed superiority'. Ask the learner to paraphrase a 60-word passage sentence by sentence.
