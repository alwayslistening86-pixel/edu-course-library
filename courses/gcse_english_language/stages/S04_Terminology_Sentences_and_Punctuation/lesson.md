# S04_Terminology_Sentences_and_Punctuation - Lesson: Terminology II: sentence forms, punctuation and effects

## Goal
The learner can name sentence types and forms, and explain the effect of sentence length and punctuation choices.

## Syllabus items taught here
- T.3 - Sentence types and forms: simple, compound, complex, minor; statement, question, command, exclamation: name sentence forms and explain their effect
- T.5 - Punctuation as a tool for effect: explain the effect of dashes, ellipses, colons, semi-colons and more

## How to teach this
Write one idea three ways on the board: 'He ran.' / 'He ran, faster than he had ever run, past the shuttered shops, past the sleeping cars, until his lungs burned.' / 'Run.' Ask which is most urgent, which is most dreamy, and what makes them different. Then teach the labels for what they noticed.

#### T.3 Sentence types and forms: simple, compound, complex, minor; statement, question, command, exclamation
By structure: a simple sentence has one clause ('The door opened.'); a compound sentence joins clauses of equal weight with a coordinating conjunction ('The door opened and she froze.'); a complex sentence has a main clause plus at least one subordinate clause ('When the door opened, she froze.'). A minor sentence lacks a finite verb ('Silence.' 'Nothing but sand.'). By function: statements, questions, commands (imperatives) and exclamations. Effects to be able to state: short simple or minor sentences give shock, emphasis or suspense; long, complex sentences can suggest reflection, accumulation or overwhelm; questions draw the reader in; commands sound authoritative or urgent. Marks come from linking form to effect and context, not naming a form alone.

#### T.5 Punctuation as a tool for effect
Punctuation shapes reading speed and tone. A full stop gives finality; a comma marks a slight pause or lists; a colon introduces an explanation or list and creates anticipation; a semi-colon links closely connected clauses and shows balance; a dash (single) can add a dramatic afterthought or interruption, and paired dashes or brackets add parenthesis; an ellipsis suggests trailing off, hesitation or suspense; an exclamation mark shows intensity but overuse looks weak; inverted commas show speech or irony ('so-called'). In analysis, be specific: 'The dash interrupts the sentence, mimicking the narrator's sudden realisation.' Also note that punctuation is a technical accuracy skill in Section B (AO6), so this stage feeds writing too.
