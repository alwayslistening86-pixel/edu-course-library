# S03_Terminology_Word_Classes_Phrases_Clauses - Lesson: Terminology I: word classes, phrases, clauses and verb forms

## Goal
The learner can label the main word classes, identify phrases and clauses and explain what verb forms do.

## Syllabus items taught here
- T.1 - Word classes: nouns, verbs, adjectives, adverbs, pronouns, determiners, prepositions, conjunctions: label words and explain their effect
- T.2 - Phrases, clauses and subordination: identify phrases, main and subordinate clauses
- T.4 - Verb forms: tense, aspect, voice and mood, modal verbs: analyse how verb choices shape meaning

## How to teach this
Ask the learner to say what 'run' is. When they say 'a verb', offer 'a run in her tights' and 'the morning run' and show that class depends on use in a sentence. Never teach terminology as a list to memorise; always pair a label with its effect.

#### T.1 Word classes: nouns, verbs, adjectives, adverbs, pronouns, determiners, prepositions, conjunctions
A noun names something (common: 'gate'; proper: 'Lagos'; abstract: 'dread'; collective: 'flock'). A verb expresses action, state or occurrence ('slammed', 'was'). An adjective modifies a noun ('silver rain'); an adverb modifies a verb, adjective or another adverb ('hurriedly', 'very'). Pronouns replace nouns ('she', 'them'); determiners introduce nouns ('the', 'a', 'my', 'those'); prepositions show relationships ('under', 'beyond'); conjunctions link ('and', 'because', 'although'). Effect matters more than labels: strong verbs ('hauled', 'hurled') carry violent energy; a run of adjectives builds atmosphere; abstract nouns ('anticipation') let a writer turn feelings into things. A mark-worthy note is 'the verb "hurled" suggests force and finality', not 'the writer uses a verb'.

#### T.2 Phrases, clauses and subordination
A phrase is a group of words without its own subject and finite verb ('under the bridge', 'the tall man in grey'). A clause has a subject and finite verb. A main clause makes sense alone ('The rain fell'); a subordinate clause depends on a main one ('although it was late', 'which she had hidden', 'when the bell rang'). Subordinators such as 'because', 'although', 'while', 'if' and 'which' signal them. Relative clauses add detail to a noun. Effects: subordination lets a writer add layers of description or reasoning; a main clause alone can be direct and decisive. Practise by taking 'He waited' and building it out: 'He waited, although the light was fading and the road, which had been empty all day, stayed empty.' Ask the learner to label each part.

#### T.4 Verb forms: tense, aspect, voice and mood, modal verbs
Tense places an action in time (past 'walked', present 'walks', future 'will walk'). Aspect shows whether an action is ongoing or complete ('was walking', 'has walked'). Voice: active ('The dog chased the boy') or passive ('The boy was chased'), and the passive can hide who acted, useful for an evasive or official tone. Mood: indicative (statements), imperative (commands: 'Listen.'), and subjunctive ('If I were you'). Modal verbs ('must', 'might', 'could', 'should', 'will') show certainty or obligation: 'must' is forceful, 'might' is tentative. Effect: present tense in narration creates immediacy; the imperative in an advert commands; modals of certainty ('will') make a writer sound authoritative. Ask the learner to rewrite 'Volunteers are needed' as an imperative and describe the change of tone.
