# S02_Texts_Periods_and_What_Sources_Look_Like - Lesson: What the sources are: text types, time periods and substantial texts

## Goal
The learner can say what kinds of text the papers use, which centuries they come from, and how to approach a substantial unseen text.

## Syllabus items taught here
- 3.0.1 - Text types the specification requires: literature, literary non-fiction and other writing: name the range of text types used as reading sources
- 3.0.2 - Time periods: 19th, 20th and 21st century texts: place sources in their period and know which paper uses which
- 3.0.3 - Substantial texts; transient content excluded: understand that sources are substantial, high quality and unseen

## How to teach this
Give the learner four very short extracts (one line each: a 1850s diary entry, a modern travel blog, a magazine review, a novel opening) and ask them to sort by type and century and say what gave each away. Then reveal that this sorting is exactly what the specification asks them to be able to read fluently.

#### 3.0.1 Text types the specification requires: literature, literary non-fiction and other writing
The spec requires study of literature and extended literary non-fiction, and other writing such as essays, reviews and journalism, both printed and online. In the exams this becomes a literature fiction extract on Paper 1 (a novel or short story) and, on Paper 2, non-fiction and literary non-fiction. Genres named for Paper 2 include high-quality journalism, articles, reports, essays, travel writing, accounts, sketches, letters, diaries, autobiography and biographical passages. Literary non-fiction is real writing that uses literary techniques: a memoir describing a storm, for instance, may use simile and structure as a novelist does. Ask the learner to explain the difference between an article and a piece of literary non-fiction, then find a sentence in each that would fit either.

#### 3.0.2 Time periods: 19th, 20th and 21st century texts
Across the qualification, sources come from the 19th, 20th and 21st centuries. Paper 1's fiction extract is from the 20th or 21st century. Paper 2 pairs a 19th-century source with a 20th- or 21st-century one, chosen so that the comparison shows viewpoints and perspectives over time. The 19th-century text is usually harder: longer sentences, older vocabulary, formal tone and attitudes that may seem strange today (see stage S16). Teach a habit: on the first read of a Paper 2 pair, label each source with its likely century and one word describing its attitude, then plan how the difference in period may explain the difference in view. Do not overclaim: date alone never explains a perspective, the text does.

#### 3.0.3 Substantial texts; transient content excluded
The spec says each text studied must be a substantial piece of writing making significant demands on students in content, structure and quality of language, and that texts that are essentially transient, such as instant news feeds, must not be included. All exam texts are unseen. The number and length of texts are not prescribed, but in practice sources run to around 60 lines with line numbers printed so questions can direct you to 'lines 5 to 15'. Practical consequences: sources have enough material to analyse for a whole extended answer, questions point to line ranges you must stay within, and reading the whole source calmly before answering is worth the time. Practise reading a substantial unseen text at pace with a pencil, marking key words, shifts and feelings.

## Explicitly not here
Analysis methods, the questions themselves, and the terminology of language and structure.
