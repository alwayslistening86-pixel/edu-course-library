# S18_Descriptive_and_Narrative_Craft_Paper_1_Section_B - Lesson: Descriptive and narrative writing craft; the Paper 1 Section B task and Question 5

## Goal
The learner can transform given material imaginatively, plan and write a timed descriptive or narrative response to a Paper 1 Question 5 prompt.

## Syllabus items taught here
- 3.1.2.1.3 - Using language imaginatively and creatively: craft original, vivid language rather than cliche
- 3.1.2.1.4 - Using information provided by others to write in different forms: transform given material into a new form
- W.D - Descriptive writing craft: sustain focused, sensory, controlled description
- W.N - Narrative writing craft: shape a short story with a strong opening, development and ending
- W.1 - Planning and timing a writing task: plan quickly and use time on a 40-mark task well
- 4.2.2 - Paper 1 Section B: descriptive or narrative writing from a prompt, scenario or visual image: know what the Paper 1 writing task offers and demands
- P1.Q5 - Paper 1 Question 5: descriptive or narrative writing (24 + 16 marks, AO5 and AO6): write one 40-mark creative response under time

## How to teach this
Show the learner a single evocative image (an empty fairground at dusk) and give them 90 seconds to write down every sense impression they can. Then ask: 'Is this a moment (description) or a story (narrative)? What happens if nothing happens?' Use this to introduce the two Question 5 options.

#### 3.1.2.1.3 Using language imaginatively and creatively
Imaginative language avoids the first cliche that comes to mind (heart racing, tears streaming) and instead builds a fresh, specific image from real sensory detail: not 'the sea was rough' but 'the sea dragged shingle back with every wave, like something clearing its throat'. Teach the 'make it strange' technique: describe something ordinary as if seeing it for the first time, using an unexpected comparison rooted in the scene's own logic (a fairground description might compare silence to a held breath, not to something unrelated). Creativity is rewarded when it still serves the task's purpose, not when it is decoration bolted on.

#### 3.1.2.1.4 Using information provided by others to write in different forms
Some tasks (especially transformation-style practice, though Question 5 itself gives an image or a single-line title) ask the learner to take given material -- a set of facts, a short article, a title -- and turn it into a new form: a set of bullet points into a descriptive paragraph, a news event into a first-person narrative. Teach the skill as three steps: extract the core facts or mood, decide the new form's conventions, then reshape (not copy) the material into that form, adding invented sensory and emotional detail the original did not contain. This underlies both creative writing and, later, viewpoint writing that responds to a given source.

#### W.D Descriptive writing craft
A strong description has no real plot, but is not shapeless: teach a spatial or sensory structure (wide shot to close-up, or one sense at a time, or before/during/after a change in light or weather), controlled paragraphing (one clear focus per paragraph: sound, then sight, then a single human detail), and restraint with figurative language (two or three well-placed images beat ten crowded ones). End on a deliberate final image rather than trailing off. Common error: drifting into narrative ('and then he walked over') when the task asked only for description -- teach the learner to notice and cut sudden plot movement in a pure description task.

#### W.N Narrative writing craft
A short story in 45 minutes needs a tight shape: teach the 'small story' principle -- one character, one moment of change, a single scene or a short time span, not a whole life. A reliable structure: an intriguing opening line, a small amount of scene-setting, one complication or turn, a decision or realisation, a short resolving final line that echoes or answers the opening. Warn against overambitious plots (a war, a murder, a decade) that cannot be done justice in 400 words; a small, well-observed moment (a missed train, an overheard argument) marks higher than a rushed epic. Dialogue should be used sparingly and purposefully, not to pad length. From 2026, if the learner chooses to write only an opening (now explicitly offered as an option), the same shape still applies in miniature: an intriguing opening line, scene-setting, and the first hint of a complication or turn are enough -- there is no requirement to reach a decision, realisation or resolving line, and a learner who runs out of time partway through a full-story attempt should not simply stop, but should recognise early whether they are writing a complete story or an opening and shape their time and paragraphing accordingly.

#### W.1 Planning and timing a writing task
For a 40-mark, 45-minute writing task, teach a fixed time budget: about 5 minutes planning (a four-point outline: opening image/line, development, turn, ending), about 35 minutes drafting, about 5 minutes proofreading. The plan should fix the point of view, the basic shape, and one or two key images or turns before writing starts, so the response is not invented sentence by sentence under pressure. Teach the learner to write the plan visibly (a few words per stage) rather than skip it, because a visible plan is also evidence of AO5b's organisation.

#### 4.2.2 Paper 1 Section B: descriptive or narrative writing from a prompt, scenario or visual image
Section B gives one image and a choice of two tasks: usually 'write a description suggested by this image' or a narrative from a given title or opening line, and the learner picks one. It is worth 40 marks (24 AO5 content/organisation, 16 AO6 technical accuracy) and is the single biggest question on either paper. There is no set text or source to respond to here: the image or title is a springboard, and the response is wholly the learner's invention. Teach the learner to glance at both options and choose the one they can picture most vividly in the first ten seconds, rather than defaulting to the same choice every time.

#### P1.Q5 Paper 1 Question 5: descriptive or narrative writing (24 + 16 marks, AO5 and AO6) -- 2026 wording
Question 5 is answered after Section A's reading questions, using the remaining time (roughly 45 minutes of the 1h45 paper). The rubric offers 'EITHER write a description... OR write the opening/story...' based on a printed image and/or title. From 2026, both options are worded to reduce two common sources of pressure: the descriptive option now explicitly reminds the learner that they can use their imagination and do not have to describe only what the picture literally shows; the narrative option now explicitly offers writing just the opening of a story, not necessarily a complete one with an ending, as an accepted alternative to a full short story. Neither change alters the marks or the marking grids: it is still marked on the same two grids as Paper 2 Question 5, AO5 up to 24 (Level 4, 19-24: compelling, convincing, complex ideas, sophisticated control) and AO6 up to 16 (Level 4, 13-16: wide vocabulary range, sophisticated punctuation range used for effect, accurate spelling throughout including ambitious vocabulary), and an opening is still judged by the same criteria as a complete story -- shape, control and technical accuracy, not whether it reaches an ending. Teach the learner that examiners reward ambition attempted with control over ambition attempted without it: a simpler, well-controlled piece (or a vivid, controlled opening) outscores an overreaching, messy one.

## Explicitly not here
Purpose/audience/AO5 strands in the abstract (S17); viewpoint and argument writing, Paper 2's task (S19-S20); technical accuracy in detail (S20-S21).
