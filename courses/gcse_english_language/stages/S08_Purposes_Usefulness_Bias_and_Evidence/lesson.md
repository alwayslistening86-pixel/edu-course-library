# S08_Purposes_Usefulness_Bias_and_Evidence - Lesson: Reading for purpose: usefulness, bias and evidence

## Goal
The learner can read in different ways for different purposes, judge how useful a text is, and distinguish evidenced statements from unsupported opinion and bias.

## Syllabus items taught here
- 3.1.1.2 - Reading in different ways for different purposes; usefulness, relevance and presentation: choose a reading approach for a purpose and judge how useful a text is
- 3.1.1.5 - Bias, misuse of evidence, and supported versus unsupported statements: spot bias and tell evidenced claims from opinion

## How to teach this
Give the learner two texts about the same topic: an advertisement for a hotel and a neutral inspector's report. Ask: which would you use to book a room, which to judge the hotel's safety, and why? Purpose decides how you read.

#### 3.1.1.2 Reading in different ways for different purposes; usefulness, relevance and presentation
The spec asks students to read in different ways for different purposes, and to compare and evaluate the usefulness, relevance and presentation of content for those purposes. Reading modes: skimming (gist), scanning (finding specific detail), close reading (analysis), and comparative reading (across texts). Usefulness asks: does this text contain what I need? Relevance asks: is it about my actual question? Presentation asks: do headings, layout, tone, images and vocabulary help or hinder that purpose? An eyewitness diary is valuable for personal impressions but poor for statistics; a government report is the reverse. In the exam this shows up in Q2 and Q4 where you decide what each source offers, and in Q5 where you choose form for audience. Ask the learner to rank three texts for a stated purpose and defend the ranking.

#### 3.1.1.5 Bias, misuse of evidence, and supported versus unsupported statements
A fact can be checked; an opinion is a view; a supported claim gives evidence for its view. Bias is a one-sided presentation that hides or distorts counter-evidence. Signs: emotive or loaded words ('so-called experts'), selective statistics, generalisations ('everybody knows'), absent voices, unnamed sources, and slippery causation (assuming because two things happen together, one causes the other). Misuse of evidence includes a real statistic used for the wrong point, anecdote presented as proof, or a quotation with the context removed. Teach a three-question test for any claim: what is the evidence, who says so, and what has been left out? This underpins Paper 2, where a 19th-century and a modern writer may be biased in different ways, and it also improves your own persuasive writing by showing what a sceptical reader will challenge.

## Explicitly not here
Writing an extended comparison (S15) or evaluating fiction (S14).
