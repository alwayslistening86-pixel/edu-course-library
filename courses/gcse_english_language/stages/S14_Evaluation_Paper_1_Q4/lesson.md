# S14_Evaluation_Paper_1_Q4 - Lesson: Evaluating a text: Paper 1 Question 4 (20 marks) and different responses

## Goal
The learner can write a sustained 20-mark critical evaluation of a statement about part of a fiction source, using context, alternative readings and precise evidence.

## Syllabus items taught here
- AO4 - AO4: evaluate texts critically and support this with appropriate textual references: judge how effective a text is and defend the judgement
- P1.Q4 - Paper 1 Question 4: evaluate a statement about part of the source (20 marks, AO4): write a full critical evaluation with judicious references
- 3.1.1.6 - Reflecting critically using the text's context and wider reading: bring context and reading experience to a critical judgement
- 3.1.1.7 - Recognising the possibility of different responses to a text: acknowledge and weigh alternative readings

## How to teach this
Say a statement about a text, such as 'the writer makes the town seem welcoming', and ask the learner to argue against it first. Then ask them to write one sentence that agrees in part. Evaluation grows from the tension between a claim and the text.

#### AO4 AO4: evaluate texts critically and support this with appropriate textual references
AO4 (12.5% of the GCSE, Paper 1 Q4 only) reads: 'Evaluate texts critically and support this with appropriate textual references.' The descriptors are: Level 4 develops a convincing and critical response to the statement, shows perceptive understanding of writer's methods, selects judicious detail and evaluates critically and in detail the effect on the reader; Level 3 makes a clear and relevant response, shows clear understanding of methods, selects relevant references and evaluates clearly the effect; Level 2 makes some response to the statement, some attempts at evaluation, some understanding of methods; Level 1 simple and limited. Marks: L1 1-5, L2 6-10, L3 11-15, L4 16-20. Reference to the writer's methods and the 'I agree/disagree' can be implicit, and credit is given for quality.

#### P1.Q4 Paper 1 Question 4: evaluate a statement about part of the source (20 marks, AO4) -- 2026 wording
The question directs you to the second part or another named part of the source ('from line 20 to the end') and asks 'To what extent do you agree?' with prompts to consider your own impressions, evaluate how the writer describes the focus, and support with references. Before 2026 the statement to evaluate was introduced as coming from 'a student', for example 'A student said this part of the source shows...'; from 2026 that framing is dropped and the statement is simply given directly ('This part of the source shows...'), which AQA's own stated aim was to simplify the wording; the 2026 wording also makes explicit that a learner may 'agree and/or disagree' with the statement (a full or partial agreement was already the strongest response before 2026, so this is a clarification, not a change of method) and states plainly which part of the extract the question concerns -- the task, the statement's content, the marks (20) and the mark scheme's four levels are all unchanged. Spend about 20 minutes and write four or five substantial paragraphs. Plan: a short opening line stating your position ('I agree to a large extent, although...'); three or four points, each moving through the text in order, each with a quotation, an analysis of method (language and structure) and the effect on the reader; and a closing judgement that answers the statement directly. Use the statement's key words in every paragraph. Stay within the given lines. Avoid retelling the story. Spend time on evaluative verbs: 'the writer strongly suggests', 'this undermines', 'this complicates'.

#### 3.1.1.6 Reflecting critically using the text's context and wider reading
The spec asks students to reflect 'critically and evaluatively on text, using the context of the text and drawing on knowledge and skills gained from wider reading'. In the exam this does not mean bringing in outside facts; it means using what the passage shows about setting, time and society, and what you know of how similar stories work. A newcomer arriving in a strange city is a familiar pattern (a threshold, a moment of hope followed by doubt); recognising the pattern strengthens your judgement ('as in many stories of arrival, the excitement is undercut by uncertainty'). Keep it brief and tied to the text. Wider reading also improves your own writing, which is why the spec includes it. Ask the learner to name two novels or films that use the same pattern.

#### 3.1.1.7 Recognising the possibility of different responses to a text
Readers respond differently, and the spec asks you to recognise this. In an evaluation, show it with tentative or comparative language: 'This could suggest excitement, but it could equally hint at fear', 'a reader might see the pause as reluctance, although it may simply be respect'. This does not mean sitting on the fence; state the reading you find most convincing and say why. Both agreement and disagreement can earn top marks if convincingly supported. Alternative interpretations reflect the AO4 phrase 'critically'. Practise by taking one image ('a closed white door') and writing two possible responses, then choosing one.
