# BTF_S03_Governance_Ethics_and_Corporate_Responsibility - Lesson: Governance, ethics and corporate responsibility

## Goal
The learner explains corporate governance, the role of a board of directors and non-executive directors, corporate codes of ethics, and how ethical conflicts and dilemmas in a business context should be handled.

## Syllabus items taught here
- BTF.3a - Corporate governance and the board
- BTF.3b - Board committees
- BTF.3c - Corporate codes of ethics
- BTF.3d - Ethical conflicts and dilemmas
- BTF.3e - Sustainable business practices

## How to teach this
Ask: why might a listed company's board include directors with no day-to-day management role at all? Have the learner predict the result of every example before running it, and run code for real whenever they can.

#### BTF.3a Corporate governance and the board
**Corporate governance** is the system by which companies are directed and controlled, balancing the interests of shareholders, management and other stakeholders. The **UK Corporate Governance Code** (for listed companies, 'comply or explain') sets expectations for board leadership and company purpose, division of responsibilities, composition/succession/evaluation, audit/risk/internal control, and remuneration. A unitary **board of directors** typically comprises **executive directors** (also managing the business day-to-day) and **non-executive directors (NEDs)**, who bring independent oversight and challenge, sit on committees (audit, remuneration, nomination) and should not be involved in day-to-day management, preserving their objectivity.

#### BTF.3b Board committees
**Board committees**: the **audit committee** (mainly independent NEDs) oversees financial reporting integrity, the external auditor relationship and internal controls; the **remuneration committee** sets executive pay, aligning it with long-term performance; the **nomination committee** oversees board appointments and succession planning - each exists partly to manage the self-interest and self-review threats that would arise if executives set their own pay or oversaw their own financial reporting unchecked.

#### BTF.3c Corporate codes of ethics
**Corporate codes of ethics** set an organisation's own expected standards of behaviour for staff, often building on a professional body's code (see the Assurance module's ICAEW Code of Ethics for the accountancy-specific version) but extending to all employees, covering matters like anti-bribery, whistleblowing, conflicts of interest, and supplier/customer treatment. A well-embedded code, supported by tone from the top and training, shapes organisational culture; a code that exists only on paper, unsupported by genuine leadership behaviour, does little.

#### BTF.3d Ethical conflicts and dilemmas
**Ethical conflicts and dilemmas** in a business context arise where competing obligations or interests pull in different directions (e.g. loyalty to an employer versus a duty to report wrongdoing, or short-term shareholder returns versus long-term sustainability). A structured approach - identify the facts and stakeholders, identify the ethical issue(s) and relevant principles/codes, consider alternative courses of action and their consequences, then decide and be prepared to justify the decision - helps resolve dilemmas consistently rather than on an ad hoc basis; where a serious concern cannot be resolved internally, **whistleblowing** (raising a concern, often protected by law, e.g. under the Public Interest Disclosure Act) may be the appropriate route.

#### BTF.3e Sustainable business practices
**Sustainable business practices**: increasingly, governance frameworks expect a business to consider its wider environmental and social impact, not just short-term shareholder return - e.g. through sustainability reporting (see the Accounting module's ISSB reference), supply chain due diligence, and setting measurable ESG targets - reflecting a shift from viewing sustainability purely as a cost, to viewing it as core to long-term value and risk management.
