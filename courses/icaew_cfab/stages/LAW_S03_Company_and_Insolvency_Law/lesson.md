# LAW_S03_Company_and_Insolvency_Law - Lesson: Company and insolvency law

## Goal
The learner explains the differences between business structures, company formation and capital-raising, shareholder rights, director duties, and the main insolvency procedures.

## Syllabus items taught here
- LAW.3a - Incorporation and separate legal personality
- LAW.3b - Company/LLP formation and pre-incorporation contracts
- LAW.3c - Financing and capital, capital maintenance
- LAW.3d - Shareholder resolutions and director duties
- LAW.3e - Fixed and floating charges, insolvency procedures

## How to teach this
Ask: why is a limited company described as having a 'separate legal personality' from its shareholders - and what famous case established this? Have the learner predict the result of every example before running it, and run code for real whenever they can.

#### LAW.3a Incorporation and separate legal personality
**Incorporation and separate legal personality**: a registered company is a separate legal person from its shareholders and directors (established in *Salomon v Salomon & Co Ltd* [1897]), meaning it can own property, sue and be sued, and enter contracts in its own name, and its shareholders' liability for the company's debts is normally limited to the amount unpaid on their shares. Courts may exceptionally **'pierce the corporate veil'** (treat the company and its controllers as one) in narrow circumstances, e.g. where the company structure is used as a facade to evade an existing legal obligation.

#### LAW.3b Company/LLP formation and pre-incorporation contracts
**Company/LLP formation**: incorporating a company requires filing required documents (including a memorandum of association and, historically, articles of association - the company's internal rulebook governing matters like director powers and shareholder rights) with Companies House, along with statutory details (registered office, statement of capital, details of directors/persons with significant control); an LLP is formed by registering an incorporation document. A **promoter** who sets up a company and enters **pre-incorporation contracts** on its behalf before it legally exists is generally personally liable on those contracts, since the company does not yet exist to be bound, unless the contract provides otherwise or is later validly adopted/novated by the company.

#### LAW.3c Financing and capital, capital maintenance
**Financing and capital**: shares may be issued at a **premium** (above nominal value, credited to a share premium account) or, exceptionally and subject to strict conditions, at a **discount**; **pre-emption rights** generally require a company to offer new shares to existing shareholders first, in proportion to their holding, before issuing to outsiders, protecting against dilution. Capital-raising methods include a **placing** (shares sold to selected institutional investors) and an **Initial Public Offering (IPO)** (shares offered to the public, typically alongside a stock exchange listing). Under the Companies Act 2006, most companies must file statutory accounts (and, above size thresholds, have them audited) with Companies House. **Capital maintenance** rules (e.g. restrictions on a reduction of capital, share redemption/purchase and profit distributions) exist to protect creditors, broadly requiring that dividends are paid only out of distributable (realised) profits.

#### LAW.3d Shareholder resolutions and director duties
**Ownership and management**: shareholders influence the company through **general meetings** and **resolutions** - an **ordinary resolution** (simple majority, e.g. appointing a director) versus a **special resolution** (75% majority, e.g. changing the articles); shareholders also have statutory rights to challenge mismanagement (e.g. a derivative claim on the company's behalf, or an unfair prejudice petition). **Directors** are appointed and can be removed by ordinary resolution (with statutory notice requirements); they owe statutory duties under the Companies Act 2006, including to act within their powers, to promote the success of the company for the benefit of its members as a whole (having regard to wider factors including employees, the environment, and the company's reputation - reflecting sustainability responsibilities), to exercise independent judgement, to exercise reasonable care/skill/diligence, to avoid conflicts of interest, and not to accept benefits from third parties.

#### LAW.3e Fixed and floating charges, insolvency procedures
**Insolvency**: a **fixed charge** attaches to a specific identifiable asset (e.g. a building), restricting the company's ability to deal with it without the lender's consent; a **floating charge** hovers over a class of changing assets (e.g. inventory) until it **crystallises** (e.g. on default or liquidation) into a fixed charge over the assets then held; charges generally must be registered at Companies House within a strict time limit to be effective against other creditors/a liquidator. Insolvency procedures include a **company voluntary arrangement (CVA)** (an agreement with creditors to pay debts over time, often while trading continues), **administration** (an administrator takes control to try to rescue the company, achieve a better result for creditors than liquidation, or realise assets for secured/preferential creditors), **receivership** (a receiver appointed, typically by a secured lender, to recover what is owed to them from specific charged assets), and **liquidation** (winding up the company, realising its assets, and distributing proceeds to creditors in a strict statutory order of priority - fixed-charge holders, then liquidation expenses/preferential creditors, then floating-charge holders, then unsecured creditors, then shareholders - before the company is dissolved).
