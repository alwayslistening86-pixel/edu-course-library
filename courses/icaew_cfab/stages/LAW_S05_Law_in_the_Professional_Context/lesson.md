# LAW_S05_Law_in_the_Professional_Context - Lesson: Law in the professional context: data protection and employment law

## Goal
The learner explains data protection obligations, when someone is an employee versus self-employed, the key features of an employment contract, wrongful and unfair dismissal, redundancy, and key employer responsibilities.

## Syllabus items taught here
- LAW.5a - Data Protection Act 2018 and UK GDPR
- LAW.5b - Employment status
- LAW.5c - Employment contracts
- LAW.5d - Wrongful versus unfair dismissal
- LAW.5e - Statutory redundancy
- LAW.5f - Equality Act 2010 and Modern Slavery Act 2015

## How to teach this
Ask: why does it matter, legally, whether someone working for a business is an 'employee' rather than genuinely self-employed? Have the learner predict the result of every example before running it, and run code for real whenever they can.

#### LAW.5a Data Protection Act 2018 and UK GDPR
**Data protection**: the **Data Protection Act 2018** (alongside UK GDPR) governs the processing of **personal data** (information relating to an identifiable living individual), requiring it to be processed lawfully, fairly and transparently; collected for specified, explicit purposes; adequate, relevant and limited to what is necessary; accurate; kept no longer than necessary; and processed securely. The **Information Commissioner's Office (ICO)** enforces compliance, with significant fines for serious breaches; a business's information systems (and any technology used to process personal data - see the Business, Technology and Finance module) must be designed with these principles in mind.

#### LAW.5b Employment status
**Employment status**: whether someone is an **employee**, a **worker**, or genuinely **self-employed** is determined by the substance of the relationship, not just its label, looking at factors including **mutuality of obligation** (is the business obliged to offer, and the individual obliged to accept, work), **control** (does the business control how/when/where the work is done), and **personal service** (must the individual do the work personally, or can they send a substitute). Employment status matters because employees receive significantly greater statutory protections (e.g. unfair dismissal rights, redundancy pay) than workers or the genuinely self-employed, and affects tax/NIC treatment (see the Principles of Taxation module).

#### LAW.5c Employment contracts
**Employment contracts**: formed like any contract (offer, acceptance, consideration - the wage - and intention to create legal relations), but employees must be given a **written statement of employment particulars** covering key terms (pay, hours, holiday, notice); a contract can be terminated by notice (per its terms or statutory minimum notice, whichever is longer), by mutual agreement, on completion of a fixed term, or summarily for **gross misconduct**.

#### LAW.5d Wrongful versus unfair dismissal
**Wrongful versus unfair dismissal**: **wrongful dismissal** is a breach-of-contract claim (e.g. dismissal without the contractual/statutory notice required, with no justification such as gross misconduct), available to any employee regardless of length of service, with damages generally limited to what should have been paid during the notice period. **Unfair dismissal** is a statutory claim (normally requiring at least two years' continuous employment) that the dismissal was not for one of the potentially fair reasons (conduct, capability, redundancy, illegality, or 'some other substantial reason') or that a fair process was not followed; remedies include reinstatement, re-engagement, or compensation (a basic award plus a compensatory award).

#### LAW.5e Statutory redundancy
**Redundancy**: arises where a job itself disappears (e.g. the business closes, relocates, or needs fewer employees doing that kind of work) rather than any fault of the employee; an eligible employee (normally two years' continuous service) is entitled to a **statutory redundancy payment**, calculated from age, length of service (capped) and weekly pay (capped), and the employer must follow a fair selection process and, where appropriate, consult.

#### LAW.5f Equality Act 2010 and Modern Slavery Act 2015
**Employer responsibilities**: the **Equality Act 2010** prohibits discrimination against employees/job applicants on protected characteristics (e.g. age, sex, race, disability, religion); the **Modern Slavery Act 2015** requires larger organisations to publish an annual statement on the steps taken to prevent modern slavery/human trafficking in their business and supply chains; and employers increasingly face sustainability-related regulation and reporting obligations (see the Business, Technology and Finance module) affecting how they run their workforce and supply chain.
