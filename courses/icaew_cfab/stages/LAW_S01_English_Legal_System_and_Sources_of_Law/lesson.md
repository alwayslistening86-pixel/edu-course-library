# LAW_S01_English_Legal_System_and_Sources_of_Law - Lesson: The English legal system and sources of law

## Goal
The learner explains the relationship between civil and criminal law, the structure of the English court system, alternative dispute resolution, and the impact of the Human Rights Act.

## Syllabus items taught here
- LAW.1a - Civil law, criminal law and sources of law
- LAW.1b - Structure of the English court system
- LAW.1c - Alternative dispute resolution
- LAW.1d - European Convention on Human Rights and the Human Rights Act 1998
- LAW.1e - International law and regulations

## How to teach this
Ask: is a company being sued by a customer for breach of contract facing a civil case or a criminal case - and why does that distinction change what the court can order? Have the learner predict the result of every example before running it, and run code for real whenever they can.

#### LAW.1a Civil law, criminal law and sources of law
**Civil law versus criminal law**: **civil law** resolves disputes between private parties (individuals, companies), decided on the **balance of probabilities**, with remedies aimed at compensating the claimant (e.g. damages, an injunction) - a breach of contract or a negligence claim is civil. **Criminal law** is brought by the state against a defendant for conduct society has decided to punish (e.g. fraud, theft), decided **beyond reasonable doubt**, with penalties including fines and imprisonment. The same conduct can sometimes give rise to both a criminal prosecution and a civil claim (e.g. a fraudulent director could face prosecution and a civil claim for the funds). Sources of English law include **legislation** (Acts of Parliament and secondary legislation), **case law** (judicial precedent - a binding decision from a higher court must be followed by lower courts in similar future cases), and, in a professional context, ethical codes.

#### LAW.1b Structure of the English court system
**Structure of the English court system**: civil claims generally start in the **County Court** (lower value/complexity) or the **High Court** (higher value/complexity, split into the King's Bench, Chancery and Family Divisions); appeals go to the **Court of Appeal (Civil Division)** and ultimately the **Supreme Court**. Criminal cases start in the **Magistrates' Court** (summary/lower-level offences) or are sent to the **Crown Court** (more serious, indictable offences, often with a jury); appeals go to the Court of Appeal (Criminal Division) and then the Supreme Court.

#### LAW.1c Alternative dispute resolution
**Alternative dispute resolution (ADR)**: methods of resolving a dispute without full court litigation - **arbitration** (a private, binding decision by an arbitrator, often faster and more confidential than court, common in commercial contracts), **mediation** (a neutral third party helps the parties reach their own agreement, non-binding unless a settlement is reached and recorded), and **negotiation**. ADR's benefits typically include lower cost, greater speed, confidentiality, and preserving business relationships compared with litigation.

#### LAW.1d European Convention on Human Rights and the Human Rights Act 1998
**Human rights**: the **European Convention on Human Rights (ECHR)** sets out fundamental rights (e.g. right to a fair trial, right to respect for private life); the **Human Rights Act 1998** incorporates most ECHR rights into UK domestic law, meaning UK courts must interpret legislation compatibly with Convention rights where possible, and public authorities must act compatibly with them.

#### LAW.1e International law and regulations
**International law and regulations**: cross-border business may be governed by non-English law in specified circumstances, e.g. **ICC Incoterms** (standard international trade terms defining when risk/cost passes between buyer and seller, such as FOB or CIF), the **UN Convention on Contracts for the International Sale of Goods (CISG)** (a default framework for international sale-of-goods contracts between parties in different contracting states, unless excluded), and, for some transactions, **Islamic finance principles** (e.g. prohibiting interest/riba, structuring finance around asset-backed or profit-sharing arrangements instead).
