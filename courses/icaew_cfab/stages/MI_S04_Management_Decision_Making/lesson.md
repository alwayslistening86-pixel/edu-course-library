# MI_S04_Management_Decision_Making - Lesson: Management decision making: breakeven, limiting factor and investment appraisal

## Goal
The learner calculates breakeven point, contribution and margin of safety, allocates a scarce resource to maximise contribution, and appraises an investment using payback, NPV, IRR and ARR.

## Syllabus items taught here
- MI.4a - Breakeven, contribution and margin of safety
- MI.4b - Limiting factor decisions
- MI.4c - Investment appraisal: payback, ARR, NPV, IRR
- MI.4d - NPV worked example and appraisal limitations

## How to teach this
Ask: if a product's selling price barely covers its variable cost, is making more of it ever the right call when a factory has limited machine hours? Have the learner predict the result of every example before running it, and run code for real whenever they can.

#### MI.4a Breakeven, contribution and margin of safety
**Breakeven analysis**: **contribution per unit** = selling price - variable cost per unit; **breakeven point** (units) = total fixed costs / contribution per unit (the output level where total contribution exactly covers fixed costs, so profit is nil); **margin of safety** = budgeted/actual sales - breakeven sales, often expressed as a % of budgeted sales, showing how far sales could fall before a loss is made. *Worked example*: selling price £25, variable cost £15, so contribution = £10/unit; fixed costs £60,000: breakeven = 60,000/10 = 6,000 units. Budgeted sales 8,000 units: margin of safety = 8,000 - 6,000 = 2,000 units (25% of budgeted sales).

#### MI.4b Limiting factor decisions
**Limiting factor (scarce resource) decisions**: where one resource (e.g. machine hours, skilled labour) restricts output and several products compete for it, profit is maximised by ranking products by **contribution per unit of the scarce resource** (not contribution per unit alone) and allocating the scarce resource to the highest-ranked product first. *Worked example*: Product A has contribution £20/unit and needs 2 machine hours/unit (contribution/hour = £10.00); Product B has contribution £18/unit and needs 1 machine hour/unit (contribution/hour = £18.00). Product B should be made first, since its contribution per scarce machine hour (£18.00) exceeds Product A's (£10.00).

#### MI.4c Investment appraisal: payback, ARR, NPV, IRR
**Investment appraisal**: **payback period** = the time for cumulative net cash inflows to repay the initial investment (simple, cash-focused, but ignores cash flows after payback and the time value of money). **Accounting Rate of Return (ARR)** = average annual accounting profit / average investment x 100% (uses profit, not cash, and also ignores the time value of money). **Net Present Value (NPV)** discounts every future cash flow to today's value using a discount factor 1/(1+r)^n, then nets off the initial investment; a positive NPV means the project is worth undertaking at that cost of capital, and NPV is regarded as the technically best appraisal method taught at this level, since it is the only one of the three that properly accounts for the time value of money. **Internal Rate of Return (IRR)** is the discount rate at which NPV = 0 - a project is worthwhile if its IRR exceeds the cost of capital.

#### MI.4d NPV worked example and appraisal limitations
*Worked example, NPV*: initial investment £15,000; net cash inflows £6,000, £7,000, £6,000 in years 1-3; discount factors at 10% are 0.909, 0.826, 0.751. NPV = -15,000 + 6,000(0.909) + 7,000(0.826) + 6,000(0.751) = -15,000 + 5454 + 5782 + 4506 = £742, a positive NPV, so the project is worthwhile at a 10% cost of capital. Advantages of investment appraisal generally include structuring a large, risky decision with quantified evidence; a disadvantage is that all methods depend on uncertain cash-flow forecasts, and only NPV/IRR incorporate the time value of money and risk (via the discount rate) directly. Sustainability factors (e.g. carbon cost, regulatory risk) are increasingly built into the forecast cash flows or reflected in a risk-adjusted discount rate.
