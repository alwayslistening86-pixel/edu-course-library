# BTF_S04_Finance_Function_and_Sources_of_Finance - Lesson: The finance function and sources of finance

## Goal
The learner explains the role of the accounting/finance function within a business, the main sources of short-term and long-term finance, and how financial information is provided to different users.

## Syllabus items taught here
- BTF.4a - The accounting and finance function
- BTF.4b - Sources of short-term and long-term finance
- BTF.4c - Cost of debt versus equity finance
- BTF.4d - Financial information provided by a business

## How to teach this
Ask: why might a business choose a bank overdraft for day-to-day cash flow but a long-term loan or share issue to fund a new factory? Have the learner predict the result of every example before running it, and run code for real whenever they can.

#### BTF.4a The accounting and finance function
**The accounting and finance function** within a business typically covers financial accounting (statutory reporting), management accounting (internal decision support - see the Management Information module), treasury (managing cash, funding and financial risk), tax compliance, and internal audit/risk (see the Assurance module); it interacts with every other business function (operations, sales, HR) by recording and reporting the financial consequences of their activities and supporting their decisions with information.

#### BTF.4b Sources of short-term and long-term finance
**Sources of finance**: **short-term** sources include a **bank overdraft** (flexible, interest paid only on the amount drawn, but repayable on demand and often at a higher rate), **trade credit** (delaying payment to suppliers, effectively an interest-free short-term source if paid within terms, but risks damaging supplier relationships/losing early-payment discounts if overused), and **invoice discounting/factoring** (raising cash against outstanding receivables). **Long-term** sources include a **bank loan** (fixed repayment schedule, interest cost, may require security), **share issue** (raises equity finance with no repayment obligation and no fixed interest cost, but dilutes existing shareholders' control and ownership), **retained earnings** (internally generated, no issue costs, but limits how much profit is available to distribute), and **leasing** (obtaining use of an asset without the large upfront capital outlay of buying it).

#### BTF.4c Cost of debt versus equity finance
*Worked example, cost of debt vs share issue*: a company needs £2,000,000. Borrowing at 6% costs £120,000 interest per year (tax-deductible, and repayable), whereas issuing new shares raises the same amount with no fixed annual cost, but existing shareholders' percentage ownership (and control) is diluted, and any future dividend is paid from post-tax profit.

#### BTF.4d Financial information provided by a business
**Financial information provided by a business**: statutory financial statements (for shareholders, HMRC, lenders, and, for larger companies, the public via Companies House); management accounts (internal, frequent, detailed, for decision-making); regulatory returns (e.g. VAT returns to HMRC); and increasingly sustainability/ESG reports. The appropriate level of detail and frequency depends on the user and their decision needs.
