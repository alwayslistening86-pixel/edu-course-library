# ASR_S01_Concept_Process_and_Need_for_Assurance - Lesson: The concept, process and need for assurance

## Goal
The learner explains what an assurance engagement is, why users want one, who is involved, the expectation gap, how an engagement is planned (including materiality), and the distinction between fraud and error.

## Syllabus items taught here
- ASR.1a - Concept of assurance
- ASR.1b - Why users desire assurance reports
- ASR.1c - Functions and responsibilities of parties involved
- ASR.1d - Expectation gap
- ASR.1e - Planning an assurance engagement and materiality
- ASR.1f - Reporting to the engaging party
- ASR.1g - Professional scepticism and judgement
- ASR.1h - Fraud versus error
- ASR.1i - Assurance over sustainability reporting

## How to teach this
Ask: a bank is about to lend £2m based on a company's accounts. Why might it insist those accounts are audited first, rather than just trusting the directors? Have the learner predict the result of every example before running it, and run code for real whenever they can.

#### ASR.1a Concept of assurance
**Assurance** is a professional's independent opinion on whether a subject matter (typically financial statements, but increasingly non-financial information such as sustainability reporting) is free from material misstatement, expressed to increase the confidence of intended users. **Reasonable assurance** (the level given by a statutory audit) is high but not absolute, expressed positively ('in our opinion, the financial statements give a true and fair view'); **limited assurance** (e.g. a review engagement) is a lower, negatively-worded level ('nothing has come to our attention to suggest...'), reflecting less extensive procedures.

#### ASR.1b Why users desire assurance reports
**Why users want assurance**: users (shareholders, lenders, HMRC, employees, suppliers) are often separated from management (the people who actually prepare the information) and cannot verify it themselves; assurance reduces the risk they rely on materially misstated information, addressing the inherent conflict of interest, remoteness, complexity and consequence of the information to the user.

#### ASR.1c Functions and responsibilities of parties involved
**Parties to an assurance engagement**: the **practitioner** (who gives the opinion), the **responsible party** (who prepares the subject matter, e.g. the directors), and the **intended user(s)** (who rely on the opinion, e.g. shareholders). The **engagement letter** sets out the scope, respective responsibilities and terms before work begins.

#### ASR.1d Expectation gap
**The expectation gap** is the difference between what users believe an assurance engagement (especially an audit) provides and what it actually provides - e.g. some users wrongly believe an audit guarantees no fraud exists, or guarantees the company will not fail; it is narrowed by clearer audit reports, professional standards, and better public understanding of assurance's inherent limitations.

#### ASR.1e Planning an assurance engagement and materiality
**Planning an engagement**: the practitioner agrees the engagement terms, understands the entity and its environment, assesses risk (see ASR_S02), and sets **materiality** - the threshold above which a misstatement could reasonably be expected to influence users' decisions. Materiality is judgemental, often benchmarked as a percentage of a relevant base (e.g. profit before tax, revenue, or total assets), and performance materiality is normally set lower than overall materiality to reduce the risk that the aggregate of small, undetected misstatements exceeds it. *Worked example*: profit before tax is £840,000. Using a common benchmark of 5% of profit before tax, materiality = £42,000.

#### ASR.1f Reporting to the engaging party
**Reporting**: the assurance report is addressed to the engaging party (often the shareholders, for a statutory audit) and states the practitioner's conclusion clearly, distinguishing an **unmodified/unqualified opinion** (no material misstatement found) from a **modified/qualified opinion** (issues found, ranging from a qualified opinion for a material-but-not-pervasive issue, to an adverse opinion or disclaimer of opinion for more serious cases).

#### ASR.1g Professional scepticism and judgement
**Professional scepticism and professional judgement**: professional scepticism is an attitude that includes a questioning mind, being alert to conditions indicating possible misstatement (whether due to fraud or error), and critically assessing evidence rather than simply accepting management's explanations; professional judgement is the application of relevant training, knowledge and experience to make informed decisions about the appropriate course of action in the circumstances of the engagement.

#### ASR.1h Fraud versus error
**Fraud versus error**: both cause a misstatement, but **fraud** is intentional (deception for personal or corporate gain - e.g. management override of controls to inflate profit, or employee theft with cover-up), while **error** is unintentional (a genuine mistake, e.g. a miscalculation or misapplication of an accounting policy). Fraud is inherently harder to detect than error because it is deliberately concealed.

#### ASR.1i Assurance over sustainability reporting
**Sustainability assurance**: as sustainability reporting grows (see the Business, Technology and Finance module), assurance over it is increasingly demanded so that non-financial disclosures (e.g. carbon emissions data) carry the same credibility as financial statements; the appropriate level of assurance (reasonable or limited) depends on the maturity of the underlying data and measurement systems, which for many sustainability metrics is less developed than for financial reporting.
