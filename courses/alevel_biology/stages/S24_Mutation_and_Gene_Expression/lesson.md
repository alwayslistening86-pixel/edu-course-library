# S24_Mutation_and_Gene_Expression - Lesson: Gene mutations, stem cells, regulation of gene expression and cancer

## Goal
The learner explains gene mutations and their effects, stem cells (totipotent, pluripotent, multipotent, unipotent; iPS cells and their uses), transcription factors and oestrogen, epigenetics (methylation, acetylation), RNA interference, and the roles of tumour suppressor genes, oncogenes, methylation and oestrogen in cancer.

## How to teach this
Ask how every cell in your body has the same DNA, yet a nerve cell and a skin cell are so different. Teach from structure to function: what the molecule, cell or organ is like, how that lets it work, then an example or data to interpret. Use AQA's precise terms (e.g. 'water potential', 'complementary', 'tertiary structure') and insist on them in answers. #### 3.8.1 Alteration of the sequence of bases in DNA can alter the structure of proteins (A-level only)
Gene mutations: addition, deletion, substitution, inversion, duplication and translocation of bases. Mutations occur spontaneously, and their rate is increased by mutagenic agents. Some mutations change the amino acid sequence; this may change the tertiary structure of the protein (and the shape of an active site); additions and deletions cause a frame shift; because of degeneracy, some substitutions have no effect.

#### 3.8.2.1 Most of a cell’s DNA is not translated (A-level only)
Totipotent cells (early embryo) can divide and produce any type of body cell; during development they translate only part of their DNA, becoming specialised. Pluripotent cells (embryonic stem cells) can form any tissue but not whole organisms; multipotent (adult stem cells, e.g. in bone marrow) form a limited number of cell types; unipotent form only one (e.g. cardiomyocytes). Stem cells could treat human disorders (e.g. bone marrow transplants, and potentially diabetes or spinal injury). Induced pluripotent stem cells (iPS cells) are produced from adult somatic cells using appropriate protein transcription factors; they avoid some ethical issues of embryonic stem cells.

#### 3.8.2.2 Regulation of transcription and translation (A-level only)
Transcription of genes is stimulated or inhibited by specific transcription factors that move from the cytoplasm into the nucleus and bind to the promoter region. Oestrogen: a lipid-soluble steroid hormone, diffuses through the membrane and binds a receptor on a transcription factor, changing its shape so it can bind DNA and initiate transcription. Epigenetics: heritable changes in gene function without changes to the base sequence, caused by environmental changes that inhibit transcription: increased methylation of DNA (methyl groups added to cytosine) or decreased acetylation of associated histones (making chromatin more condensed). RNA interference: small interfering RNA (siRNA) or microRNA binds to complementary mRNA, which is then broken down (or its translation blocked), inhibiting translation.

#### 3.8.2.3 Gene expression and cancer (A-level only)
Tumours may be benign (slow-growing, encapsulated, non-invasive) or malignant (grow rapidly, invade tissues, spread by metastasis). Tumour suppressor genes slow cell division and cause apoptosis; mutations or abnormal hypermethylation inactivate them. Proto-oncogenes stimulate division; mutations make them oncogenes, permanently activated; hypomethylation can activate them. Increased oestrogen concentrations are linked to some breast cancers (oestrogen activates genes promoting division). Evaluate evidence on correlations between genetic and environmental factors and cancer, and the role of screening.
