# S08_Gas_Exchange - Lesson: Surface area to volume ratio and gas exchange

## Goal
The learner relates surface area to volume ratio to exchange and metabolism, explains gas exchange in single-celled organisms, insects, fish and plant leaves (with xerophytic adaptations) and in humans (ventilation, alveoli, lung disease data), estimates results and calculates areas and volumes.

## Syllabus items taught here
- 3.3.1 - Surface area to volume ratio
- 3.3.2 - Gas exchange
- MS 0.4 - Estimate results
- MS 4.1 - Calculate the circumferences, surface areas and volumes of regular shapes

## How to teach this
Ask why an elephant needs lungs but an amoeba doesn't. Teach from structure to function: what the molecule, cell or organ is like, how that lets it work, then an example or data to interpret. Use AQA's precise terms (e.g. 'water potential', 'complementary', 'tertiary structure') and insist on them in answers. Every numerical answer and statistic here was computed when the course was built.

#### 3.3.1 Surface area to volume ratio
The size and metabolic rate of an organism affect the amount of each material exchanged. As organisms get larger, the surface area to volume ratio falls, so diffusion across the body surface is too slow; larger organisms have specialised exchange surfaces and mass transport. Smaller animals have a larger SA:V and lose heat faster, so they have higher metabolic rates. *Example:* a cube of side 1 cm has SA:V = 6:1; side 2 cm: 24:8 = 3:1; side 4 cm: 96:64 = 1.5:1.

#### 3.3.2 Gas exchange
Adaptations for efficient exchange: large surface area, thin (short diffusion distance), steep concentration gradient maintained. Single-celled organisms: diffusion across the body surface. Insects: spiracles lead to tracheae and tracheoles, which deliver air directly to tissues; abdominal pumping ventilates; spiracles close to limit water loss (a trade-off). Fish: gills with many filaments and lamellae; counter-current flow (blood and water flow in opposite directions) keeps a diffusion gradient along the whole lamella. Dicotyledonous plant leaves: stomata, air spaces in the spongy mesophyll; xerophytes reduce water loss with thick waxy cuticles, sunken stomata, hairs, rolled leaves (trapping moist air, reducing the water potential gradient). Humans: trachea, bronchi, bronchioles, alveoli; alveolar epithelium one cell thick, capillary endothelium thin, many alveoli; ventilation: inspiration (external intercostal muscles contract, ribs up and out; diaphragm contracts and flattens; volume increases, pressure falls below atmospheric) and expiration (largely passive, with internal intercostals contracting in forced expiration). Pulmonary ventilation = tidal volume x ventilation rate. Interpret data on lung disease (e.g. fibrosis, emphysema, asthma, and risk factors like smoking), distinguishing correlation from causation.

#### MS 0.4 Estimate results
Estimate before calculating: e.g. an adult's pulmonary ventilation at rest is about 0.5 dm^3 x 12 breaths min^-1 ≈ 6 dm^3 min^-1.

#### MS 4.1 Calculate the circumferences, surface areas and volumes of regular shapes
Areas and volumes of regular shapes: circle circumference 2πr, area πr^2; sphere surface area 4πr^2, volume (4/3)πr^3; cylinder surface area 2πr^2 + 2πrh, volume πr^2h. *Example:* a spherical cell of radius 10 μm has SA:V = 3/r = 0.3 μm^-1.
