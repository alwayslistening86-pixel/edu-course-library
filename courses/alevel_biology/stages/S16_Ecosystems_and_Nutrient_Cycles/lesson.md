#### 3.5.3 Energy and ecosystems (A-level only)
Plants synthesise organic compounds from atmospheric or aquatic CO2; most of the sugars are used in respiration, the rest make other biological molecules forming biomass. Biomass can be measured as mass of carbon or dry mass per unit area (or volume) per time; the chemical energy store in dry biomass is estimated by calorimetry. Gross primary production (GPP): the chemical energy store in plant biomass in a given area or volume. Net primary production: NPP = GPP - R (respiratory losses); available for growth and reproduction and for other trophic levels. Net production of consumers: N = I - (F + R) (I = chemical energy in ingested food, F = losses in faeces and urine). Primary and secondary productivity: the rate of production per unit area per unit time (e.g. kJ ha^-1 year^-1). Farming practices increase efficiency of energy transfer: simplifying food webs to reduce energy losses to non-human food chains (pesticides, weeding), and reducing respiratory losses in livestock (restricting movement, keeping warm). *Example:* GPP 22 000 and R 12 000 kJ m^-2 year^-1: NPP = 10000 kJ m^-2 year^-1. Efficiency = energy transferred ÷ energy available x 100%.

#### 3.5.4 Nutrient cycles (A-level only)
Nutrients are recycled within natural ecosystems. Microorganisms (saprobionts) feed on dead organisms and wastes by extracellular digestion, releasing nutrients. Mycorrhizae (fungal associations with roots) increase the surface area for water and mineral absorption. Nitrogen cycle: nitrogen fixation (Rhizobium in root nodules, free-living bacteria) of N2 to ammonia; ammonification (saprobionts produce ammonium from nitrogen compounds in dead organisms and waste); nitrification (NH4+ → nitrite → nitrate, by nitrifying bacteria, needing oxygen); denitrification (nitrate → N2, in anaerobic conditions). Phosphorus cycle: phosphate ions from weathering of rocks, taken up by plants, passed through food chains, returned by decomposition; slow return to rocks by sedimentation. Fertilisers (natural: manure; artificial: inorganic) replace the nitrates and phosphates lost when crops are harvested. Effects of fertilisers: leaching into waterways; eutrophication (algal bloom blocks light, plants below die, saprobionts decompose them and use up dissolved oxygen by respiration, fish die); reduced species diversity.
