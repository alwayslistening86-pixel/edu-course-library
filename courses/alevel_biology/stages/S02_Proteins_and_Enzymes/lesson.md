# S02_Proteins_and_Enzymes - Lesson: Proteins and enzymes

## Goal
The learner describes amino acids, peptide bonds and the four levels of protein structure, the biuret test, the induced-fit model of enzyme action, the factors affecting enzyme activity (including inhibitors), carries out required practical 1, and finds rates from linear graphs and tangents.

## How to teach this
Ask why a fever of 42 °C is dangerous, and link it to the shape of a protein. Teach from structure to function: what the molecule, cell or organ is like, how that lets it work, then an example or data to interpret. Use AQA's precise terms (e.g. 'water potential', 'complementary', 'tertiary structure') and insist on them in answers. Every numerical answer and statistic here was computed when the course was built.

#### 3.1.4.1 General properties of proteins
Amino acids: a central carbon with an amino group (-NH2), a carboxyl group (-COOH), a hydrogen and a variable R group; 20 are common to all organisms. Condensation between two amino acids forms a peptide bond (dipeptide); many form a polypeptide. Primary structure: the sequence of amino acids. Secondary: hydrogen bonds between N-H and C=O give α-helices and β-pleated sheets. Tertiary: further folding held by hydrogen bonds, ionic bonds and disulfide bridges (between R groups), giving a specific 3D shape. Quaternary: more than one polypeptide chain (e.g. haemoglobin). Proteins have many functions (enzymes, antibodies, transport, structure). Biuret test: add biuret reagent (sodium hydroxide and copper(II) sulfate): purple (lilac) shows protein.

#### 3.1.4.2 Many proteins are enzymes
Enzymes lower the activation energy of reactions. Induced-fit model: the active site changes shape slightly as the substrate binds, forming an enzyme-substrate complex and putting strain on bonds; this model replaced the lock-and-key model as evidence showed active sites are flexible. Specificity comes from the tertiary structure of the active site, complementary to one substrate. Factors: temperature (rate rises with kinetic energy and collisions, then falls as hydrogen and ionic bonds break and the active site denatures), pH (changes the charges of R groups in the active site, breaking ionic and hydrogen bonds), enzyme and substrate concentration (more enzyme-substrate complexes, until another factor limits). Competitive inhibitors have a similar shape to the substrate and bind to the active site (effect reduced by more substrate); non-competitive inhibitors bind elsewhere (the allosteric site), changing the active site's shape (effect not reduced by more substrate).

#### RP1 Required practical 1: the effect of a named variable on the rate of an enzyme-controlled reaction
**Method (e.g. temperature and trypsin with milk, or catalase and hydrogen peroxide):** set up water baths at a range of temperatures; equilibrate the enzyme and substrate separately; mix and start timing; measure the time for a change (e.g. milk to clear, measured with a colorimeter or by eye against a cross) or the volume of oxygen produced at intervals (gas syringe or upturned measuring cylinder). Keep pH (buffer), concentrations and volumes constant; repeat at each temperature and calculate means. Rate = 1/time, or the gradient of a product-time graph.

#### MS 3.5 Calculate rate of change from a graph showing a linear relationship
Rate from a straight-line graph = change in y ÷ change in x, with units. *Example:* oxygen produced rises linearly from 0 to 24 cm^3 in 60 s: rate = 0.4 cm^3 s^-1.

#### MS 3.6 Draw and use the slope of a tangent to a curve as a measure of rate of change
For a curve (e.g. product against time), draw a tangent at the chosen time (the initial rate at t = 0), use a large triangle, and give the gradient with units.
