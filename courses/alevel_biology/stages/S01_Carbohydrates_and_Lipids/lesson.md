# S01_Carbohydrates_and_Lipids - Lesson: Monomers, polymers, carbohydrates and lipids

## Goal
The learner explains monomers, polymers, condensation and hydrolysis, describes monosaccharides, disaccharides and polysaccharides (starch, glycogen, cellulose) and their tests, and describes triglycerides and phospholipids and the emulsion test.

## Syllabus items taught here
- 3.1.1 - Monomers and polymers
- 3.1.2 - Carbohydrates
- 3.1.3 - Lipids

## How to teach this
Ask why plants store energy as starch but animals store it as glycogen, and why neither stores it as glucose. Teach from structure to function: what the molecule, cell or organ is like, how that lets it work, then an example or data to interpret. Use AQA's precise terms (e.g. 'water potential', 'complementary', 'tertiary structure') and insist on them in answers. #### 3.1.1 Monomers and polymers
Monomers are the small units from which larger molecules (polymers) are made: monosaccharides, amino acids and nucleotides. A condensation reaction joins two molecules with a chemical bond and eliminates a water molecule; a hydrolysis reaction breaks a chemical bond between two molecules using a water molecule.

#### 3.1.2 Carbohydrates
Monosaccharides: glucose, galactose, fructose. Glucose has two isomers, α-glucose and β-glucose (the OH on carbon 1 is below the ring in α and above in β). Disaccharides form by condensation, making a glycosidic bond: maltose = glucose + glucose; sucrose = glucose + fructose; lactose = glucose + galactose. Polysaccharides: glycogen and starch are polymers of α-glucose (starch: amylose, unbranched and helical, compact; amylopectin, branched), insoluble so they don't affect water potential, and branched forms have many ends for rapid hydrolysis; glycogen is more highly branched (rapid release of glucose for animals' higher metabolic rate). Cellulose is a polymer of β-glucose: alternate molecules are inverted, forming straight, unbranched chains linked by hydrogen bonds into microfibrils, giving strength to plant cell walls. Tests: Benedict's for reducing sugars (heat; blue to green, yellow, orange or brick-red precipitate); non-reducing sugars (sucrose): negative Benedict's, then boil with dilute HCl to hydrolyse, neutralise with NaHCO3 and repeat Benedict's (now positive); iodine in potassium iodide for starch (orange-brown to blue-black).

#### 3.1.3 Lipids
Triglycerides: one glycerol + three fatty acids joined by condensation, forming ester bonds. Fatty acids are saturated (no C=C) or unsaturated (one or more C=C, kinked chains, lower melting point). Properties: a high ratio of C-H bonds releases much energy on oxidation (energy store); insoluble, so they don't affect water potential; low mass for the energy stored. Phospholipids: one fatty acid is replaced by a phosphate-containing group; the head is hydrophilic and the tails hydrophobic, so they form a bilayer in membranes. Emulsion test: shake the sample with ethanol, then add water: a milky-white emulsion shows lipid.
