# S18_Nerve_Impulses_and_Synapses - Lesson: Nerve impulses and synaptic transmission

## Goal
The learner explains the resting potential, the action potential and its propagation (including the all-or-nothing principle, refractory period, myelination and saltatory conduction, and factors affecting speed), and synaptic transmission (cholinergic synapses, summation, inhibition, and drugs).

## Syllabus items taught here
- 3.6.2.1 - Nerve impulses (A-level only)

## How to teach this
Ask how a signal crosses the tiny gap between two neurones when electricity can't jump it. Teach from structure to function: what the molecule, cell or organ is like, how that lets it work, then an example or data to interpret. Use AQA's precise terms (e.g. 'water potential', 'complementary', 'tertiary structure') and insist on them in answers. #### 3.6.2.1 Nerve impulses (A-level only)
A myelinated motor neurone has dendrites, a cell body (with the nucleus), a long axon wrapped in a myelin sheath formed by Schwann cells, gaps in the sheath (nodes of Ranvier) and axon terminals at a neuromuscular junction. Resting potential (about -70 mV): the sodium-potassium pump actively transports 3 Na+ out for every 2 K+ in; the membrane is more permeable to K+, which diffuses out; the inside is negative. Action potential: a stimulus opens voltage-gated Na+ channels; Na+ diffuses in (depolarisation to about +40 mV); Na+ channels close and voltage-gated K+ channels open; K+ diffuses out (repolarisation), overshooting (hyperpolarisation) before the resting potential is restored. All-or-nothing: an action potential occurs only if the threshold is reached, and is always the same size; a stronger stimulus gives more frequent impulses. Refractory period: new action potentials can't be generated immediately, so impulses are discrete, travel in one direction, and their frequency is limited. Propagation: local currents depolarise the next section of membrane. Myelinated axons: depolarisation only at nodes of Ranvier, so impulses jump (saltatory conduction), much faster. Speed also increases with axon diameter (less resistance) and temperature (faster diffusion, up to the point where proteins denature).

#### 3.6.2.2 Synaptic transmission (A-level only)
A synapse: the presynaptic knob (with many mitochondria for ATP, synaptic vesicles containing neurotransmitter, and voltage-gated Ca2+ channels), the synaptic cleft (about 20 nm), and the postsynaptic membrane with specific receptors (ligand-gated Na+ channels). At a cholinergic synapse: the action potential arrives; voltage-gated Ca2+ channels open, Ca2+ diffuses in; vesicles move and fuse with the presynaptic membrane, releasing acetylcholine (ACh) by exocytosis; ACh diffuses across the cleft and binds receptors on the postsynaptic membrane; Na+ channels open, Na+ enters, depolarising it; if threshold is reached, an action potential results. Acetylcholinesterase hydrolyses ACh (to choline and ethanoic acid), which are reabsorbed and resynthesised. Synapses are unidirectional (receptors only on the postsynaptic side). Summation: spatial (several presynaptic neurones) and temporal (repeated impulses from one). Inhibitory synapses cause Cl- to enter and K+ to leave the postsynaptic neurone, hyperpolarising it. The neuromuscular junction is similar but between a motor neurone and a muscle fibre: it is always excitatory, uses ACh, and the postsynaptic membrane (sarcolemma) is folded to give more receptors, with acetylcholinesterase in the clefts. Drugs can mimic neurotransmitters, block receptors, inhibit breakdown, or alter release; predict their effects.
