#### 3.7.4 Populations in ecosystems (A-level only)
Populations of different species form a community; a community and the non-living components form an ecosystem. Within a habitat, each species occupies a niche governed by adaptation to biotic and abiotic conditions. An ecosystem supports a certain size of population (the carrying capacity), affected by variation in abiotic factors, interspecific competition, intraspecific competition, and predation. Population size can be estimated using: randomly placed quadrats (random coordinates) or quadrats along a belt transect, for slow-moving or non-motile organisms (percentage cover, frequency, density); mark-release-recapture for motile organisms: estimated population = (number in first sample x number in second sample) ÷ number of marked individuals recaptured, assuming the marks don't affect survival and are not lost, marked individuals mix randomly, and there are no births, deaths or migration. *Example:* 64 marked, 72 caught later, 9 marked: N = 512. Succession: pioneer species colonise; they change the abiotic conditions (e.g. forming soil), making the environment more suitable for other species that out-compete them; biodiversity increases, leading to a climax community. Conservation of habitats frequently involves management of succession (e.g. grazing or burning to maintain heathland).

#### RP12 Required practical 12: the effect of a named environmental factor on the distribution of a given species
**Method:** a belt transect from one area to another (e.g. shade to open ground, or across a sand dune system) with quadrats at regular intervals, measuring the environmental factor (light meter, soil moisture or pH probe) and the abundance of the species (percentage cover or count) in each; or random quadrats in two areas with different conditions, compared with a statistical test (e.g. Spearman's rank for correlation). Many quadrats improve reliability.

#### MS 1.5 Understand the principles of sampling as applied to scientific data
Sampling: random sampling (coordinates from random number tables) avoids bias; systematic sampling (transects) is used where conditions change across an area; take a large enough number of samples for a representative result.

#### MS 0.5 Use calculators to find and use power, exponential and logarithmic functions
Calculators: powers and logs. Bacterial populations growing exponentially: N = N0 x 2^n after n generations. *Example:* 100 bacteria dividing every 20 minutes for 4 hours (12 generations): 409,600 bacteria; log10 of that is 5.61.

#### MS 2.5 Use logarithms in relation to quantities that range over several orders of magnitude
Log scales: exponential growth plotted on a log scale is a straight line; population sizes spanning 10^2 to 10^9 fit on one axis. Each unit on a log10 scale is a factor of 10.
