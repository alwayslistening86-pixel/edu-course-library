# S22_Populations_Evolution_Speciation - Test: Populations, Hardy-Weinberg, evolution and speciation

## How to run this
A real checkpoint in the style of AQA's papers: structured questions with marks shown (and some multiple choice). Give the whole test at once, with no hints; the learner shows working and may use a calculator. Then mark against the mark schemes below and this stage's entry in `rubric.json`.

## Test items
1. In a population of 5000 plants, 450 have white flowers (recessive, rr). Calculate the number of heterozygous plants, stating one assumption. [4 marks]
2. Explain how allopatric speciation occurs. [5 marks]
3. Explain why genetic drift has a greater effect in small populations. [2 marks]
4. Define sympatric speciation and give an example of a mechanism that causes it. [2 marks]
5. Describe disruptive selection and its effect on a population. [2 marks]

## Answer key (for the tutor only)
1. [4] M1 q^2 = 450/5000 = 0.09, q = 0.3; M1 p = 0.7, 2pq = 0.42; A1 2100 plants; B1 e.g. random mating/no selection/large population/no migration or mutation.
2. [5] B1 populations geographically isolated (e.g. by a mountain range or water); B1 no gene flow between them; B1 different selection pressures/mutations/genetic drift; B1 allele frequencies change differently; B1 eventually they can't interbreed to produce fertile offspring (reproductive isolation).
3. [2] B1 changes in allele frequency happen by chance (random) rather than selection; B1 in a small population chance events change the proportions of alleles more.
4. [2] B1 speciation in the same area: reproductive isolation without geographical separation; B1 e.g. a mutation changing courtship behaviour/flowering time, or polyploidy, preventing interbreeding.
5. [2] B1 both extreme phenotypes are favoured over the intermediate; B1 may lead to two distinct groups (and possibly speciation).
