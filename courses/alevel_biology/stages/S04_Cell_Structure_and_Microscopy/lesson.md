# S04_Cell_Structure_and_Microscopy - Lesson: Eukaryotic and prokaryotic cells, viruses and methods of studying cells

## Goal
The learner describes the structure and function of eukaryotic organelles, compares prokaryotic cells and viruses, explains magnification, resolution, cell fractionation and ultracentrifugation, and handles units, standard form and orders of magnitude.

## How to teach this
Ask why an electron microscope can show ribosomes when a light microscope never can, however much it magnifies. Teach from structure to function: what the molecule, cell or organ is like, how that lets it work, then an example or data to interpret. Use AQA's precise terms (e.g. 'water potential', 'complementary', 'tertiary structure') and insist on them in answers. Every numerical answer and statistic here was computed when the course was built.

#### 3.2.1.1 Structure of eukaryotic cells
Eukaryotic organelles and functions: cell-surface membrane; nucleus (nuclear envelope, pores, nucleolus, chromatin: holds DNA); mitochondria (double membrane, cristae, matrix: aerobic respiration); chloroplasts in plants and algae (double membrane, thylakoids in grana, stroma: photosynthesis); Golgi apparatus and vesicles (modify and package proteins and lipids, make lysosomes); lysosomes (hydrolytic enzymes); ribosomes (80S in cytoplasm or on RER; protein synthesis); rough endoplasmic reticulum (protein synthesis and transport) and smooth ER (lipid synthesis); cell wall (cellulose in plants and algae, chitin in fungi); cell vacuole (in plants: tonoplast, cell sap, support). In multicellular organisms, cells are specialised, and organised into tissues, organs and organ systems. A specialised cell's structure reflects its function (e.g. many mitochondria in muscle cells).

#### 3.2.1.2 Structure of prokaryotic cells and of viruses
Prokaryotic cells are much smaller, with cytoplasm lacking membrane-bound organelles; smaller (70S) ribosomes; no nucleus: a single circular DNA molecule, free in the cytoplasm and not associated with proteins; a murein (peptidoglycan) cell wall. Some have plasmids, a capsule and flagella. Viruses are acellular and non-living: genetic material (DNA or RNA), a capsid, attachment proteins, and some have a lipid envelope and enzymes such as reverse transcriptase.

#### 3.2.1.3 Methods of studying cells
Magnification = image size ÷ actual size. Resolution: the minimum distance apart at which two objects can be distinguished. Optical microscopes: resolution limited by the wavelength of light (about 0.2 μm); electron microscopes use electrons with shorter wavelength: transmission EM (TEM, thin specimens, highest resolution, 2D internal detail) and scanning EM (SEM, 3D surface images, lower resolution); both need a vacuum, so specimens are dead, and preparation may create artefacts. Graticule and stage micrometer: calibrate the eyepiece graticule at each objective. Cell fractionation: homogenise tissue in a cold (reduces enzyme activity), isotonic (prevents osmotic damage to organelles) and buffered (prevents protein denaturation) solution; filter; ultracentrifugation: spin at increasing speeds, removing the pellet each time: nuclei first, then mitochondria (and chloroplasts), then ribosomes. *Example:* an image 36 mm long of a cell 12 μm long: magnification = 36 000 μm/12 μm = x3000.

#### MS 0.1 Recognise and make use of appropriate units in calculations
Units: 1 mm = 1000 μm; 1 μm = 1000 nm. Convert to the same unit before calculating magnification.

#### MS 0.2 Recognise and use expressions in decimal and standard form
Standard form: a mitochondrion of 2 μm = 2 x 10^-6 m; a ribosome of 25 nm = 2.5 x 10^-8 m.

#### MS 1.8 Make order of magnitude calculations
Orders of magnitude: a eukaryotic cell (about 10^-5 m) is about 10 times the length of a bacterium (10^-6 m) and about 100 to 1000 times that of a virus (10^-7 to 10^-8 m).
