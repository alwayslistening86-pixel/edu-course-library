# S07_Immune_System - Lesson: Cell recognition and the immune system

## Goal
The learner explains antigens and cell recognition, phagocytosis, the cellular and humoral responses (T and B cells, antibodies), primary and secondary responses, vaccination, herd immunity and ethics, monoclonal antibodies and ELISA, HIV and why antibiotics don't work against viruses.

## How to teach this
Ask why you catch colds again and again but chickenpox usually only once. Teach from structure to function: what the molecule, cell or organ is like, how that lets it work, then an example or data to interpret. #### 3.2.4 Cell recognition and the immune system
Each cell has specific molecules on its surface (usually proteins), antigens, that identify it; the immune system recognises pathogens, cells from other organisms of the same species, abnormal body cells and toxins. Phagocytosis: a phagocyte engulfs a pathogen into a phagosome; a lysosome fuses with it and hydrolytic enzymes (lysozymes) destroy it; the phagocyte presents the antigens (antigen-presenting cell). Cellular response: helper T cells with complementary receptors bind the presented antigen and are stimulated to divide; they stimulate cytotoxic T cells (which kill infected cells by producing perforin), B cells and phagocytes. Humoral response: B cells with complementary antibodies on their surface take in the antigen and present it; helper T cells activate them; clonal selection and division form plasma cells (secrete antibodies) and memory cells. Antibodies are proteins, made by plasma cells, that bind specifically to antigens. Structure: four polypeptide chains (two heavy, two light) held together by disulfide bridges; a constant region (the same in all antibodies of that class) and two variable regions forming the antigen-binding sites, whose shape is complementary to one antigen. They form antigen-antibody complexes, causing agglutination and marking pathogens for phagocytosis. The primary response is slow; memory cells give a faster, larger secondary response. Active immunity (exposure or vaccination; memory cells) vs passive immunity (antibodies given or from the mother; no memory, short-lived). Vaccines contain antigens; herd immunity protects those not vaccinated; there are ethical issues in testing and using vaccines and monoclonal antibodies. Antigenic variability means new vaccines are needed (e.g. influenza). Monoclonal antibodies (from one clone of plasma cells) target specific cells (drugs attached to target cancer cells), and are used in medical diagnosis and pregnancy tests; ELISA uses antibodies and an enzyme-linked colour change to detect antigens or antibodies (direct and indirect), with washing to remove unbound antibody. HIV: structure (RNA, reverse transcriptase, capsid, envelope, attachment proteins); it infects helper T cells, and replication inside them destroys them, leading to AIDS (opportunistic infections). Antibiotics are ineffective against viruses because viruses lack the cell structures (e.g. murein walls) that antibiotics target.
