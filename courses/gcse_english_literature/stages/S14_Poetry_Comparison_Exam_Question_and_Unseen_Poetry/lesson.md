# S14_Poetry_Comparison_Exam_Question_and_Unseen_Poetry - Lesson: Writing the Paper 2 poetry comparison question, and approaching unseen poetry alone and in comparison

## Goal
The learner can write a full comparative response to a named Power and Conflict poem, and can independently analyse and compare unseen poems never studied before.

## Syllabus items taught here
- POE.EX - Poetry: the Paper 2 comparison exam question and a model paragraph: compare a named poem with a self-selected second poem in the AQA format
- UNS.1 - Unseen poetry: approaching a single unseen poem (24 marks, AO1+AO2): respond to and analyse an unfamiliar poem cold, under time pressure
- UNS.2 - Unseen poetry: comparing two unseen poems on methods (8 marks, AO2 only): compare how two unfamiliar poets achieve their effects, without content marks
- UNS.3 - Unseen poetry: strategies for unfamiliar vocabulary, form and allusion under time pressure: use structural and contextual clues to make sense of a poem with no prior knowledge

## How to teach this
Give the learner a short poem they have never seen before (invent a simple four-line verse about, for example, a lighthouse) and ask them to say what it seems to be about and how they know, before any formal technique is taught -- this shows that unseen poetry is a confidence exercise as much as a knowledge one.

#### POE.EX Poetry: the Paper 2 comparison exam question and a model paragraph
The question prints one named poem from the studied cluster and asks the learner to compare it with one other poem from the same anthology, self-selected, on a given focus (for example 'how power is presented'). Model opening for comparing Ozymandias with Kamikaze on power: 'Both poems present power as something that can turn against those who hold or once held it: Shelley's ruler is reduced to a broken statue, his boastful words now ironic, while Garland's pilot loses social power and belonging for the one time he chooses not to submit to duty's power over him.' Teach the pattern: state the shared idea first, then weave both poems together point by point (never poem one in full, then poem two), closing on how each poem's form supports its version of the idea.

#### UNS.1 Unseen poetry: approaching a single unseen poem (24 marks, AO1+AO2)
With no prior knowledge, teach a fixed first-read method: read the poem twice, once for basic meaning (what is literally happening, who is speaking, to whom) and once noting anything that stands out (an unusual word, a shift in tone, a repeated image), before planning. Build a response the same way as a studied poem: personal response with reference (AO1) plus language/form/structure analysis with effect (AO2), but without any context marks, since AO3 does not apply to unseen poetry. Teach the learner that unseen poetry rewards close, confident reading of what is actually on the page over anxious guessing at hidden meanings the poem may not contain.

#### UNS.2 Unseen poetry: comparing two unseen poems on methods (8 marks, AO2 only)
This second, shorter question asks only for a comparison of how the two poets achieve their effects (form, structure, language), not what the poems are about in content terms, and carries no marks for personal response or context. Teach a fast, method-focused approach: identify one clear formal or technical similarity or difference (for example, one poem uses regular rhyme while the other uses free verse) and explain briefly what effect each choice has, rather than attempting full separate analyses of both poems under time pressure. Because it is worth only 8 marks in a short time, teach the learner to aim for two or three sharp comparative points rather than an exhaustive account.

#### UNS.3 Unseen poetry: strategies for unfamiliar vocabulary, form and allusion under time pressure
When a poem contains an unfamiliar word, allusion or reference, teach the learner to use surrounding context (the rest of the sentence or stanza) to infer a plausible meaning rather than freezing, since markers reward sensible, text-supported inference over silence or invented detail. If a poem's form is unfamiliar (not a sonnet or ballad the learner recognises), teach them to describe what they can observe directly -- stanza length, rhyme pattern present or absent, line length -- rather than naming a form incorrectly. Teach calm triage under time pressure: a confident, well-supported response to what is clearly present in the poem outperforms a nervous attempt to display knowledge the poem does not actually require.
