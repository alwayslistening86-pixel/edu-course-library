# S02_Core_Literary_Essay_Skills - Lesson: The transferable skills every literature essay needs: quotation, language analysis, structure analysis, context, comparison, planning and accuracy

## Goal
The learner can apply seven core essay skills to any set text before those skills are practised on Macbeth, A Christmas Carol, An Inspector Calls and the poetry anthology.

## Syllabus items taught here
- 3.3.1 - Skill: selecting and integrating well-chosen quotation and reference: embed brief, precise evidence rather than long or generic quotation
- 3.3.2 - Skill: analysing language devices and their effect: name a device, quote briefly, and explain its effect on meaning or reader
- 3.3.3 - Skill: analysing form and structure: explain how a text's shape (not just its words) creates meaning
- 3.3.4 - Skill: writing about context purposefully: fold context into an argument about meaning, not a separate history paragraph
- 3.3.5 - Skill: comparing two texts or two poems: sustain a genuine comparison across a whole response, not a two-halves answer
- 3.3.6 - Skill: planning and structuring an essay response: build a four/five-point plan before writing under time pressure
- 3.3.7 - Skill: technical accuracy in extended essay writing: write accurately at speed without losing argument quality

## How to teach this
Give the learner one plain sentence about a text they know nothing about yet (invent one: 'The old king wept when he saw the empty throne') and ask them to turn it into an analytical sentence using at least one of the skills below, before naming which skill they used.

#### 3.3.1 Skill: selecting and integrating well-chosen quotation and reference
The strongest evidence is short (a handful of words), precisely chosen (it proves exactly the point being made, not something roughly nearby) and grammatically folded into the essay's own sentence rather than dropped in awkwardly. Compare a weak version ('Scrooge is described as "a squeezing, wrenching, grasping, scraping, clutching, covetous old sinner" which shows he is mean') with a strong one ('Dickens's relentless list of grasping verbs presents Scrooge's meanness as compulsive, almost physical'). Teach the rule: quote only the words doing the work, and always follow with analysis, never let a quotation end a paragraph unexplained.

#### 3.3.2 Skill: analysing language devices and their effect
Teach the fixed three-step method: name the device (metaphor, sibilance, verb choice, dialect spelling), give the brief textual example, then explain the effect on meaning, tone or audience -- never stop after naming or quoting. A weak analysis says 'the writer uses imagery'; a strong one says 'the writer uses imagery of decay to suggest that power, however grand, cannot last.' Encourage precision over quantity: one device explained in depth outscores four devices merely listed.

#### 3.3.3 Skill: analysing form and structure
Form is the kind of text and its conventions (a five-act tragedy, a dramatic monologue, a three-stanza poem); structure is how ideas, scenes or stanzas are ordered and shaped -- what comes first, what shifts, how it ends, and why. Teach the learner to ask three questions of any text: what does it open with and why; where does a turn or shift happen; how does the ending relate to the beginning (does it resolve, echo or subvert it)? Structural analysis is often the weakest area in student essays, so make it a deliberate, separate step in every plan, not an afterthought.

#### 3.3.4 Skill: writing about context purposefully
Context should answer 'why does this matter to the text's meaning', not 'what do I know about this period'. Teach the sentence pattern 'because [context], [character/writer] presents [idea] as...', which forces context to serve an argument rather than sit beside it. A common error is a standalone context paragraph reciting facts with no link back to the question; teach the learner to weave one context clause into an existing analytical sentence instead of adding a separate block.

#### 3.3.5 Skill: comparing two texts or two poems
Genuine comparison discusses both texts together within each point (similarity or difference on one idea, in one paragraph), not text one in full followed by text two in full. Teach comparative connectives (similarly, in contrast, whereas, both writers) and the discipline of building each paragraph around a shared idea (how each poet presents the physical experience of conflict, for instance) rather than a shared poem. This skill underpins the poetry anthology question and the second unseen poetry question, both of which specifically reward comparison, not two separate mini-essays.

#### 3.3.6 Skill: planning and structuring an essay response
Under exam time pressure, a rushed unplanned essay drifts and repeats itself. Teach a fast plan (3-5 minutes): a one-line overall argument answering the question directly, then three or four points that develop that argument, each with a rough piece of evidence attached before writing starts. Teach the learner that a visible, even scribbled plan is worth the time because it prevents the single biggest cause of lost marks -- an essay that loses its own argument halfway through.

#### 3.3.7 Skill: technical accuracy in extended essay writing
Beyond the two AO4-marked questions, accurate spelling and controlled sentence structures make every essay easier to read and therefore easier to mark generously. Teach a short list of commonly misspelled essay words and names to get automatically right (definitely, believe, Macbeth, Priestley, hierarchy, presents, suggests) and the habit of varying sentence openings and lengths rather than starting every sentence 'This shows...'. Teach proofreading as a specific final pass, separate from checking content, run in the last two minutes of any timed essay.

## Explicitly not here
Applying these seven skills to the actual set texts, which begins with Macbeth in the next stage.
