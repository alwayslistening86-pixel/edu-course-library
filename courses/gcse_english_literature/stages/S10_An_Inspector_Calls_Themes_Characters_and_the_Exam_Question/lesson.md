# S10_An_Inspector_Calls_Themes_Characters_and_the_Exam_Question - Lesson: An Inspector Calls: themes of responsibility, class and generational change, the play's key characters, and the Paper 2 exam question

## Goal
The learner can discuss the play's major themes and characters with textual support and write a Paper 2 Section A response using minimal, precise reference to this in-copyright text.

## Syllabus items taught here
- IC.TH1 - An Inspector Calls theme: social responsibility and collective guilt: explain the Inspector's central message using each character's share of responsibility
- IC.TH2 - An Inspector Calls theme: class and inequality: contrast how different characters treat Eva Smith because of her class
- IC.TH3 - An Inspector Calls theme: generational change, old versus young: contrast the older and younger Birlings' reactions to the Inspector
- IC.CH1 - An Inspector Calls character: Inspector Goole: explain the Inspector's dramatic function as a moral device, not a realist character
- IC.CH2 - An Inspector Calls character: Mr and Mrs Birling: explain what the older Birlings represent and how they resist change
- IC.CH3 - An Inspector Calls character: Sheila and Eric: explain how the younger Birlings develop across the play
- IC.CH4 - An Inspector Calls character: Gerald Croft and the absent presence of Eva Smith: explain Gerald's ambiguous position and Eva's symbolic function despite never appearing
- IC.LANG - An Inspector Calls: dramatic devices (dramatic irony, stage directions, lighting): analyse Priestley's stagecraft and its effect on an audience
- IC.EX - An Inspector Calls: the Paper 2 extract-based exam question and a model paragraph: answer an extract-plus-whole-play modern-text question in the AQA format, quoting minimally as the text is in copyright

## How to teach this
Ask the learner: which Birling changes the most by the end of the play, and which changes least? Their answer opens directly into the generational theme and character work below. Remind them throughout: no quotation over a few words for this text.

#### IC.TH1 An Inspector Calls theme: social responsibility and collective guilt
The Inspector's investigation method -- treating each character's small choice as one link in a single chain that ends in Eva Smith's death -- is designed to prove that no one's individual contribution can be dismissed as insignificant. Priestley structures the play to argue directly against Mr Birling's opening speech about self-interested individualism, showing its consequences played out concretely through one woman's ruined life. Teach the argument that the play does not present guilt as equally distributed (some characters' actions are far more serious than others) but insists that everyone questioned bears some genuine responsibility, however small their individual link seems.

#### IC.TH2 An Inspector Calls theme: class and inequality
Eva Smith, a working-class woman with no surname power or wealth, is shown to have no real protection against those above her socially: sacked on a whim, dismissed from another job out of jealousy, judged and denied help by a charity committee, and ultimately abandoned. Each Birling and Gerald exercises casual power over her precisely because of the class gap between them, often without even registering it as significant at the time. Teach the argument that Priestley is not just telling one woman's story but using her as representative of many working-class women vulnerable to the unchecked power of the wealthy.

#### IC.TH3 An Inspector Calls theme: generational change, old versus young
Mr and Mrs Birling remain largely unmoved by the Inspector's revelations, quick to deny responsibility and relieved once they believe the investigation was fake; Sheila and Eric, by contrast, are genuinely changed, accepting responsibility and criticising their parents' refusal to learn. Priestley uses this generational split to suggest hope: the younger generation, shaped by different values, might build the more responsible, collective society the play argues for, even if the older generation cannot change. Teach the learner that Gerald sits ambiguously between these groups, showing some guilt but ultimately siding with the older Birlings' desire to move on.

#### IC.CH1 An Inspector Calls character: Inspector Goole
The Inspector functions less as a realistic police detective than as a dramatic device or moral conscience, his name suggesting 'ghoul' or ghost, and his precise, controlled questioning (one character at a time, refusing to be rushed) designed to extract confession and self-reflection rather than gather evidence conventionally. His mysterious final disappearance and the ambiguity over whether he was ever a real inspector reinforce his symbolic rather than literal function. Teach the learner that essays scoring highly on this character typically discuss his dramatic purpose (forcing self-examination, representing collective conscience) rather than treating him simply as a mystery-plot detective.

#### IC.CH2 An Inspector Calls character: Mr and Mrs Birling
Mr Birling is characterised by pompous self-importance, confident wrong predictions about the future, and a refusal to accept responsibility once the Inspector leaves, representing an older, purely capitalist, self-interested worldview. Mrs Birling is characterised by rigid social snobbery and moral hypocrisy, judging Eva harshly on a charity committee while unaware her own son is implicated, and refusing to soften even once this is revealed. Teach the learner that both characters are used by Priestley to represent an unrepentant older generation whose values the play holds directly responsible for social harm.

#### IC.CH3 An Inspector Calls character: Sheila and Eric
Sheila develops from a spoiled, excitable young woman into the character who most clearly absorbs the Inspector's message, repeatedly warning her parents not to dismiss what has happened and rejecting Gerald's attempt to pretend nothing occurred. Eric, initially presented as unstable and drinking heavily, is revealed as directly responsible for Eva's pregnancy and turns his anger toward his parents' hypocrisy once the full picture emerges, showing real if belated moral growth. Teach the learner that both younger Birlings function as evidence for Priestley's argument that change is possible, contrasted directly against their parents' refusal to change.

#### IC.CH4 An Inspector Calls character: Gerald Croft and the absent presence of Eva Smith
Gerald occupies an ambiguous middle position: he shows some genuine guilt and honesty about his affair with Eva (as Daisy Renton), unlike the Birling parents, but ultimately aligns with them in wanting to dismiss the Inspector's visit as a hoax once doubt is raised. Eva Smith herself never appears on stage, yet her absent presence structures the entire play, each character's account building a composite picture of her -- a deliberate choice that makes her representative of many such women rather than a single individualised character. Teach the learner that her absence is itself analysable: Priestley denies her a voice on stage partly to focus attention on the characters' responsibility rather than her personal story.

#### IC.LANG An Inspector Calls: dramatic devices (dramatic irony, stage directions, lighting)
Dramatic irony is central: the 1945 audience knows Mr Birling's confident predictions (an unsinkable ship, no chance of war) are wrong, undermining his authority on the play's central moral question too. Priestley's detailed stage directions specify lighting that shifts from 'pink and intimate' before the Inspector's arrival to harder, brighter light once he begins questioning, visually marking the shift from comfortable self-deception to uncomfortable scrutiny. Teach the learner that stage directions in a play script carry real analytical weight (lighting, entrances, pauses) and should be treated as authorial technique, just like language choices in dialogue.

#### IC.EX An Inspector Calls: the Paper 2 extract-based exam question and a model paragraph
The question gives a printed extract and asks something like 'Starting with this extract, explore how Priestley presents [responsibility/class/Mr Birling] in An Inspector Calls', requiring analysis of the extract first, then wider references across the whole play, with only minimal quotation since the text is in copyright. Model opening: 'In this extract, Priestley presents Mr Birling's overconfidence through his dismissive tone about the Titanic, a moment of dramatic irony that immediately undermines his authority for the 1945 audience.' Then move outward, using close paraphrase rather than further quotation: 'This same overconfidence resurfaces once the Inspector leaves, when Birling assumes the whole investigation can simply be dismissed, showing he has learned nothing.' Teach the pattern: extract detail first (minimal quotation), then a linked whole-play reference in paraphrase, repeated for each point.
