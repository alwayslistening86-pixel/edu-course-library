# S09_An_Inspector_Calls_Overview_Context_Acts_and_Structure - Lesson: An Inspector Calls: whole-play overview, context, the plot of Acts 1-3, and its cyclical structure

## Goal
The learner can summarise the whole play, explain its dual 1912/1945 context, narrate and explain the significance of each act, and explain the effect of the play's structure.

## How to teach this
Ask the learner: if a play is set in 1912 but was first performed in 1945, right after a world war, what might the audience know that the characters on stage do not? Use their answer to introduce the play's dramatic irony before summarising the plot. Note throughout: An Inspector Calls is in copyright, so quote only single short phrases, never a full line of dialogue.

#### IC.0 An Inspector Calls: whole-play overview and the well-made-play-with-a-twist form
Set in one evening, the wealthy Birling family's celebratory dinner is interrupted by a mysterious Inspector investigating the suicide of a young working-class woman, Eva Smith. Through questioning, each family member (and Gerald Croft, Sheila's fiancé) is revealed to have played a part in a chain of events leading to her death: unfair dismissal, exploitation, betrayal and refusal of help. After the Inspector leaves, the family discovers no such Inspector or suicide is officially recorded, and they begin to relax -- until a phone call announces a real girl has just died and a real inspector is on his way, reopening the whole situation. Teach the shape as a moral trap disguised as a detective plot, structured to force each character (and the audience) to confront responsibility.

#### IC.CTX An Inspector Calls: context (1912 setting versus 1945 first performance, Edwardian class society, Priestley's socialism)
Priestley set the play in 1912, a rigidly class-stratified Edwardian society just before the Titanic's sinking and the First World War -- events the audience of 1945 knew about but the characters on stage confidently dismiss (Mr Birling wrongly calls the Titanic unsinkable and predicts no war). Written and first performed in 1945, just after the Second World War and the same year Britain elected a Labour government committed to a welfare state, the play uses this dramatic irony to argue that the older generation's confident, selfish individualism had already been proven catastrophically wrong by history. Priestley was an open socialist, and the play is often read as direct political persuasion for collective social responsibility.

#### IC.1 An Inspector Calls Act 1: the Birling family and Eva Smith's dismissal
The Birlings celebrate Sheila's engagement to Gerald Croft while Mr Birling delivers a speech praising individual self-interest and dismissing any idea of collective responsibility. The Inspector arrives investigating Eva Smith's suicide and reveals Mr Birling sacked her from his factory for asking for fair wages, establishing him as the first, and in his view least serious, link in the chain of responsibility. Sheila is then shown to have had Eva dismissed from a later job, out of jealousy, for a trivial complaint -- and unlike her father, Sheila immediately shows genuine distress and guilt. Teach this act as establishing the investigation's method: each character is questioned in turn, and their differing reactions (denial versus guilt) begin dividing the family.

#### IC.2 An Inspector Calls Act 2: Sheila, Gerald and Mrs Birling's involvement
Gerald is revealed to have had an affair with Eva Smith (under the name Daisy Renton) while engaged to Sheila, keeping her as a mistress before ending it -- Sheila is hurt but respects his honesty once questioned, unlike her parents' continued denial. Mrs Birling, questioned last in this act, admits she used her influence on a charity committee to deny Eva help when she was pregnant and desperate, judging her harshly and refusing responsibility -- without yet realising the father of that unborn child is her own son, Eric. Teach this act as widening the circle of guilt from individual acts (Act 1) into more sustained, cold moral failure (Mrs Birling's committee decision).

#### IC.3 An Inspector Calls Act 3: Eric's confession and the twist ending
Eric confesses he also had a relationship with Eva, got her pregnant, stole money to support her, and that she refused his stolen money once she realised where it came from -- directly contradicting his mother's committee ruling made without knowing this. The Inspector delivers a final, direct speech about collective responsibility ('we are members of one body... we are responsible for each other') and warns of consequences if this lesson goes unlearned. After he leaves, the family discovers no Inspector by that name is on the police force and no suicide is recorded, and the older generation quickly relaxes -- until the closing phone call reveals a real girl has just died and a real inspector is coming, reopening everything. Teach this ending as deliberately ambiguous and urgent rather than neatly resolved.

#### IC.STRUCT An Inspector Calls: structure (cyclical form, the telephone call twist, controlled timing of revelations)
The play observes the classical unities (one setting, roughly real time, a single continuous action across three acts), which concentrates tension and denies characters any escape from the Inspector's questioning. Priestley controls revelations precisely: each character's guilt is revealed only once the previous one's has been fully explored, building a cumulative, escalating picture rather than revealing everything at once. The structure is cyclical: the play seems to end with the family's relief, then loops back to the same crisis via the final phone call, structurally enacting the Inspector's warning that the lesson must be learned or the same events will simply repeat. Teach this circularity as the play's central structural argument: history, like the plot, will repeat until responsibility is genuinely accepted.
