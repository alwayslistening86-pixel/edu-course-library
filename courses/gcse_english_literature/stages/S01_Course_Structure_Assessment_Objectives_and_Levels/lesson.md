# S01_Course_Structure_Assessment_Objectives_and_Levels - Lesson: How the qualification is assessed: Papers 1 and 2, the four assessment objectives, weightings and the levels-of-response ladder

## Goal
The learner can describe both papers' structure and marks, state all four assessment objectives in their own words, and explain how the six-level ladder decides a mark.

## Syllabus items taught here
- 2.P1 - Paper 1 Shakespeare and the 19th-century novel: structure: know Paper 1 is 1h45, 64 marks, 40% of the GCSE, with Section A Shakespeare (34 marks) and Section B the 19th-century novel (30 marks)
- 2.P2 - Paper 2 Modern texts and poetry: structure: know Paper 2 is 2h15, 96 marks, 60% of the GCSE, with Section A modern texts (34), Section B poetry anthology (30) and Section C unseen poetry (32)
- AO1 - AO1: read, understand and respond to texts, using textual references including quotation: give an informed personal response backed by well-chosen textual reference
- AO2 - AO2: analyse language, form and structure using relevant subject terminology: explain how a writer's methods create meaning, with accurate terminology
- AO3 - AO3: show understanding of the relationships between texts and the contexts in which they were written: use context purposefully to illuminate meaning, not as a bolt-on
- AO4 - AO4: use a range of vocabulary and sentence structures with accurate spelling and punctuation (5% overall, Shakespeare and modern-text questions only): write essays that are technically accurate under exam pressure
- 4.3.1 - AO weightings by paper and overall (AO1 37.5%, AO2 42.5%, AO3 15%, AO4 5%): recall which AOs are weighted where and use it to allocate essay time and effort
- MS.1 - Levels of response: the six-level ladder used to grade every essay: use the level-then-mark process to self-assess an essay

## How to teach this
Draw two boxes on paper, one labelled 'Paper 1' and one 'Paper 2', and ask the learner to guess how the marks split between them before telling them the real numbers (64 vs 96). Ask why they think Paper 2 is worth more (three sections, more texts to know).

#### 2.P1 Paper 1 Shakespeare and the 19th-century novel: structure
Paper 1 is 1 hour 45 minutes, worth 64 marks, 40% of the whole GCSE. It has two sections, both answered from printed extracts on the paper: Section A is one question on the studied Shakespeare play (Macbeth here), worth 34 marks -- 30 marks across AO1/AO2/AO3 plus 4 separate marks for AO4 (technical accuracy); Section B is one question on the studied 19th-century novel (A Christmas Carol here), worth 30 marks with no separate AO4 component. Both questions ask the learner to respond to a printed extract AND to the play or novel as a whole, so revision must cover the entire text, not just famous scenes.

#### 2.P2 Paper 2 Modern texts and poetry: structure
Paper 2 is 2 hours 15 minutes, worth 96 marks, 60% of the whole GCSE. Three sections: Section A is a choice of one essay from two options on the studied modern text (An Inspector Calls here), worth 34 marks (30 + 4 AO4), answered without an extract printed on the paper; Section B is a comparison of one named poem (printed on the paper) with a second, self-chosen poem from the same anthology cluster (Power and Conflict here), worth 30 marks, no AO4; Section C is unseen poetry, two poems never studied before, Question 1 worth 24 marks (a single poem) and Question 2 worth 8 marks (a brief comparison), no AO3 or AO4.

#### AO1 AO1: read, understand and respond to texts, using textual references including quotation
AO1 asks for two things together: a genuine, developed personal response (an argument about what a text means or does, not just a summary of what happens) and textual reference to support every point -- direct quotation where it is short and precise, or close paraphrase where a quotation would be clumsy. AO1 marks are lost by pure narration ('then this happens, then this happens') with no interpretation, and by unsupported assertion ('Scrooge is horrible') with no evidence. Teach the pairing 'point plus proof' as the smallest unit AO1 rewards: never write a claim about a text without immediately grounding it in the text itself.

#### AO2 AO2: analyse language, form and structure using relevant subject terminology
AO2 asks how a writer creates meaning and effect through their craft: word choice, imagery, sentence and line construction (language); the kind of text and its conventions -- verse, prose, dramatic monologue, five-act tragedy (form); and the order and shaping of ideas, scenes or stanzas (structure). Naming a device (metaphor, dramatic irony, caesura) is only the first step; AO2 marks come from explaining its effect on meaning or on an audience/reader. Teach the formula 'device, example, effect' and insist the effect clause always appears, since a list of spotted devices with no explained effect scores poorly.

#### AO3 AO3: show understanding of the relationships between texts and the contexts in which they were written
AO3 rewards context used to illuminate meaning: what a Jacobean audience believed about kingship, what Victorian England's attitude to poverty was, why a play written for 1912 was first staged in 1945. Context is not a set of memorised facts to recite in a separate paragraph; it must be woven into an argument about why the text says what it says. Teach the test: could this context sentence be deleted without weakening the point about the text? If yes, it is decoration, not AO3; if the point collapses without it, it is doing real work.

#### AO4 AO4: use a range of vocabulary and sentence structures with accurate spelling and punctuation
AO4 is only assessed on two questions across the whole qualification -- the Shakespeare question and the modern-text question -- worth 4 marks each out of 34, so 5% of the total GCSE overall. It rewards essay writing that is accurate (correct spelling, including of literary terms and character names) and varied (a mix of sentence lengths and forms used to control emphasis, not just accuracy for its own sake). Teach the learner that AO4 is a small but easy win: proofreading the final two minutes of a Shakespeare or modern-text answer specifically for spelling of key names and terms is high value for low effort.

#### 4.3.1 AO weightings by paper and overall (AO1 37.5%, AO2 42.5%, AO3 15%, AO4 5%)
Across the whole qualification, AO2 (language/form/structure analysis) carries the most weight at 42.5%, then AO1 (response and reference) at 37.5%, then AO3 (context) at 15%, then AO4 (technical accuracy) at 5%. This tells the learner where marks concentrate: an essay heavy on plot summary and light on analysis of technique is leaving the largest pot of marks (AO2) unclaimed. Teach the learner to count sentences in their own practice paragraphs and check roughly how many are doing AO1 work (point and evidence) versus AO2 work (analysing method and effect) versus AO3 (context) -- the balance should lean towards AO1 and AO2.

#### MS.1 Levels of response: the six-level ladder used to grade every essay
Every essay question is marked on a six-level ladder, moving from Level 1 (simple, narrative-based, little textual reference) up to Level 6 (critical, exploratory, a genuinely developed and convincing argument with well-integrated evidence and terminology). A marker first decides which level the whole response best matches as a package (not by adding up separate ticks), then places a precise mark within that level's range depending on how consistently strong the response is. Teach the learner to self-assess practice essays against level descriptors rather than guessing a mark, since understanding what separates Level 3 ('explains') from Level 5 ('analyses effect') is the fastest route to improvement.

## Explicitly not here
The generic essay-writing skills these AOs demand (S02); the texts themselves (later stages).
