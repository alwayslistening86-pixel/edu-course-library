# S11_Power_and_Conflict_Poetry_I_Overview_and_Four_Founding_Poems - Lesson: The Power and Conflict cluster overview, and four poems on power, control and nature's force

## Goal
The learner can explain what unites the cluster and analyse Ozymandias, London, the Prelude extract and My Last Duchess with accurate reference.

## Syllabus items taught here
- POE.0 - Power and Conflict cluster overview: what the cluster explores and how to prepare all 15 poems: explain the cluster's shared concerns and organise revision across all 15 poems
- POE.1 - Poem: Ozymandias (Percy Bysshe Shelley): analyse how Shelley presents the decay of power and human vanity
- POE.2 - Poem: London (William Blake): analyse how Blake presents suffering and oppression in the city
- POE.3 - Poem: extract from The Prelude, stealing the boat (William Wordsworth): analyse how Wordsworth presents nature's power to awe and discipline
- POE.4 - Poem: My Last Duchess (Robert Browning): analyse how Browning presents obsessive control through a dramatic monologue

## How to teach this
Ask the learner: what different kinds of 'power' can you think of (political, physical, personal, natural)? List them together, then explain that all 15 poems in this cluster explore some version of power or conflict, which is why comparison across them is always possible.

#### POE.0 Power and Conflict cluster overview: what the cluster explores and how to prepare all 15 poems
The fifteen poems explore power in many forms: political and military power (Ozymandias, Charge of the Light Brigade), power over other people (My Last Duchess, London), the power of nature (Storm on the Island, Exposure), and the personal, psychological power of conflict's aftermath (Remains, War Photographer, Kamikaze). Teach the learner to build a simple grid while revising: for each poem, note its central idea about power or conflict, one key structural feature, and one or two very short pieces of evidence, so that any two poems can be paired quickly for the comparison question regardless of which one the exam names.

#### POE.1 Poem: Ozymandias (Percy Bysshe Shelley)
A traveller describes a ruined statue in the desert: a shattered stone face with a 'sneer of cold command' beside a pedestal boasting 'Look on my Works, ye Mighty, and despair!' -- surrounded only by empty, endless sand. Shelley presents political power as ultimately temporary and self-defeating: the ruler's arrogant boast is ironically undercut by the total decay around it, since nothing of his empire survives except broken stone. The sonnet form (traditionally about love) is subverted to mock power instead, and the fragmented, irregular rhyme scheme mirrors the statue's own physical fragmentation. Public domain: short direct quotation is fine here.

#### POE.2 Poem: London (William Blake)
Blake's speaker walks through London hearing suffering everywhere: in 'every cry of every Man', in infants' fear, and in the 'mind-forg'd manacles' that trap people psychologically as much as any physical chain. Blake presents power as oppressive and self-perpetuating, blaming institutions (church, monarchy, in the reference to the 'hapless Soldier's sigh' running in blood down palace walls) for suffering that people have also internalised and cannot see past. The relentless repetition of 'every' and the poem's tight, controlled rhyme scheme suggest suffering is both universal across the city and inescapably systematic, not random. Public domain: short direct quotation is fine here.

#### POE.3 Poem: extract from The Prelude, stealing the boat (William Wordsworth)
A boy steals a boat at night to row on a lake; as he rows, a huge peak seems to rise up and 'stride' after him, terrifying him into rowing home, and the memory haunts his mind afterwards with a sense of 'unknown modes of being'. Wordsworth presents nature's power as morally instructive and awe-inducing rather than simply beautiful: the mountain acts almost as a conscience, punishing a small transgression with a lasting, humbling fear. Blank verse (unrhymed) and long, flowing sentences mirror both the boy's initially free rowing and nature's overwhelming, uncontainable scale. Public domain: short direct quotation is fine here.

#### POE.4 Poem: My Last Duchess (Robert Browning)
A Duke, speaking to a visitor arranging his next marriage, shows off a portrait of his previous wife while revealing, apparently without realising it, that he had her killed for smiling too readily at others rather than reserving her favour for him alone. Browning presents personal, controlling power as monstrous and self-incriminating: the dramatic monologue form lets the Duke's own casual, chilling words expose his cruelty to the reader even as he believes he is simply making polite conversation. The poem's flowing enjambment (lines running on without pause) mirrors the Duke's smooth, controlled surface, concealing violent, possessive jealousy beneath. Public domain: short direct quotation is fine here.
