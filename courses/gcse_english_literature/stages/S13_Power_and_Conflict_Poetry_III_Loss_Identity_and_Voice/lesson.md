# S13_Power_and_Conflict_Poetry_III_Loss_Identity_and_Voice - Lesson: Six poems on loss, identity and voice: Poppies, War Photographer, Tissue, The Emigree, Checking Out Me History and Kamikaze

## Goal
The learner can analyse each of these six poems' presentation of loss, memory, identity or power, with accurate, minimal reference respecting copyright.

## Syllabus items taught here
- POE.10 - Poem: Poppies (Jane Weir): analyse how Weir presents a parent's grief and loss through war
- POE.11 - Poem: War Photographer (Carol Ann Duffy): analyse how Duffy presents the photographer's detachment and the public's indifference
- POE.12 - Poem: Tissue (Imtiaz Dharker): analyse how Dharker presents the fragility of power through the extended metaphor of paper
- POE.13 - Poem: The Emigree (Carol Rumens): analyse how Rumens presents memory and an idealised, possibly imagined homeland
- POE.14 - Poem: Checking Out Me History (John Agard): analyse how Agard presents the power of controlling historical narrative through form and dialect
- POE.15 - Poem: Kamikaze (Beatrice Garland): analyse how Garland presents duty, shame and family conflict through perspective shifts

## How to teach this
Ask the learner: which of these six poems might be spoken by someone who was never actually a soldier at all (a parent, a photographer, an exile, a child)? Use this to introduce a set of poems about conflict's effect on people who experience it indirectly or personally rather than in battle.

#### POE.10 Poem: Poppies (Jane Weir)
A parent describes the day their child leaves for the army (or possibly recalls their death), focusing on small, tender domestic details -- smoothing a uniform, a cat's collar's bell -- rather than any battlefield scene. Weir presents a parent's grief and pride as tangled together, using the extended metaphor of releasing a wild bird (which the parent cannot actually do) to express the impossibility of protecting or holding onto a child who has gone. The poem's free verse and fragmented, non-chronological structure reflect grief's refusal to follow an orderly timeline. In-copyright: quote no more than a handful of words at a time.

#### POE.11 Poem: War Photographer (Carol Ann Duffy)
A photographer develops war photographs alone in a darkroom back home, the ordered ritual of his 'spools of suffering' work contrasting with the chaos of what he witnessed abroad, while he anticipates that his editor will select only one image and readers will feel a moment's sympathy before moving on with their lives. Duffy presents public indifference to distant suffering as troubling, and the photographer's professional detachment as both a necessary coping mechanism and something that unsettles him. The contrast between the calm, controlled darkroom setting and the horror of the memories it develops drives the poem's central tension. In-copyright: quote no more than a handful of words at a time.

#### POE.12 Poem: Tissue (Imtiaz Dharker)
Using the extended metaphor of thin, fragile paper -- religious texts, maps, architectural plans, banknotes -- Dharker suggests that even the most solid-seeming structures of human power (borders, money, buildings) are built on something as impermanent and changeable as paper. The poem imagines light passing through paper as a symbol of a more open, flexible kind of power than rigid human structures allow, ending with an image of the human body itself as similarly fragile tissue. Free verse and short, loosely connected stanzas mirror the poem's argument that rigid, fixed structures are less true to life than something adaptable and light. In-copyright: quote no more than a handful of words at a time.

#### POE.13 Poem: The Emigree (Carol Rumens)
A speaker recalls a city she left as a child, describing it in warm, sunlit terms even though the country is now, she admits, troubled by war or oppression -- her memory of it remains bright and untainted regardless of what has actually happened there since. Rumens presents memory and identity as something that can resist political reality, the speaker's idealised homeland functioning almost as a personal, private power that oppressive forces cannot take from her, even as she cannot physically return. Light and sun imagery recur throughout to represent this persistent, possibly imagined, positive memory. In-copyright: quote no more than a handful of words at a time.

#### POE.14 Poem: Checking Out Me History (John Agard)
Agard's speaker, writing partly in Caribbean dialect, criticises a British education that taught him European history and nursery-rhyme figures in detail while omitting Black historical figures such as Toussaint L'Ouverture and Mary Seacole. Agard presents the power to control historical narrative as a form of oppression, and reclaiming and telling one's own history as an act of resistance and self-assertion -- the poem's title itself plays on 'checking out' as both examining critically and withdrawing, like a library book. The mixed use of standard English and dialect, alongside an unconventional, irregular structure, itself performs the poem's resistance to a single imposed 'correct' voice. In-copyright: quote no more than a handful of words at a time.

#### POE.15 Poem: Kamikaze (Beatrice Garland)
A Japanese kamikaze pilot turns back from his suicide mission, drawn by memories of his childhood and the natural beauty of the sea, and the poem then shows the lasting shame and social rejection he faces afterwards from his own family and community for surviving rather than dying as duty demanded. Garland presents conflicting loyalties -- duty and honour versus love of life and family -- and shows that surviving a war can bring its own lasting, painful form of conflict within a community's judgement. The poem is narrated by the pilot's daughter, adding distance and a sense of inherited, second-hand shame passed through a family. In-copyright: quote no more than a handful of words at a time.
