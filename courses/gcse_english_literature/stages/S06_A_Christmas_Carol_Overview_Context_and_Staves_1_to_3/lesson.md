# S06_A_Christmas_Carol_Overview_Context_and_Staves_1_to_3 - Lesson: A Christmas Carol: whole-novella overview, Victorian context, and Staves 1-3

## Goal
The learner can summarise the whole novella and its five-stave structure, explain why its social message mattered in 1843, and narrate and explain the significance of Staves 1-3.

## Syllabus items taught here
- ACC.0 - A Christmas Carol: whole-novella overview, the stave/carol structure: summarise the whole novella's plot and explain its five-stave musical structure
- ACC.CTX - A Christmas Carol: Victorian context (poverty, the Poor Law, workhouses, Dickens's reformist purpose): use 1843 social context to explain why Dickens wrote the novella
- ACC.1 - A Christmas Carol Stave 1: Marley's ghost and Scrooge introduced: narrate and explain the significance of Stave 1
- ACC.2 - A Christmas Carol Stave 2: the Ghost of Christmas Past: narrate and explain the significance of Stave 2
- ACC.3 - A Christmas Carol Stave 3: the Ghost of Christmas Present: narrate and explain the significance of Stave 3

## How to teach this
Ask the learner why Dickens might have called his chapters 'staves' rather than 'chapters'. Let them guess (a stave is a set of musical lines) before explaining that the novella is structured like a piece of music in movements, which frames everything that follows.

#### ACC.0 A Christmas Carol: whole-novella overview, the stave/carol structure
A Christmas Carol is a short novella (a novella, not a full novel) told in five 'staves' instead of chapters -- a stave being a set of musical lines, reinforcing the title's idea of the story as a carol, a song of celebration and moral lesson. Shape: Stave 1 introduces Scrooge and Marley's warning ghost; Staves 2-4 are visited in turn by the Ghosts of Christmas Past, Present and Yet to Come, each showing Scrooge a different view of his life; Stave 5 shows his transformation and redemption on Christmas morning. Teach the shape as a single night's supernatural intervention compressed into one transformative experience, structured like a moral fable with a clear beginning, testing middle and resolved end.

#### ACC.CTX A Christmas Carol: Victorian context (poverty, the Poor Law, workhouses, Dickens's reformist purpose)
Written in 1843, when the New Poor Law (1834) forced the destitute into harsh workhouses rather than giving direct relief, a system Dickens strongly opposed as cruel and dehumanising -- referenced directly when Scrooge suggests the poor should go to workhouses or prisons. Dickens had experienced child poverty himself (his father was imprisoned for debt) and wrote the novella partly as deliberate social campaigning, following a visit to a ragged school for destitute children. The stark contrast the book draws between the wealthy, who could choose generosity, and the poor, who had no such choice, reflects a real and urgent debate in 1840s England about social responsibility for the poor.

#### ACC.1 A Christmas Carol Stave 1: Marley's ghost and Scrooge introduced
Scrooge is introduced through a famous list of miserly qualities -- 'a squeezing, wrenching, grasping, scraping, clutching, covetous old sinner' -- and shown rejecting his nephew's Christmas invitation and turning away charity collectors with the workhouse comment. That night, the ghost of his dead business partner Jacob Marley appears, weighed down by chains forged from his own greed in life, warning Scrooge that he faces the same fate unless he changes, and announcing that three more spirits will visit him. Teach this stave as establishing both Scrooge's present character (through action and description, not just narration) and the supernatural warning mechanism that structures the rest of the book.

#### ACC.2 A Christmas Carol Stave 2: the Ghost of Christmas Past
The Ghost of Christmas Past shows Scrooge scenes from his own younger life: a lonely schoolboy left at school over Christmas; his warm apprenticeship under the generous Fezziwig, whose Christmas party contrasts sharply with Scrooge's present meanness; and the moment his fiancée Belle leaves him because he has come to love money more than her. Scrooge becomes visibly distressed watching these memories, showing that some capacity for feeling still survives beneath his hardened present self. Teach this stave as establishing that Scrooge was not always this way, which is essential to the book's argument that redemption is genuinely possible.

#### ACC.3 A Christmas Carol Stave 3: the Ghost of Christmas Present
The Ghost of Christmas Present, a huge, generous figure surrounded by abundant food, shows Scrooge Christmas as it is currently celebrated by others, especially his clerk Bob Cratchit's poor but loving family, including his fragile son Tiny Tim, and his nephew Fred's warm, forgiving gathering where Scrooge is mocked kindly rather than cruelly. Most strikingly, the ghost reveals two starving children hidden beneath his robe, named Ignorance and Want, warning that Ignorance especially will bring doom if society does not act. Teach this stave as the emotional and moral centre of the book, contrasting the warmth of those with little against Scrooge's cold wealth.
