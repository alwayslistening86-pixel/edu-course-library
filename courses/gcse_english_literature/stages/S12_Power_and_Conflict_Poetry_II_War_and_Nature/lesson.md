# S12_Power_and_Conflict_Poetry_II_War_and_Nature - Lesson: Five poems on war, nature and the soldier's experience: Charge of the Light Brigade, Exposure, Storm on the Island, Bayonet Charge and Remains

## Goal
The learner can analyse each of these five poems' presentation of conflict, nature or its psychological aftermath, with accurate reference respecting each poem's copyright status.

## Syllabus items taught here
- POE.5 - Poem: The Charge of the Light Brigade (Alfred, Lord Tennyson): analyse how Tennyson presents heroism and futility in war
- POE.6 - Poem: Exposure (Wilfred Owen): analyse how Owen presents suffering, boredom and nature as an enemy in the trenches
- POE.7 - Poem: Storm on the Island (Seamus Heaney): analyse how Heaney presents nature's power as a metaphor for fear
- POE.8 - Poem: Bayonet Charge (Ted Hughes): analyse how Hughes presents the disorientation and instinct of a soldier in action
- POE.9 - Poem: Remains (Simon Armitage): analyse how Armitage presents the psychological aftermath of conflict

## How to teach this
Ask the learner to guess, before reading any summary: which of these five poems might be about a real historical battle, and which might be about a soldier's memory long after a war has ended? Use their guesses to sort the five poems into 'during' and 'after' conflict before teaching each one.

#### POE.5 Poem: The Charge of the Light Brigade (Alfred, Lord Tennyson)
Tennyson dramatises a real 1854 cavalry charge into 'the valley of Death', ordered by a mistake, in which soldiers ride to near-certain destruction obeying orders without question ('Theirs not to reason why, / Theirs but to do and die'). Tennyson presents both the horror and futility of the doomed charge and genuine admiration for the soldiers' discipline and courage, refusing to simply condemn the war or simply glorify it. The insistent, galloping dactylic rhythm mimics the physical charge itself, while repetition ('Cannon to right of them, / Cannon to left of them') conveys being surrounded and trapped. Public domain: short direct quotation is fine here.

#### POE.6 Poem: Exposure (Wilfred Owen)
Owen, writing from direct First World War experience, presents soldiers suffering not from enemy action but from the freezing, relentless cold and boredom of exposure, wondering 'what are we doing here?' with no clear answer. Nature itself is presented as an active enemy ('the merciless iced east winds that knive us'), arguably more dangerous than the distant, largely absent human enemy. The repeated, unresolved refrain 'but nothing happens' undercuts any expectation of dramatic battle, instead conveying numbing, purposeless suffering, and the poem's irregular, halting rhythm mirrors the soldiers' physical and mental exhaustion. Public domain: short direct quotation is fine here.

#### POE.7 Poem: Storm on the Island (Seamus Heaney)
A community on an island prepares confidently for a storm, believing their solid houses and lack of trees to overturn make them safe, but the poem's final image of fearing something as apparently harmless as 'a huge nothing' undercuts that confidence entirely. Heaney presents nature's power as something that can be prepared for but never fully controlled, and the poem can be read as an extended metaphor for conflict or threat more broadly, since war-style language ('bombarded', 'strafes') deliberately borrows the vocabulary of conflict for a natural event. In-copyright: quote no more than a handful of words at a time, such as 'huge nothing'.

#### POE.8 Poem: Bayonet Charge (Ted Hughes)
Hughes drops the reader directly into a soldier's disorientating charge mid-action, stripped of any patriotic context or abstract cause -- the soldier's earlier ideals ('king, honour, human dignity') are described as suddenly meaningless, replaced by pure animal instinct and terror. The present-tense, breathless syntax and violent, visceral imagery immerse the reader in the physical chaos of the moment rather than reflecting on it from a safe distance. Hughes presents war as stripping away comforting abstraction to reveal raw, undignified survival instinct as the only reality that remains once battle actually begins. In-copyright: quote no more than a handful of words at a time.

#### POE.9 Poem: Remains (Simon Armitage)
A soldier recalls shooting a possibly unarmed looter during a raid, and the poem tracks how that single moment refuses to stay in the past, resurfacing in memory and dreams long after he has returned home and left the army. Armitage presents the psychological aftermath of conflict (closely resembling post-traumatic stress) as more devastating and long-lasting than the physical danger of combat itself, with the casual, conversational tone of the opening contrasting sharply with the guilt-ridden, fragmented ending. The image of the body's blood staying with him is presented almost as literally inescapable, reflecting memory that will not fade. In-copyright: quote no more than a handful of words at a time.
