# S03_Macbeth_Overview_Context_and_Acts_1_to_3 - Lesson: Macbeth: whole-play overview, Jacobean context, and the plot of Acts 1-3

## Goal
The learner can summarise the whole play, explain why its ideas mattered to a 1606 audience, and narrate and explain the significance of Acts 1-3.

## Syllabus items taught here
- MAC.0 - Macbeth: whole-play overview, plot summary and tragic genre: summarise the whole play's plot and place it in the tragedy genre
- MAC.CTX - Macbeth: Jacobean context (kingship, the Great Chain of Being, witchcraft, the Gunpowder Plot, James I): use Jacobean beliefs to explain why the play's ideas mattered to its first audience
- MAC.1 - Macbeth Act 1: the witches' prophecy and the seed of ambition: narrate and explain the significance of Act 1's key scenes
- MAC.2 - Macbeth Act 2: the murder of Duncan: narrate and explain the significance of Act 2's key scenes
- MAC.3 - Macbeth Act 3: Banquo's murder and the banquet scene: narrate and explain the significance of Act 3's key scenes

## How to teach this
Ask the learner: 'If you had to explain Macbeth in three sentences to someone who had never heard of it, what would you say?' Let them try, then refine together into a tight summary, before explaining that everything studied later hangs off this shape.

#### MAC.0 Macbeth: whole-play overview, plot summary and tragic genre
Macbeth is a tragedy: a noble character brought down by a fatal flaw (his ambition) acting on a supernatural temptation. Shape: three witches prophesy Macbeth will become king; he murders King Duncan, encouraged by Lady Macbeth, and takes the throne; guilt and paranoia drive him to further murders (Banquo, Macduff's family) to protect his position; his allies desert him, Lady Macbeth's guilt destroys her sanity and she dies; Macbeth is killed in battle by Macduff, and order is restored under Malcolm. Teach the shape as a rise-and-fall arc: ambition gained through crime, power maintained through more crime, and a final collapse that restores the natural order the crimes disrupted.

#### MAC.CTX Macbeth: Jacobean context (kingship, the Great Chain of Being, witchcraft, the Gunpowder Plot, James I)
Written around 1606 for King James I, who believed in the Divine Right of Kings (that a monarch's authority came directly from God) and who had recently survived the Gunpowder Plot (1605), an attempted assassination. The Great Chain of Being held that the universe had a fixed, God-given order from God down through kings to commoners; disrupting it (regicide) was believed to cause chaos in the natural world too -- explaining the play's storms, strange omens and darkness after Duncan's murder. James I was also personally fascinated by witchcraft and had written a book on it (Daemonologie), which explains the witches' prominence and their status as genuinely dangerous, not comic, figures to a 1606 audience.

#### MAC.1 Macbeth Act 1: the witches' prophecy and the seed of ambition
The witches greet Macbeth with three titles -- Thane of Glamis (true), Thane of Cawdor (soon true) and 'king hereafter' (not yet true) -- and greet Banquo with a prophecy that his descendants, not Macbeth's, will be kings. When the Cawdor title is confirmed almost immediately, Macbeth's ambition is ignited because the prophecy suddenly seems credible. Lady Macbeth, on reading his letter, resolves to push him towards murder and calls on spirits to 'unsex' her, stripping away feminine compassion so she can act with ruthless resolve; by the act's end she has largely persuaded a hesitant Macbeth to kill Duncan that very night.

#### MAC.2 Macbeth Act 2: the murder of Duncan
Macbeth hallucinates a floating dagger leading him towards Duncan's chamber, then murders the sleeping king off-stage. He returns badly shaken, having brought the incriminating daggers back with him rather than leaving them with the drugged guards as planned; Lady Macbeth, more composed, returns them and smears the guards with blood herself. The Porter's comic scene, imagining himself as the gatekeeper of Hell, darkly underlines that a kind of hell has entered the castle. Duncan's sons, Malcolm and Donalbain, flee for their own safety, which makes them look suspicious and clears Macbeth's path to the throne.

#### MAC.3 Macbeth Act 3: Banquo's murder and the banquet scene
Now king, Macbeth fears Banquo because of the witches' prophecy about his descendants, and hires murderers to kill Banquo and his son Fleance; Banquo is killed but Fleance escapes, leaving the prophecy about his line still alive and threatening. At the coronation banquet, Macbeth sees Banquo's ghost sitting in his own chair, visible only to him, and his terrified, incoherent reaction in front of the assembled lords makes his guilt and instability publicly visible for the first time. This is the act's turning point: Macbeth has moved from a single, hidden crime to open, escalating violence, and his grip on power is starting to depend on fear rather than legitimacy.
