# S20_Data_Types_Sources_Interpretation_and_Practical_Ethical_Issues - Lesson: Research methods: qualitative/quantitative and official/non-official data, primary/secondary sources, interpreting data, and practical and ethical issues

## Goal
The learner distinguishes qualitative/quantitative and official/non-official data, distinguishes primary/secondary sources, interprets data presented graphically, and explains practical and ethical issues in sociological research.

## How to teach this
Ask: 'A newspaper graph and a government census both give you "data" about society. Are they equally trustworthy, and equally easy to check for yourself? What might make the difference?'

#### 3.7h Qualitative and quantitative data: strengths, weaknesses and when each is used
Quantitative data is numerical, allowing statistical analysis, comparison and identification of broad patterns across large groups, but can lack depth and miss the meanings behind the numbers. Qualitative data is descriptive (words, images, detailed accounts), offering richer insight into people's meanings and experiences, but is harder to analyse systematically or generalise from confidently. Researchers choose between them, or combine them, depending on whether their research question calls for breadth and pattern, or depth and meaning.

#### 3.7i Official statistics and non-official (unofficial) statistics
Official statistics are collected and published by government or other official bodies (for example, census data, recorded crime figures), often covering large populations consistently over time, but collected for administrative purposes that may not exactly match a sociologist's research question, and subject to the dark-figure-type problems covered elsewhere in the specification. Non-official statistics are collected by other bodies such as charities, businesses or academic researchers, which can be more closely tailored to a specific research question but may be smaller-scale, less consistently collected, or reflect the particular interests of whoever produced them.

#### 3.7j Primary and secondary sources of data
Primary data is collected first-hand by the researcher specifically for their study (through a questionnaire, interview or observation the researcher conducts themselves). Secondary data already exists, collected by someone else for another purpose (official statistics, previous studies, historical records, media content), which the researcher then makes use of. Primary data can be tailored precisely to the research question but takes time and resources to collect; secondary data is often quicker and cheaper to access but may not fit the research question exactly.

#### 3.7k Interpreting data presented in graphs, diagrams, charts and tables
Interpreting a graph, chart or table means correctly reading specific values, identifying the overall pattern or trend it shows (an increase, a decrease, a comparison between groups), and, where asked, suggesting a plausible sociological explanation for that pattern. A common exam skill is describing the pattern precisely using figures from the source, rather than a vague general impression, before offering any wider explanation.

#### 3.7l Practical issues in research: time, cost and access
Practical issues affect what research is realistically possible: time (some methods, such as long-term participant observation, take far longer than a questionnaire), cost (larger samples, interviews and travel all cost more than a simple postal questionnaire), and access (whether the researcher can actually reach and gain the cooperation of the group they want to study, which can be especially difficult for sensitive or hard-to-reach groups).

#### 3.7m Ethical issues in research: informed consent, confidentiality and preventing harm to participants
Informed consent means participants agree to take part having been told what the research involves and what will happen to their data, and generally must be free to withdraw. Confidentiality means protecting participants' identities and personal information, for example by anonymising data in any published findings. Preventing harm means ensuring research does not cause participants physical, psychological, social or reputational harm, which is a particular concern for covert methods or research on sensitive or vulnerable groups.
