# S14_Data_on_Crime - Lesson: Data on crime: sources, patterns and trends, the dark figure, and evaluating official statistics

## Goal
The learner names and describes sources of crime data, interprets patterns and trends, explains the dark figure of crime, and evaluates the usefulness and reliability of official crime statistics.

## Syllabus items taught here
- 3.5.4a - Sources of crime data: official statistics, victim surveys and self-report studies: name and describe sources
- 3.5.4b - Patterns and trends shown by crime data: interpret trends
- 3.5.4c - The 'dark figure' of crime: explain the concept
- 3.5.4d - Evaluating the usefulness and reliability of official crime statistics: evaluate with a judgement

## How to teach this
Ask: 'If a crime happens but the victim never reports it, does it show up in the official crime figures? What might that mean for how much we can trust those figures?'

#### 3.5.4a Sources of crime data: official statistics, victim surveys and self-report studies
Official statistics are figures on crimes recorded by the police, published by government. Victim surveys (such as the Crime Survey for England and Wales) ask a representative sample of the public directly whether they have been a victim of crime, whether or not they reported it to police, aiming to capture crime that goes unrecorded. Self-report studies ask people to anonymously report crimes they themselves have committed, aiming to capture offending that never comes to police attention at all. Each source has a different, partial view of the true level of crime.

#### 3.5.4b Patterns and trends shown by crime data
Crime data can be used to identify patterns (which crimes are most/least common, which groups appear most in recorded offending or victimisation, geographical variation) and trends over time (whether particular crime types are rising or falling). Interpreting trends requires care: apparent rises can reflect real increases in offending, but can also reflect changes in what is recorded, in public willingness to report, or in policing priorities and recording practices.

#### 3.5.4c The 'dark figure' of crime
The dark figure of crime refers to the gap between crimes that actually occur and crimes that are officially recorded in police statistics. It arises because some crimes are never reported (for example, due to embarrassment, fear, or belief that nothing will be done) and some reported crimes are never officially recorded. Victim surveys and self-report studies exist partly to try to estimate and reduce this dark figure, though they too have limitations and cannot fully capture it.

#### 3.5.4d Evaluating the usefulness and reliability of official crime statistics
Official statistics are useful because they are large-scale, collected consistently over time, and allow comparison across areas and years. However, their reliability is limited by the dark figure of unreported and unrecorded crime, by changes in law and recording practice over time (making historical comparison difficult), and by the influence of policing priorities (for example, increased stop-and-search in an area can increase recorded crime there without a real increase in offending). A balanced evaluation weighs these strengths and limitations rather than treating official statistics as simply true or simply useless.
