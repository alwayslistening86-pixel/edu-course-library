# S02_The_Sociological_Approach_Concepts_and_Classical_Theorists - Lesson: The sociological approach: defining society and key concepts, the consensus/conflict debate, and classical theorists Durkheim, Marx and Weber

## Goal
The learner defines core sociological concepts, explains the consensus versus conflict debate, and explains the contextual contribution of Durkheim, Marx and Weber to the discipline.

## Syllabus items taught here
- 3.1a - Sociology as the study of society: defining society, socialisation, norms, values and roles: define key concepts
- 3.1b - Debates within sociology: consensus versus conflict views of society: explain the core disagreement
- 3.1c - How sociological ideas have evolved over time: explain change in the discipline
- 3.1d - Classical theorist: Durkheim, and the functionalist view of society: contextualise Durkheim

## How to teach this
Ask: 'You were not born knowing how to queue politely, or that it is rude to stare. How did you learn these unwritten rules, and what would sociologists call this process?'

#### 3.1a Sociology as the study of society: defining society, socialisation, norms, values and roles
Sociology is the systematic study of society: how people live together, and the patterns, structures and relationships that shape their lives. Society refers to a group of people who share a culture and interact within defined structures. Socialisation is the lifelong process of learning a society's culture. Norms are specific unwritten rules of expected behaviour (queuing, saying please); values are broader shared beliefs about what is important or right (honesty, equality). A role is the expected pattern of behaviour attached to a social position (parent, student, employee).

#### 3.1b Debates within sociology: consensus versus conflict views of society
A consensus view (associated with functionalism) sees society as fundamentally based on shared values and cooperation, with institutions working together for the good of society as a whole, like organs in a body. A conflict view (associated with Marxism and feminism) sees society as based on competing interests and inequality, with some groups benefiting at the expense of others, and institutions often serving to maintain that unequal power. This core disagreement shapes how each perspective explains almost every topic in the specification.

#### 3.1c How sociological ideas have evolved over time
Sociology developed as a discipline responding to the massive social changes of industrialisation and urbanisation in the 19th century, when thinkers began asking how societies hold together and change. Since then, ideas have evolved: early theories (functionalism, Marxism) offered broad, structural explanations of whole societies; later approaches (interactionism, feminism) shifted focus, some towards small-scale everyday interaction, others towards previously overlooked inequalities such as gender. Ideas are also revisited and updated as societies change (for example, later feminist and 'New Right' theories responding to more recent conditions).

#### 3.1d Classical theorist: Durkheim, and the functionalist view of society
Émile Durkheim, writing in the late 19th and early 20th century, is a founding figure of functionalism. He argued that society is held together by a shared set of norms and values (which he termed the collective conscience), and that social institutions (family, education, religion) exist because they perform necessary functions that keep society stable and orderly. His ideas underpin the functionalist perspective applied throughout this specification, for example to the family and to education.

#### 3.1e Classical theorist: Marx, and the conflict view of society
Karl Marx, writing in the 19th century, argued that society is fundamentally shaped by economic relationships and division into social classes with opposing interests: the bourgeoisie (owners of the means of production) and the proletariat (workers). He argued that other social institutions serve to maintain this unequal class system rather than serve society as a whole. His ideas underpin the Marxist perspective applied throughout this specification, for example to education and to social stratification.

#### 3.1f Classical theorist: Weber, and social action
Max Weber, writing in the late 19th and early 20th century, is associated with social action theory, emphasising the meanings individuals attach to their own actions, alongside broader structural factors. He also contributed influential work on social class (alongside wealth, status and party as separate sources of social position) and on types of authority (traditional, charismatic, rational-legal). His ideas are drawn on later in this specification, particularly for social stratification and power and authority.
