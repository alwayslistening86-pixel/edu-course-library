# S15_Functionalist_Theory_and_Socio_Economic_Class - Lesson: Social stratification: Davis and Moore's functionalist theory, alternative critiques, and socio-economic class (Marx and Weber)

## Goal
The learner explains Davis and Moore's functionalist theory of stratification, feminist and Marxist critiques of it, and Marx's and Weber's theories of social class.

## Syllabus items taught here
- 3.6.1a - Davis and Moore's functionalist theory of stratification: role allocation and functional importance: explain the theory
- 3.6.1b - Feminist and Marxist alternatives to the functionalist theory of stratification: explain the critique
- 3.6.2a - Marx's view of social class: bourgeoisie and proletariat: explain the theory
- 3.6.2b - Weber's view of class, status and party: explain the theory
- 3.6.2c - Identifying class divisions in contemporary Britain: apply to today

## How to teach this
Ask: 'Why do some jobs (a surgeon) get paid far more than others (a cleaner)? A functionalist has one answer to this; a Marxist has a very different one.'

#### 3.6.1a Davis and Moore's functionalist theory of stratification: role allocation and functional importance
Kingsley Davis and Wilbert Moore argued that social stratification (a hierarchy of unequal social positions) exists in every society because it serves a necessary function: allocating people to the roles most functionally important to society, and motivating the most able people to undergo the long training needed for the most demanding, important roles by offering greater rewards (income, status). Inequality, on this view, is a necessary and broadly fair mechanism for filling important positions with the most capable people.

#### 3.6.1b Feminist and Marxist alternatives to the functionalist theory of stratification
Critics argue Davis and Moore's theory fails to explain why some clearly important roles (such as caring professions, often performed by women) are poorly rewarded, while some highly rewarded roles are not obviously the most 'functionally important'. Feminists argue the theory ignores how stratification is shaped by gender, not just ability and importance. Marxists argue stratification is not a fair, functional mechanism at all, but reflects the ruling class's power to control resources and reward those who serve capitalism's interests, regardless of a role's true social value.

#### 3.6.2a Marx's view of social class: bourgeoisie and proletariat
Marx defined social class primarily by a person's relationship to the means of production (factories, land, capital): the bourgeoisie own and control it, while the proletariat, who own only their labour power, must sell that labour to survive. Marx argued this relationship creates an inherent conflict of interest between the two classes, since the bourgeoisie's profit depends on paying workers less than the value they produce.

#### 3.6.2b Weber's view of class, status and party
Weber argued that social position depends on three separate dimensions rather than economic relationship alone: class (economic position, including income and market situation, more finely graded than Marx's two broad classes), status (the social prestige or honour a person is accorded, which does not always match their income), and party (political power and the ability to influence decisions, for example through membership of an organisation). A person can, for example, have high status but limited economic class position, or vice versa.

#### 3.6.2c Identifying class divisions in contemporary Britain
Contemporary sociologists identify and debate ongoing class divisions in Britain using measures including occupation, income, wealth and, in more recent research, cultural and social factors alongside economic ones. Learners should be able to describe broad class categories used in contemporary Britain (such as working class, middle class and upper class, or more detailed official classifications) and discuss evidence for continuing, changing class-based inequality in areas such as income, wealth and life chances.
