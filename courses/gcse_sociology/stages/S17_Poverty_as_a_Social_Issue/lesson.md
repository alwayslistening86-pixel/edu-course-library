# S17_Poverty_as_a_Social_Issue - Lesson: Poverty as a social issue: absolute and relative poverty, the culture of poverty, Murray's underclass theory, and alleviating poverty

## Goal
The learner defines absolute and relative poverty using Townsend's concept, explains the culture-of-poverty explanation and Murray's underclass theory, and explains government attempts and globalisation's effect on poverty.

## Syllabus items taught here
- 3.6.4a - Absolute and relative poverty; Townsend's concept of relative deprivation: define and explain
- 3.6.4b - The culture of poverty explanation: explain the theory
- 3.6.4c - Murray: the underclass and New Right theory: explain the theory
- 3.6.4d - Government attempts to alleviate poverty: describe and evaluate
- 3.6.4e - Globalisation and its impact on poverty: explain the link

## How to teach this
Ask: 'Is someone in poverty only if they cannot afford food and shelter at all, or could someone with a roof and enough to eat still be "in poverty" in a meaningful sense, if they cannot afford what is normal in their society?'

#### 3.6.4a Absolute and relative poverty; Townsend's concept of relative deprivation
Absolute poverty is lacking the basic resources needed to sustain physical life (adequate food, shelter, clean water). Relative poverty is having significantly less than what is considered a normal or acceptable standard of living within one's own society, even if basic survival needs are met. Peter Townsend developed the influential concept of relative deprivation, arguing poverty should be measured relative to a society's customary living standards, since being unable to participate in normal social life (for example, affording a child's school trip) is itself a meaningful form of disadvantage, not just a lack of bare survival resources.

#### 3.6.4b The culture of poverty explanation
The culture of poverty explanation argues that poverty can become self-perpetuating because people who grow up in poverty may develop distinct attitudes, values and behaviours (for example, a sense of fatalism, or different attitudes to planning and saving) that are passed on to children and, even if circumstances later improve, can make it harder to escape poverty. Critics argue this explanation risks 'blaming the poor' for their situation while underplaying structural causes such as unemployment, low wages, or inadequate welfare provision.

#### 3.6.4c Murray: the underclass and New Right theory
Charles Murray, associated with New Right theory, argued that a distinct 'underclass' had developed, characterised by long-term welfare dependency, family instability (including rising lone parenthood) and low labour-market participation, which he argued was in part caused or worsened by overly generous welfare provision reducing the incentive to work. This view has been strongly criticised by sociologists who argue it understates structural causes of poverty (such as unemployment and low pay) and stigmatises those experiencing poverty.

#### 3.6.4d Government attempts to alleviate poverty
Government approaches to reducing poverty include cash benefits and tax credits for low-income households, a minimum/living wage, free or subsidised services (healthcare, education), and targeted schemes to support specific groups (such as children or pensioners). Evaluating such policies involves weighing their effectiveness at actually reducing poverty against criticisms such as cost, potential effects on work incentives, or whether support reaches those who need it most.

#### 3.6.4e Globalisation and its impact on poverty
Globalisation (the increasing economic, social and cultural interconnection of countries worldwide) affects poverty in complex ways: it can create jobs and economic growth in some regions through international trade and investment, while contributing to job losses in others (for example, where production moves to lower-wage countries) and to increased inequality both within and between countries, as gains from globalisation are not shared evenly.
