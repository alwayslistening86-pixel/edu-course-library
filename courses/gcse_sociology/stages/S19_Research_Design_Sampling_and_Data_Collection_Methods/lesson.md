# S19_Research_Design_Sampling_and_Data_Collection_Methods - Lesson: Research methods: research design, pilot studies, sampling, questionnaires, interviews, observations and mixed methods

## Goal
The learner formulates a research aim/hypothesis, explains pilot studies, names and evaluates sampling methods, and evaluates questionnaires, interviews, observations and mixed-methods approaches.

## Syllabus items taught here
- 3.7a - Research design: aims and hypotheses: formulate an aim/hypothesis
- 3.7b - Pilot studies: explain purpose and value
- 3.7c - Sampling methods: random, systematic, stratified, quota and snowball sampling, and sampling frames: name, explain and choose
- 3.7d - Questionnaires: strengths and weaknesses: evaluate the method
- 3.7e - Interviews: structured, unstructured and semi-structured, strengths and weaknesses: evaluate the method
- 3.7f - Observations: participant/non-participant and overt/covert, strengths and weaknesses: evaluate the method
- 3.7g - Mixed-methods approaches and triangulation: explain and evaluate

## How to teach this
Ask: 'If you wanted to find out whether teenagers' attitudes to family life have changed, how would you decide exactly who to ask, and how would you ask them?' Use this to move through the full research process from question to method.

#### 3.7a Research design: aims and hypotheses
A research aim states the general purpose of a study in broad terms (for example, 'to investigate attitudes to marriage among young adults'). A hypothesis is a more specific, testable statement predicting a relationship between two things (for example, 'young adults with cohabiting parents are less likely to plan to marry'), which the research is then designed to test through evidence, either supporting or challenging it.

#### 3.7b Pilot studies
A pilot study is a small-scale trial run of a research method before the main study, used to identify and fix problems (for example, confusing questionnaire wording, a question that does not work as intended, or a method that takes too long) before committing time and resources to the full-scale study. Running a pilot study can significantly improve the validity and reliability of the eventual main research.

#### 3.7c Sampling methods: random, systematic, stratified, quota and snowball sampling, and sampling frames
A sampling frame is the list from which a sample is drawn (for example, an electoral register). Random sampling selects from the sampling frame with no fixed pattern, giving each person an equal chance of selection. Systematic sampling selects at regular intervals (for example, every 10th name). Stratified sampling divides the population into relevant subgroups first (for example, by age or class) and samples proportionally within each. Quota sampling sets fixed numbers of participants to find in each subgroup, without a formal sampling frame. Snowball sampling asks existing participants to recommend further participants, often used to reach hard-to-access groups.

#### 3.7d Questionnaires: strengths and weaknesses
Questionnaires can gather standardised data from large numbers of people relatively quickly and cheaply, and produce data that is easy to quantify and compare. Weaknesses include a risk of low response rates, the possibility that questions are misunderstood without a researcher present to clarify, and a risk that closed questions limit the depth and richness of the answers respondents can give.

#### 3.7e Interviews: structured, unstructured and semi-structured, strengths and weaknesses
A structured interview follows a fixed set of questions in a fixed order, like a questionnaire read aloud, making results easier to compare. An unstructured interview is more like a guided conversation, allowing the interviewer to follow up interesting points, generating richer, more detailed (qualitative) data but making comparison across interviews harder. A semi-structured interview combines both, using a set of core questions alongside flexible follow-up. Interviews generally allow clarification and depth that questionnaires cannot, but are more time-consuming and can be affected by interviewer bias, where the interviewer's presence or manner influences the answers given.

#### 3.7f Observations: participant/non-participant and overt/covert, strengths and weaknesses
In participant observation the researcher joins in with the group being studied; in non-participant observation the researcher watches without joining in. In overt observation, participants know they are being studied; in covert observation, they do not. Observation can capture real behaviour rather than what people say they do, which can be especially valuable where people might not answer honestly in an interview or questionnaire, but raises practical challenges (time, access) and, for covert research, significant ethical concerns.

#### 3.7g Mixed-methods approaches and triangulation
A mixed-methods approach combines more than one research method within a single study (for example, a questionnaire followed by follow-up interviews). Triangulation is using multiple methods or data sources to check findings against each other; where different methods produce similar findings, this increases confidence that the results reflect something real rather than an artefact of one particular method, combining, for example, the breadth of quantitative data with the depth of qualitative data.
