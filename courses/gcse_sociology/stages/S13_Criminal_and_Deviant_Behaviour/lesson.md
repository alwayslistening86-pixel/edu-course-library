# S13_Criminal_and_Deviant_Behaviour - Lesson: Criminal and deviant behaviour: class, gender, ethnicity and age, Albert Cohen, Carlen, and public debates

## Goal
The learner explains patterns of criminal/deviant behaviour by class, gender, ethnicity and age, explains Albert Cohen's and Carlen's studies, and discusses public debates on crime and punishment.

## Syllabus items taught here
- 3.5.3a - Class and crime: explain the factor with evidence
- 3.5.3b - Gender and crime: explain the factor with evidence
- 3.5.3c - Ethnicity and crime: explain the factor with evidence
- 3.5.3d - Age and crime: explain the factor with evidence
- 3.5.3e - Albert Cohen: delinquent subcultures: explain the study
- 3.5.3f - Carlen: women, crime and poverty: explain the study
- 3.5.3g - Public debates: violent crime, sentencing, treatment of young offenders, the prison system and media coverage of crime: discuss the debate

## How to teach this
Ask: 'Recorded crime statistics show clear differences by class, gender, ethnicity and age. Does that mean these groups are simply more or less "criminal" by nature — or could something else explain the pattern?'

#### 3.5.3a Class and crime
Official statistics show working-class people disproportionately represented in recorded crime, but sociologists debate how far this reflects real differences in offending versus differences in policing, detection and sentencing that make working-class crime more visible (for example, more visible 'street crime' being more heavily policed than white-collar or corporate crime, which can be harder to detect and is often committed by more affluent offenders).

#### 3.5.3b Gender and crime
Recorded crime statistics consistently show men committing far more crime than women, particularly violent crime. Explanations offered include differential socialisation (boys and girls raised with different norms around aggression and risk-taking), differences in informal social control (as Heidensohn argued), and possible differences in how the criminal justice system treats male and female offenders (sometimes described in terms of a 'chivalry thesis', itself contested).

#### 3.5.3c Ethnicity and crime
Recorded crime and criminal justice statistics show differences between ethnic groups, a pattern sociologists explain with caution, examining possible factors including socio-economic disadvantage, differential policing practices (such as stop-and-search rates), and potential bias at various stages of the criminal justice system, while stressing that such statistics must be interpreted carefully rather than taken as evidence of any group's innate criminality.

#### 3.5.3d Age and crime
Recorded offending rates are notably higher among younger age groups, particularly teenagers and young adults, before generally declining with age. Explanations include the influence of peer groups and status-seeking during adolescence, limited legitimate access to adult sources of status or income, and desistance from crime as people take on adult responsibilities such as employment, partnership and parenthood.

#### 3.5.3e Albert Cohen: delinquent subcultures
Albert Cohen argued that some working-class boys, unable to achieve status by mainstream (often middle-class) standards, especially within school, respond by rejecting those standards altogether and forming a delinquent subculture with its own status system, where non-utilitarian, malicious or destructive rule-breaking (rather than crime purely for material gain) becomes a valued way of winning status among peers.

#### 3.5.3f Carlen: women, crime and poverty
Pat Carlen's research into women's offending highlighted how poverty and a lack of legitimate rewards (from family life or from paid work) could weaken women's normal 'bonds' to conformity, making crime a more likely response when women felt they were gaining little from playing by conventional rules — offering an explanation of some female offending more attentive to class and material circumstances than earlier, purely gender-focused theories.

#### 3.5.3g Public debates: violent crime, sentencing, treatment of young offenders, the prison system and media coverage of crime
Contemporary public debates covered by this specification include: how to respond to violent crime and whether current approaches are effective; whether sentencing is too lenient or too harsh, and for whom; how young offenders should be treated, balancing punishment, rehabilitation and welfare; whether the prison system successfully rehabilitates or mainly punishes and how this affects reoffending; and how media coverage of crime may shape public perception of how much and what kind of crime occurs, sometimes creating moral panics disproportionate to the actual statistical risk.
