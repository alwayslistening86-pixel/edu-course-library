# S05_Family_Forms - Lesson: Family forms in the UK and globally: the Rapoports' family diversity, and nuclear, extended, reconstituted, lone-parent and same-sex families

## Goal
The learner explains how family forms differ across the UK and globally, explains the Rapoports' typology of family diversity, and identifies and explains nuclear, extended, reconstituted, lone-parent and same-sex families.

## Syllabus items taught here
- 3.3.2a - How family forms differ in the UK and in a global context: compare family forms
- 3.3.2b - The Rapoports' work on family diversity: explain the five types of diversity
- 3.3.2c - Nuclear families: identify and explain
- 3.3.2d - Extended families: identify and explain
- 3.3.2e - Reconstituted, lone-parent and same-sex families: identify and explain

## How to teach this
Ask: 'If someone said "the family" as though there is only one kind, what different kinds of family might they be leaving out?'

#### 3.3.2a How family forms differ in the UK and in a global context
Family structures are not fixed: within the UK, family forms have diversified over recent decades (fewer traditional nuclear households, more lone-parent and reconstituted families, greater acceptance of same-sex families). Globally, family structures vary further still, with extended family living remaining far more common in many societies than in the contemporary UK, reflecting different economic conditions, cultural values and welfare systems.

#### 3.3.2b The Rapoports' work on family diversity
Robert and Rhona Rapoport identified several distinct types of family diversity in Britain: organisational diversity (differences in family structure and division of roles), cultural diversity (differences between ethnic and religious groups), social class diversity (differences linked to income and class), life-stage diversity (families look different at different points, for example before and after children), and generational diversity (older and younger generations holding different attitudes and experiences of family life). Their central argument is that no single 'typical' family form should be treated as the norm.

#### 3.3.2c Nuclear families
A nuclear family consists of two parents and their dependent children, living together as a single household unit. It was traditionally presented by functionalists as the standard or ideal family form best suited to modern industrial society, though critics argue this both overstates how common it has ever been and understates other family forms' validity.

#### 3.3.2d Extended families
An extended family includes relatives beyond the nuclear unit living together or in close, regular contact, such as grandparents, aunts, uncles or cousins. A vertically extended family spans three or more generations (for example, grandparents living with parents and children); a horizontally extended family includes same-generation relatives such as aunts, uncles and cousins. Extended families can provide wider practical and emotional support than a nuclear family alone.

#### 3.3.2e Reconstituted, lone-parent and same-sex families
A reconstituted (or 'stepfamily') family forms when one or both partners bring children from a previous relationship into a new family unit. A lone-parent family consists of one parent raising one or more dependent children, arising through divorce, separation, bereavement or by choice. A same-sex family consists of a couple of the same sex, with or without children, and has become increasingly socially and legally recognised in the UK in recent decades, for example through civil partnerships and equal marriage.
