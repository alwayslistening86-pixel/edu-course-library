# S12_Social_Construction_of_Crime_and_Social_Control - Lesson: The social construction of crime and deviance, and social control: Merton's strain theory, Becker's labelling theory, formal and informal control, and Heidensohn

## Goal
The learner explains the social construction of crime and deviance, Merton's strain theory, Becker's labelling theory, formal and informal social control, and Heidensohn's account of female conformity.

## How to teach this
Ask: 'Jaywalking is a crime in some countries and not others; homosexuality was once illegal in Britain and is not now. What does this tell you about where the label "crime" actually comes from?'

#### 3.5.1a The social construction of crime and deviance
Crime is behaviour that breaks a society's formal, legally enacted rules, while deviance is behaviour that breaks a society's informal norms and expectations, whether or not it is illegal; the two overlap but are not identical (some deviant behaviour is not criminal, and social attitudes to what counts as either can change over time and place). The social construction of crime and deviance is the idea that what counts as criminal or deviant is not fixed or natural, but is defined and can change through social, legal and political processes.

#### 3.5.1b Merton's functionalist strain theory (anomie)
Robert Merton argued that society sets shared cultural goals (such as financial success) but does not give everyone equal legitimate means to achieve them. This mismatch produces 'strain' (or anomie), and people respond in different ways: conforming despite the strain, or 'innovating' by using illegitimate means (crime) to pursue the same culturally approved goals, among other possible responses Merton identified. Crime, on this view, is a predictable structural response to unequal opportunity rather than simple individual wrongdoing.

#### 3.5.1c Becker's interactionist labelling theory
Howard Becker argued that no act is inherently criminal or deviant; it becomes so only when a social audience, especially those with the power to enforce rules (for example, the police, courts or media), successfully labels it as such. Becker introduced the idea of a 'master status', where once someone is labelled a criminal or deviant, this label can come to dominate how others see them (and how they see themselves), potentially leading them further into a deviant identity and career.

#### 3.5.1d Structural and subcultural theories of crime and deviance compared
Structural theories (such as Merton's) explain crime by reference to the wider organisation of society (for example, unequal access to legitimate opportunity). Subcultural theories explain crime by reference to distinct groups within society that develop their own norms and values, sometimes opposed to mainstream society's, that make criminal or deviant behaviour normal or even valued within that specific group. Both differ from interactionist labelling theory, which focuses less on why the original act occurs and more on the social reaction and label attached to it afterwards.

#### 3.5.2a Formal social control: police, courts and the law
Formal social control operates through official institutions with recognised authority to enforce rules through binding, often written, laws and specified punishments: the police (who detect and investigate crime), the courts (who determine guilt and pass sentence), and the wider legal and penal system. Formal control mechanisms typically apply across the whole of society and carry the state's coercive power behind them.

#### 3.5.2b Informal social control: family, peers, media and unwritten rules and sanctions
Informal social control operates through everyday social relationships and unwritten rules rather than law: the family (through upbringing and approval/disapproval), peer groups (through acceptance or exclusion), and the media (through shaping what is seen as acceptable or shameful). Sanctions here are informal — praise, gossip, social exclusion, ridicule — rather than legal punishment, but can still be a powerful influence on behaviour.

#### 3.5.2c Heidensohn: female conformity in a patriarchal society
Frances Heidensohn argued that women commit less crime than men partly because they are subject to greater informal social control throughout their lives, within a patriarchal society: closer control by fathers and partners at home, greater social pressure to conform to respectable/domestic roles, and restricted freedom of movement (for example, safety concerns limiting time spent out in public, especially at night). She argued this heavier informal control, more than any innate difference, helps explain lower recorded female offending.
