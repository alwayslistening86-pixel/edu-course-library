# S09_Roles_and_Functions_of_Education - Lesson: Roles and functions of education: Durkheim and Parsons, meeting economic needs/social mobility/social cohesion, school types and alternatives to schooling

## Goal
The learner explains Durkheim's and Parsons' functionalist views of education, identifies education's functions, and describes school types and alternatives to mainstream schooling.

## Syllabus items taught here
- 3.4.1a - Durkheim's functionalist view: education transmits norms and values: explain Durkheim
- 3.4.1b - Parsons: education, achieved status and the meritocratic principle: explain Parsons
- 3.4.1c - Functions of education: meeting the economy's needs, social mobility and social cohesion: identify and explain
- 3.4.1d - Types of school: primary, secondary, state and private: describe each type
- 3.4.1e - Alternatives to mainstream schooling: home schooling and deschooling: describe alternatives

## How to teach this
Ask: 'Besides teaching you facts and skills, what else might school be quietly training you to do, ready for adult and working life?'

#### 3.4.1a Durkheim's functionalist view: education transmits norms and values
Émile Durkheim argued that education's core role is to transmit a society's shared norms and values to each new generation, creating a sense of social solidarity and a shared identity, alongside teaching the specific skills needed for particular jobs. Without this shared transmission of culture, he argued, a society risks fragmenting rather than holding together.

#### 3.4.1b Parsons: education, achieved status and the meritocratic principle
Talcott Parsons argued education acts as a 'bridge' between the family, where status is ascribed (given at birth, treated equally regardless of ability), and wider society, where status should be achieved through individual effort and ability. He argued schools operate on meritocratic principles — the idea that everyone is judged by the same standards and rewarded according to ability and effort — preparing pupils to accept a similarly meritocratic wider society.

#### 3.4.1c Functions of education: meeting the economy's needs, social mobility and social cohesion
Education is seen as meeting the economy's needs by teaching the skills and qualifications employers require and helping match people to appropriate jobs. It can enable social mobility, allowing individuals from less advantaged backgrounds to improve their social position through educational achievement. It also contributes to social cohesion, bringing together people from different backgrounds under a shared educational experience and shared national curriculum.

#### 3.4.1d Types of school: primary, secondary, state and private
Primary schools provide education for younger children (broadly ages 4 to 11 in England), and secondary schools for older pupils (broadly 11 to 16 or 18), with a broader, more specialised curriculum. State schools are funded by the government and free to attend, following a national curriculum. Private (independent) schools charge fees and have more freedom over curriculum and admissions; sociologists examine how access to private education relates to social class and life chances.

#### 3.4.1e Alternatives to mainstream schooling: home schooling and deschooling
Home schooling (or elective home education) is where parents take direct responsibility for their child's education outside the mainstream school system, for reasons including religious belief, dissatisfaction with local schools, or a child's particular needs. Deschooling is a more radical idea (associated with critics such as Ivan Illich) that questions whether formal, compulsory schooling is the best way to educate people at all, arguing it can be overly rigid, standardised and controlling compared with more flexible, community-based approaches to learning.
