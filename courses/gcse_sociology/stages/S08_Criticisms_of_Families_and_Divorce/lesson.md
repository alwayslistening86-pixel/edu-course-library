# S08_Criticisms_of_Families_and_Divorce - Lesson: Criticisms of the family, and divorce: pattern, reasons for the rise, and consequences

## Goal
The learner explains functionalist-critical, feminist and Marxist criticisms of the family, and explains the pattern, reasons for and consequences of divorce in Britain since 1945.

## Syllabus items taught here
- 3.3.5a - Criticisms of families: isolation, unrealistic idealisation, lost functions and reduced contact with wider kin: explain each criticism
- 3.3.5b - Feminist critique: Delphy and Leonard on women's status and roles within the family: explain the critique
- 3.3.5c - Marxist critique: Zaretsky, plus marital breakdown and dysfunctional families: explain the critique
- 3.3.6a - The pattern of divorce in Britain since 1945: describe the trend with evidence
- 3.3.6b - Reasons for the rise in divorce: legal changes, changing social attitudes, secularisation and women's changing status: explain multiple factors
- 3.3.6c - Consequences of divorce for spouses, children and the extended family, and the rise of lone-parent families: explain consequences

## How to teach this
Ask: 'If the family is supposed to be a source of support and stability, why might some sociologists argue it can also be a source of harm or inequality?'

#### 3.3.5a Criticisms of families: isolation, unrealistic idealisation, lost functions and reduced contact with wider kin
Critics argue the modern nuclear family can leave members socially isolated, cut off from the wider support once provided by extended family; that family life is often idealised in a way that does not match many people's real, sometimes difficult, experience; that functions the family once performed (education, care of the elderly, economic production) have increasingly been taken over by other institutions such as schools and the state; and that reduced regular contact with wider kin can weaken a valuable source of practical and emotional support.

#### 3.3.5b Feminist critique: Delphy and Leonard on women's status and roles within the family
Christine Delphy and Diana Leonard argued the family functions as an economic system that materially benefits men at women's expense: women's unpaid domestic labour and, historically, restricted access to paid work, gives men greater economic power within the household. They viewed the family less as a haven of harmony and more as a site of exploitation structured by patriarchy.

#### 3.3.5c Marxist critique: Zaretsky, plus marital breakdown and dysfunctional families
Eli Zaretsky argued the family under capitalism offers an illusion of a private refuge from the pressures of work, while actually helping sustain capitalism by privately absorbing workers' stress and reproducing the workforce cheaply. Beyond ideological critiques, sociologists also examine marital breakdown and 'dysfunctional' families directly, where conflict, neglect or abuse mean the family fails to provide the stability, support or socialisation that functionalist theory assumes it normally does.

#### 3.3.6a The pattern of divorce in Britain since 1945
Divorce rates in Britain rose sharply from the mid-20th century, particularly following legal reforms in the late 1960s and 1970s that made divorce considerably easier to obtain, and continued at historically high levels through the later 20th century compared with the immediate post-war period, though rates have fluctuated and in some more recent periods declined somewhat as marriage patterns themselves have also changed (including more cohabitation before or instead of marriage).

#### 3.3.6b Reasons for the rise in divorce: legal changes, changing social attitudes, secularisation and women's changing status
Legal changes made divorce cheaper, faster and more accessible (for example, widening the grounds for divorce and reducing cost/complexity). Changing social attitudes mean divorce carries much less stigma than in the past. Secularisation (declining religious influence) has weakened religious objections to divorce for many people. Women's changing status — greater access to paid employment and financial independence — means women are less likely to feel economically trapped in an unhappy marriage than in the past.

#### 3.3.6c Consequences of divorce for spouses, children and the extended family, and the rise of lone-parent families
For spouses, consequences can include emotional distress, financial hardship (particularly, evidence suggests, for women), and a change in social and family role. For children, sociologists debate effects on wellbeing, behaviour and educational outcomes, while noting that the quality of relationships before and after divorce, not divorce itself alone, strongly shapes the outcome. For the extended family, contact patterns and support roles (especially grandparents) may shift. Rising divorce has directly contributed to a substantial increase in the number of lone-parent and reconstituted families in Britain.
