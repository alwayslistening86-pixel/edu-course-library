# S18_Power_Authority_and_Power_Relationships - Lesson: Power and authority: Weber's three types of authority and sources of power, and power relationships by class, gender, sexuality, race, age, disability and religion, including Walby on patriarchy

## Goal
The learner explains Weber's three types of authority, formal and informal sources of power, Walby's theory of patriarchy, and how class, gender and other factors shape power relationships.

- 3.6.5b - Formal and informal sources of power: distinguish and give examples

#### 3.6.5a Weber's three types of authority: traditional, charismatic and rational-legal
Max Weber distinguished authority (power seen as legitimate) from mere coercive power, and identified three types: traditional authority, based on long-established custom (for example, a hereditary monarch); charismatic authority, based on a leader's personal qualities that inspire devotion and loyalty (for example, a compelling social or political leader); and rational-legal authority, based on a formal system of rules and procedures rather than the individual holding office (for example, an elected official's authority coming from their office and the law, not personal charisma).

#### 3.6.5b Formal and informal sources of power
Formal sources of power are recognised, often institutional or legal, such as political office, legal authority, or a position within an organisation's official hierarchy. Informal sources of power operate outside formal structures, such as social influence, wealth used to informally shape outcomes, or informal group leadership, and can be significant even without holding any official position or title.

#### 3.6.6a Walby: patriarchy
Sylvia Walby argued that patriarchy (a system of social structures and practices in which men dominate and exploit women) operates across several distinct structures in society simultaneously, including paid work, the household, the state, culture, sexuality and male violence, rather than through any single cause. She argued the specific form patriarchy takes has changed over time (for example, shifting from more private, household-based control towards more public forms of inequality), but that systematic gender inequality in power has persisted.

#### 3.6.6b Class, gender and sexuality in power relationships
Class shapes power through unequal access to economic resources and influence over decision-making, at both a societal and workplace level. Gender shapes power through patterns such as under-representation of women in senior political and business positions, and, as Walby argued, patriarchal structures across multiple areas of social life. Sexuality can shape power through historical and continuing inequality and discrimination faced by LGBT+ people in accessing positions of influence and equal treatment, despite significant legal progress.

#### 3.6.6c Race, age, disability and religion in power relationships
Race and ethnicity can shape power through under-representation of minority ethnic groups in senior political, legal and business positions, and through wider structural discrimination. Age can shape power through the relative influence of different generations in political and economic decision-making. Disability can shape power through barriers to full participation in public and working life. Religion can shape power through the differing social and political influence of different religious (and non-religious) groups and communities.
