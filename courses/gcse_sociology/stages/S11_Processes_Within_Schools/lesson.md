# S11_Processes_Within_Schools - Lesson: Processes within schools: streaming and setting, labelling and the self-fulfilling prophecy, Willis' counter-school culture, and perspectives compared

## Goal
The learner explains streaming/setting, labelling and the self-fulfilling prophecy in schools, Willis' study of counter-school culture, and compares interactionist, functionalist, feminist and Marxist views of school processes.

## Syllabus items taught here
- 3.4.4a - Streaming, setting and mixed-ability teaching: describe and compare
- 3.4.4b - Labelling and the self-fulfilling prophecy in schools: Ball on teacher expectations: explain the mechanism
- 3.4.4c - Willis: counter-school culture among working-class boys: explain the study
- 3.4.4d - Interactionist, functionalist, feminist and Marxist perspectives on school processes compared: compare perspectives

## How to teach this
Ask: 'If a teacher decides early on that a pupil is "not academic" and puts them in a lower set, could that decision itself end up making the prediction come true? How might that happen?'

#### 3.4.4a Streaming, setting and mixed-ability teaching
Streaming places pupils into fixed groups based on overall perceived ability across most or all subjects. Setting places pupils into different groups by ability separately for each individual subject (a pupil might be in a top set for maths but a lower set for English). Mixed-ability teaching keeps pupils of different abilities together in the same class for a given subject. Sociologists compare these approaches in terms of their effects on achievement, self-esteem and inequality between pupils.

#### 3.4.4b Labelling and the self-fulfilling prophecy in schools: Ball on teacher expectations
Labelling in schools occurs when teachers form an impression or stereotype of a pupil (as, for example, 'bright' or 'a troublemaker'), often based on factors including class, gender, ethnicity, appearance or behaviour, sometimes even before getting to know them well. A self-fulfilling prophecy occurs when this label changes how the teacher treats the pupil (more or less attention, encouragement or opportunity), which in turn shapes the pupil's own self-image and effort, eventually causing them to perform in line with the original label. Stephen Ball's research specifically examined how teacher expectations linked to streaming/setting placement affected pupils' subsequent achievement.

#### 3.4.4c Willis: counter-school culture among working-class boys
Paul Willis' study 'Learning to Labour' examined a group of working-class boys ('the lads') who developed an anti-school, counter-school culture: rejecting the school's values, mocking conformist pupils, and rebelling against teachers and formal rules. Willis argued this rebellion, while resisting the school's authority in the short term, ironically prepared the boys for the low-skilled, low-status manual jobs typically available to working-class men, effectively reproducing rather than escaping their class position.

#### 3.4.4d Interactionist, functionalist, feminist and Marxist perspectives on school processes compared
Interactionists (such as those studying labelling) focus on small-scale interaction between teachers and pupils and how meanings and identities are constructed day to day. Functionalists tend to see school processes such as streaming as a practical, broadly fair way of matching pupils to appropriate opportunities. Feminists examine how school processes may treat boys and girls differently (for example, through subject expectations or classroom interaction patterns). Marxists (as with Willis) see processes within schools as ultimately reproducing existing class inequality, whatever their surface justification.
