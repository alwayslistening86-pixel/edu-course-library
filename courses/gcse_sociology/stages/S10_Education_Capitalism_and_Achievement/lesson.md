# S10_Education_Capitalism_and_Achievement - Lesson: Education and capitalism: the correspondence principle, and educational achievement by class, gender, ethnicity, and parental choice

## Goal
The learner explains Bowles and Gintis' correspondence principle, compares it with other perspectives, and explains class, gender and ethnicity differences in achievement, including Halsey's and Ball's work.

## Syllabus items taught here
- 3.4.2a - Bowles and Gintis: the correspondence principle (Marxist perspective on education and capitalism): explain the theory
- 3.4.2b - Comparing the Marxist view of education with functionalist and other perspectives: compare perspectives
- 3.4.3a - Class-based inequalities in educational achievement: Halsey's work: explain the factor and evidence
- 3.4.3b - Gender differences in educational achievement: explain patterns and reasons
- 3.4.3c - Ethnicity and educational achievement: explain patterns and reasons
- 3.4.3d - Ball's work on parental choice and school competition (parentocracy): explain Ball

## How to teach this
Ask: 'School rewards punctuality, following instructions and accepting hierarchy. Whose interests, according to a Marxist, might that pattern of rewards actually be preparing you to serve?'

#### 3.4.2a Bowles and Gintis: the correspondence principle (Marxist perspective on education and capitalism)
Samuel Bowles and Herbert Gintis argued that the structure and daily experience of schooling closely mirrors ('corresponds to') the structure of the capitalist workplace: a hierarchy of authority, alienating, externally imposed tasks with little control for the pupil/worker, and rewards for obedience and punctuality. They argued this hidden curriculum trains pupils to accept their likely future position as compliant workers within a capitalist economy, rather than genuinely developing their full potential.

#### 3.4.2b Comparing the Marxist view of education with functionalist and other perspectives
Where functionalists (Durkheim, Parsons) see education as broadly beneficial, meritocratic and serving society as a whole, Marxists (Bowles and Gintis) argue it primarily serves the interests of a capitalist ruling class, disguising inequality as fair 'meritocracy' when opportunity is not actually equal. This comparison — a positive, consensus-based account against a critical, conflict-based account of the very same institution — is a recurring exam theme, and strong answers explicitly set the two against each other rather than describing them separately.

#### 3.4.3a Class-based inequalities in educational achievement: Halsey's work
A. H. Halsey's research documented substantial, persistent class-based inequalities in educational achievement and access to higher education across generations in Britain, showing that a pupil's social class background remained strongly linked to their educational outcomes even as overall access to education expanded. This body of evidence underpins ongoing sociological debate about how far education is genuinely meritocratic in practice.

#### 3.4.3b Gender differences in educational achievement
Sociologists examine long-term shifts in the gender achievement gap, including periods where girls have, on average, outperformed boys in many measures of attainment, and explanations offered for this (changing expectations and ambitions of girls, equal opportunities policies, coursework/assessment styles, and changing gender identities and role models), alongside continued debate about subject choices and outcomes that still differ by gender.

#### 3.4.3c Ethnicity and educational achievement
Sociologists examine patterns of educational achievement that vary between different ethnic groups in Britain, and the explanations offered for these patterns, including possible factors within schools (teacher expectations, curriculum content, labelling) and factors outside school (material circumstances, language, cultural factors), while stressing that ethnic groups are not uniform and that class and gender interact with ethnicity in shaping outcomes.

#### 3.4.3d Ball's work on parental choice and school competition (parentocracy)
Stephen Ball's research examined the effects of policies giving parents greater choice over which school their child attends, and the resulting competition between schools. He argued this can produce a 'parentocracy' in which a child's educational opportunities depend heavily on their parents' ability to research, apply to and, where relevant, afford to access the best-performing schools — meaning apparent 'choice' can in practice reproduce existing class-based inequality rather than reduce it.
