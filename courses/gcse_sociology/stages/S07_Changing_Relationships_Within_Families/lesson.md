# S07_Changing_Relationships_Within_Families - Lesson: Changing relationships within families: historical change, Willmott and Young's symmetrical family, and contemporary issues

## Goal
The learner explains how family relationships have changed from pre-industrial to contemporary Britain, explains Willmott and Young's theory, and discusses contemporary issues in parenting, teenager-adult relationships, elderly/disabled care and arranged marriage.

## Syllabus items taught here
- 3.3.4a - How relationships within families have changed: pre-industrial to contemporary Britain: explain the historical shift
- 3.3.4b - Willmott and Young: the symmetrical family and the principle of stratified diffusion: explain the theory
- 3.3.4c - Contemporary issues: the quality of parenting, and relationships between teenagers and adults: explain and evaluate
- 3.3.4d - Care of elderly and disabled relatives, and arranged marriage: explain and evaluate

## How to teach this
Ask: 'Would a family living two hundred years ago on a farm, and a family living in a city today, likely relate to each other in the same way — who works, who is in charge, how much time is spent together? What might have changed, and why?'

#### 3.3.4a How relationships within families have changed: pre-industrial to contemporary Britain
In pre-industrial Britain, families often worked together as an economic unit (for example on a farm), with several generations frequently living and working closely together and clear, rigid patriarchal authority. Industrialisation moved paid work outside the home, gradually separating work and family life and contributing to smaller, more geographically mobile nuclear households. In contemporary Britain, relationships are often described as more equal, negotiated and emotionally focused, though sociologists disagree about how complete this shift towards equality really is.

#### 3.3.4b Willmott and Young: the symmetrical family and the principle of stratified diffusion
Willmott and Young argued that family life had moved through stages towards a more 'symmetrical family', where conjugal roles are more equal, home-centred and joint, rather than sharply segregated. They explained the spread of this pattern using the principle of stratified diffusion: new patterns of family life first appear among higher social classes and then gradually spread down through the class structure over time, as other groups adopt similar norms.

#### 3.3.4c Contemporary issues: the quality of parenting, and relationships between teenagers and adults
Contemporary debates about parenting quality include concerns about time pressures on working parents, the role of technology and screen time, and differing parenting styles and their effects on children's development. Relationships between teenagers and adults are often discussed in terms of a longer, more negotiated transition to adulthood than in the past, with debates about how much independence and responsibility teenagers should be given and how conflict between generations should be managed.

#### 3.3.4d Care of elderly and disabled relatives, and arranged marriage
As life expectancy has increased, the care of elderly and disabled relatives has become a more significant family issue, raising questions about the balance between family responsibility, paid care and state provision, and about the emotional and financial strain this can place on carers, often women. Arranged marriage (where families play a significant role in selecting or approving a partner, distinct from a forced marriage, which involves no consent) remains practised within some cultural and religious communities in Britain, and is examined sociologically for both its social functions and the debates surrounding individual choice.

## Explicitly not here
