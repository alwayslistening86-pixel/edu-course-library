# S03_Core_Perspectives_Key_Concepts_and_Sociological_Skills - Lesson: The four core perspectives (functionalism, Marxism, feminism, interactionism), further key concepts, and evaluating, interpreting and applying sociology to contemporary issues

## Goal
The learner explains the core claims of functionalism, Marxism, feminism and interactionism, defines labelling/discrimination/power/authority, and critically evaluates and applies sociological ideas to sources and contemporary issues.

## How to teach this
Ask: 'A teacher expects a particular pupil to misbehave, treats them accordingly, and the pupil starts misbehaving more. What would you call this pattern, and which sociological perspective is most interested in exactly this kind of everyday interaction?'

#### 3.1g The functionalist perspective
Functionalism sees society as a system of interconnected parts (institutions) that work together, much like organs in a body, to maintain social order, stability and shared values (value consensus). Each institution is analysed in terms of the positive function it performs for society as a whole. Functionalism is criticised for underplaying conflict, inequality and the possibility that some institutions serve some groups' interests more than others.

#### 3.1h The Marxist perspective
Marxism sees society as divided by economic class conflict between those who own the means of production and those who sell their labour. Social institutions are analysed in terms of how they serve to maintain and justify this unequal system (for example, transmitting ideology that makes inequality seem natural or fair). Marxism is criticised for focusing heavily on social class and underplaying other sources of inequality, such as gender.

#### 3.1i The feminist perspective
Feminism sees society as structured by gender inequality, with institutions often serving to maintain male power (patriarchy) over women. Different feminist traditions vary in how they explain this (for example, some focus on the economic system, others on culture and socialisation), but all examine how gender shapes experience and opportunity across every topic in the specification, from the family to crime to stratification.

#### 3.1j The interactionist perspective
Interactionism focuses on small-scale, everyday face-to-face interaction rather than large social structures, examining how individuals interpret situations, construct meaning, and how labels attached to people (by teachers, police, or wider society) can shape their identity and future behaviour, as in the self-fulfilling prophecy. Interactionism is criticised for underplaying the influence of wider social structures such as class and economic power.

#### 3.1k Key concepts: labelling, discrimination, power and authority
Labelling is the process of attaching a description (often a stereotype) to a person or group, which can then shape how they are treated and even how they see themselves. Discrimination is treating a person or group unfairly because of a characteristic such as class, gender or ethnicity. Power is the ability to get others to act as you wish, even against their own interests. Authority is power that is recognised as legitimate by those subject to it (unlike, for example, coercion by force alone).

#### 3.1l Interconnections between the core topic areas, and applying research methods throughout
The specification's core topics are not separate silos: perspectives, concepts and named studies recur across families, education, crime and deviance, and social stratification (for example, functionalism appears in relation to both the family and education). Research methods (section 3.7) are examined as they apply throughout every topic, not as a standalone topic, so learners should be ready to discuss how a given study's method affects how much its findings can be trusted, whichever topic it appears in.

#### 3.2a Critically evaluating and comparing sociological theories and explanations
Evaluating a theory means judging its strengths and weaknesses, for example whether it is supported by evidence, whether it can explain exceptions, or whether it has been criticised by another perspective. Comparing explanations means setting two or more theories directly against each other on the same question, showing where they agree and disagree, rather than describing them one after another with no connection made between them.

#### 3.2b Reading and interpreting sociological extracts and data
Exam and coursework tasks sometimes present a short sociological extract or a piece of data (a quotation, a statistic, a short passage). Interpreting it means correctly identifying what point it is making or what pattern it shows, then using that specific evidence to support an answer, rather than ignoring the source or restating it without analysis.

#### 3.2c Exploring contemporary social issues to challenge everyday, common-sense understanding
Sociology often takes a topic that seems like 'common sense' (for example, 'the family is naturally the way it is') and uses evidence and theory to question that assumption, revealing that patterns which feel natural are often socially constructed and vary across time and culture. Learners should practise applying sociological concepts to current, real-world social issues, not only to textbook examples.
