# S06_Conjugal_Role_Relationships - Lesson: Conjugal role relationships: joint and segregated roles, Oakley's critique, the domestic division of labour, and perspectives compared

## Goal
The learner distinguishes joint and segregated conjugal roles, explains Oakley's feminist critique, explains the domestic division of labour across decision-making, money, childcare and leisure, and compares functionalist, feminist and Marxist views.

## Syllabus items taught here
- 3.3.3a - Joint and segregated conjugal roles: identify and distinguish
- 3.3.3b - Oakley's feminist perspective on the conventional family: explain Oakley
- 3.3.3c - The domestic division of labour in traditional and contemporary families: explain change
- 3.3.3d - Decision-making, money management, dual-career families, childcare and leisure: address each area
- 3.3.3e - Functionalist, feminist and Marxist perspectives on conjugal roles compared: compare perspectives

## How to teach this
Ask: 'In a household you know, who tends to do most of the cooking and cleaning, and who tends to make the big financial decisions? Is this split equally, or not — and does it matter?'

#### 3.3.3a Joint and segregated conjugal roles
Conjugal roles are the roles performed by partners within a marriage or relationship. Joint conjugal roles exist where partners share housework, childcare, decision-making and leisure activities relatively equally, and often socialise together. Segregated conjugal roles exist where partners have clearly separate roles, typically the male as breadwinner and the female responsible for housework and childcare, with separate leisure activities and friendship groups.

#### 3.3.3b Oakley's feminist perspective on the conventional family
Ann Oakley researched the division of domestic labour and argued that, despite claims that families had become more equal (a 'symmetrical family'), women in practice continued to carry out the great majority of housework and childcare even when also working outside the home. She argued that the idea of a genuinely joint, equal conjugal role relationship was often more myth than reality, and that women's domestic burden had, if anything, become a 'dual burden' alongside paid work.

#### 3.3.3c The domestic division of labour in traditional and contemporary families
In a traditional division of labour, roles are sharply segregated: the male is the sole or main breadwinner and the female performs virtually all domestic work. In a more contemporary division of labour, ideas of shared responsibility for housework, childcare and paid work are more widely accepted, and men's contribution to domestic tasks has generally increased over recent decades — though sociologists disagree about how far this shift is real equality versus continuing inequality behind a more equal-sounding ideology.

#### 3.3.3d Decision-making, money management, dual-career families, childcare and leisure
Specific areas of conjugal role relationships that sociologists examine include: who makes major decisions (housing, finance, children's schooling) and whether this is joint or dominated by one partner; how money is managed (a shared pool, an allowance system, or separate finances); dual-career families, where both partners work full-time and must negotiate combining two careers with domestic responsibilities; who provides the bulk of childcare; and whether leisure time and activities are shared jointly or pursued separately.

#### 3.3.3e Functionalist, feminist and Marxist perspectives on conjugal roles compared
Functionalists (for example, Parsons) traditionally saw segregated conjugal roles, with a male 'instrumental' breadwinner role and a female 'expressive' emotional/caring role, as a well-adapted division suited to industrial society. Feminists (such as Oakley) argue this division reflects and reinforces patriarchal inequality rather than natural or beneficial specialisation. Marxists link the domestic division of labour to capitalism's interests, arguing women's unpaid domestic labour helps reproduce the workforce cheaply, benefiting the capitalist economic system.
