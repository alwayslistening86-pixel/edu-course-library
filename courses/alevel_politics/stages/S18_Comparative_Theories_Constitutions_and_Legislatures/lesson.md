# S18_Comparative_Theories_Constitutions_and_Legislatures - Lesson: Rational, cultural and structural approaches; comparing UK/US constitutions and legislatures

## Goal
The learner explains the rational, cultural and structural approaches to comparative politics, and uses them to compare the UK and US constitutions and legislatures.

## Syllabus items taught here
- COMP.1 - Theoretical approaches to comparative politics: rational, cultural, structural
- COMP.2 - Comparing UK and US constitutions: codified/uncodified, sources/provisions/principles, separation of powers/checks and balances, federalism vs devolution
- COMP.3 - Comparing UK and US legislatures: powers/strengths/weaknesses of each chamber and the extent to which the two chambers in each system are equal

## How to teach this
Ask: is the UK's uncodified constitution and the USA's codified one mainly a product of different historical circumstances (structural), different political cultures (cultural), or different elites making different rational choices? Teach each area as: what the institution/process/idea is, how it works or what it claims, the debates and tensions around it, and (for the ideologies) which thinkers exemplify it. Push the learner to build a bank of specific, up-to-date examples (named Acts, cases, elections, presidents, policies) they can deploy in an essay, not just abstract description - Edexcel rewards analysis and evaluation (AO2/AO3, 65% of the marks) over bare knowledge (AO1, 35%). Current UK and US politics changes constantly (the sitting Prime Minister and Cabinet, the composition of Congress and the Supreme Court, current party leaders, ongoing court cases and legislation): this course names office-holders and events current as of when it was built, but check a reliable current-affairs source (e.g. instituteforgovernment.org.uk, parliament.uk, congress.gov, supremecourt.gov) before an exam for what has changed since. Source-based questions (Section A of Papers 1 and 2) need the learner to use the source's own detail plus their own knowledge, not just paraphrase it.

#### COMP.1 Theoretical approaches to comparative politics: rational, cultural, structural
**Theoretical approaches to comparative politics.** Rational approach: focuses on individuals within a political system, assuming they act rationally, choosing actions that give them a beneficial outcome - e.g. explaining why members of Congress vote to please their constituents/donors as rational self-interested behaviour aimed at re-election, or why UK MPs follow the party whip as a rational calculation about career advancement. Cultural approach: focuses on groups (voters, parties, pressure/interest groups) within a political system, arguing shared ideas, beliefs and values determine individuals' and institutions' actions - e.g. explaining the USA's strong individualism and suspicion of government (shaping limited government, gun rights, weaker welfare provision) or the UK's greater historical deference to established institutions (monarchy, an uncodified, evolutionary constitution) as products of distinct national political cultures. Structural approach: focuses on the institutions and processes within a political system, arguing political outcomes are largely determined by formal structures/rules rather than individual choice or culture - e.g. explaining that FPTP in the UK structurally produces different outcomes from the US's separated, checks-and-balances presidential system, regardless of who holds office or public attitudes at any given moment. Each approach offers a different, complementary lens; comparative analysis typically draws on more than one to fully explain similarities and differences between systems.

#### COMP.2 Comparing UK and US constitutions: codified/uncodified, sources/provisions/principles, separation of powers/checks and balances, federalism vs devolution
**Comparing UK and US constitutions.** Nature: the UK constitution is uncodified (no single document), unentrenched (changeable by ordinary Act of Parliament) and unitary (though heavily devolved in practice); the US Constitution is codified (a single written document), entrenched (a very high Article V amendment bar) and federal (constitutionally divided sovereignty between national and state governments) - a fundamental structural difference in both form and philosophy. Sources: the UK draws on five diffuse sources (statute, common law, convention, authoritative works, treaties); the US Constitution is the supreme law with judicial interpretation (not amendment) doing most of the work of adapting it to modern circumstances. Provisions and principles: both systems embody separation of powers and checks and balances, but very differently - UK fusion of powers (the executive sits within and depends on the legislature, unlike the US's clearer institutional separation between Congress and the presidency) makes UK checks softer/more political (a government usually controls Parliament via its majority) compared with the US's harder, more legally entrenched checks (e.g. judicial review striking down legislation, which UK courts cannot do to primary legislation). US federalism versus UK devolution: US federalism constitutionally guarantees state power (the Tenth Amendment, difficult to reverse); UK devolution is a grant of power by the sovereign Westminster Parliament, which in strict legal theory could revoke it (though this would be politically explosive and essentially unthinkable in practice for Scotland/Wales/Northern Ireland), a key structural difference even where practical outcomes (policy divergence between nations/states) can look similar.

#### COMP.3 Comparing UK and US legislatures: powers/strengths/weaknesses of each chamber and the extent to which the two chambers in each system are equal
**Comparing UK and US legislatures.** Powers, strengths and weaknesses: the US Congress has stronger formal, constitutionally guaranteed powers independent of the executive (e.g. the President cannot dissolve Congress, and Congress does not depend on maintaining the President's confidence, unlike the fused UK system where the government depends on the Commons); this gives Congress genuine institutional independence to block a president's agenda, especially under divided government, in a way the UK Commons - typically whipped and controlled by a majority government - normally does not exercise against 'its own' government. The UK Parliament, by contrast, usually acts as more of a legitimising and scrutinising body than an independent policy-making rival to the executive, given fusion of powers and party discipline, though the Lords' greater independence provides some contrast (see GOV2.2). Equality of the chambers: the US House and Senate are, despite differences in size/term/constituency, constitutionally close to equal partners (both must pass identical legislation; each has some exclusive powers) - genuine bicameral parity; the UK Commons and Lords are not equal, with the Parliament Acts and the Salisbury Convention establishing clear Commons primacy, making the UK an asymmetric bicameral system.
