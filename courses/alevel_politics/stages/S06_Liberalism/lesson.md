# S06_Liberalism - Lesson: Core ideas, tensions and thinkers of liberalism

## Goal
The learner explains liberalism's core ideas and principles, the tension between classical and modern liberalism, and the key ideas of Locke, Wollstonecraft, Mill, Rawls and Friedan.

## Syllabus items taught here
- LIB.1 - Liberalism: core ideas and principles - individualism, freedom/liberty, the state as necessary evil, rationalism, equality/social justice, liberal democracy
- LIB.2 - Tensions within liberalism: classical liberalism and modern liberalism
- LIB.3 - Liberal thinkers: Locke, Wollstonecraft, Mill, Rawls, Friedan

## How to teach this
Ask: if freedom just means 'being left alone', how can Rawls argue a truly free society needs redistribution of wealth? Teach each area as: what the institution/process/idea is, how it works or what it claims, the debates and tensions around it, and (for the ideologies) which thinkers exemplify it. Push the learner to build a bank of specific, up-to-date examples (named Acts, cases, elections, presidents, policies) they can deploy in an essay, not just abstract description - Edexcel rewards analysis and evaluation (AO2/AO3, 65% of the marks) over bare knowledge (AO1, 35%). Current UK and US politics changes constantly (the sitting Prime Minister and Cabinet, the composition of Congress and the Supreme Court, current party leaders, ongoing court cases and legislation): this course names office-holders and events current as of when it was built, but check a reliable current-affairs source (e.g. instituteforgovernment.org.uk, parliament.uk, congress.gov, supremecourt.gov) before an exam for what has changed since. Source-based questions (Section A of Papers 1 and 2) need the learner to use the source's own detail plus their own knowledge, not just paraphrase it.

#### LIB.1 Liberalism: core ideas and principles - individualism, freedom/liberty, the state as necessary evil, rationalism, equality/social justice, liberal democracy
**Liberalism: core ideas and principles.** Individualism: the primacy of the individual over any group, covering egoistical individualism (self-interested, competitive) and developmental individualism (self-realisation, personal growth as the goal of freedom). Freedom/liberty: the ability to make decisions in your own interest based on your view of human nature; for liberals freedom is 'under the law' (bounded by not harming others), linked tightly to individualism. State: 'necessary' to prevent disorder and protect rights, but potentially 'evil' as it can remove individual liberty, so should be limited - this underpins the liberal preference for limited government. Rationalism: humans are rational, capable of reason and logic, able to define their own best interests and make their own moral choices, creating a progressive society through free exchange of ideas. Equality/social justice: individuals are of equal value and should be treated impartially and fairly, covering foundational equality (equal moral worth), formal equality (equality before the law) and equality of opportunity (a fair starting point, not necessarily equal outcomes). Liberal democracy: balances the popular will (elections) with limited government and respect for civil liberties; liberals support it for legitimacy and accountability but are wary of unchecked majority rule threatening minority rights.

#### LIB.2 Tensions within liberalism: classical liberalism and modern liberalism
**Tensions within liberalism.** Classical liberalism: the earliest liberal tradition, believing individual freedom is best achieved with a minimal state ('negative freedom' - freedom from interference), laissez-faire capitalism, and strong property rights; sceptical of state intervention even to correct inequality. Modern liberalism: emerged from the late 19th/early 20th century as a reaction against unregulated free-market capitalism, which modern liberals argued had left many individuals unfree in practice (poverty, poor education and ill health constraining real choice); modern liberals therefore support 'positive freedom' (the capacity to fulfil one's potential, requiring some state support - e.g. welfare, education) and Keynesian-style state intervention in the economy to enable genuine freedom, while still valuing individual rights and limited, accountable government.

#### LIB.3 Liberal thinkers: Locke, Wollstonecraft, Mill, Rawls, Friedan
**Liberal thinkers.** John Locke (1632-1704): social contract theory - society, state and government rest on a theoretical voluntary agreement among individuals; limited government - government must be limited and based on the consent of the governed, existing to protect natural rights (life, liberty, property). Mary Wollstonecraft (1759-1797): reason - women are rational, independent beings fully capable of reason, undermining arguments for their exclusion from public life; formal equality - women should enjoy full civil liberties and be allowed careers/education to be genuinely free, an early feminist-liberal argument. John Stuart Mill (1806-1873): the harm principle - individuals should be free to do anything except harm others, a foundational limit on state interference; tolerance - popularity of a view does not make it correct, so free, open debate (even of unpopular ideas) benefits society (On Liberty). John Rawls (1921-2002): theory of justice - society must be just and guarantee each citizen a life worth living, including fair distribution of resources; the veil of ignorance - a hypothetical scenario where individuals designing society's rules do not know their own future position in it, which Rawls argues would lead rational people to choose a fairer, more redistributive society (justifying modern-liberal welfare positions). Betty Friedan (1921-2006): legal equality - women are as capable as men and oppressive laws/social attitudes restricting them must be overturned (The Feminine Mystique); equal opportunity - women held back by the limited range of jobs deemed 'acceptable', requiring liberal-feminist reform to open opportunity.
