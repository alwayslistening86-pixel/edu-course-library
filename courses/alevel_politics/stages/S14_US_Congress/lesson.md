# S14_US_Congress - Lesson: Structure and powers; representation; the legislative function; oversight; interpretations and debates

## Goal
The learner explains the structure of Congress and the distribution of its powers, representation and voting behaviour within Congress, the legislative function, oversight, and interpretations/debates about Congress's changing role.

## Syllabus items taught here
- CONG.1 - The structure of Congress and the distribution of powers: bicameral nature, membership, election cycle, enumerated/exclusive/concurrent powers
- CONG.2 - Representation: congressional elections, incumbency, and factors affecting voting behaviour in Congress (parties/caucuses, constituency, interest groups, lobbyists)
- CONG.3 - The legislative function: the legislative process, differences between the chambers, and the policy significance of Congress
- CONG.4 - Oversight: factors influencing Congress-presidency relations, and Congress as a check on the presidency and Supreme Court
- CONG.5 - Interpretations and debates: Congress's changing role/powers, adequacy of representation, changing party significance, and effectiveness of its constitutional powers

## How to teach this
Ask: the US Congress can override a presidential veto, declare war, and control all federal spending - so why do people still call the presidency the most powerful branch? Teach each area as: what the institution/process/idea is, how it works or what it claims, the debates and tensions around it, and (for the ideologies) which thinkers exemplify it. Push the learner to build a bank of specific, up-to-date examples (named Acts, cases, elections, presidents, policies) they can deploy in an essay, not just abstract description - Edexcel rewards analysis and evaluation (AO2/AO3, 65% of the marks) over bare knowledge (AO1, 35%). Current UK and US politics changes constantly (the sitting Prime Minister and Cabinet, the composition of Congress and the Supreme Court, current party leaders, ongoing court cases and legislation): this course names office-holders and events current as of when it was built, but check a reliable current-affairs source (e.g. instituteforgovernment.org.uk, parliament.uk, congress.gov, supremecourt.gov) before an exam for what has changed since. Source-based questions (Section A of Papers 1 and 2) need the learner to use the source's own detail plus their own knowledge, not just paraphrase it.

#### CONG.1 The structure of Congress and the distribution of powers: bicameral nature, membership, election cycle, enumerated/exclusive/concurrent powers
**Structure of Congress and distribution of powers.** Bicameral: the House of Representatives (435 members, apportioned by state population, two-year terms, all seats up every election) and the Senate (100 members, two per state regardless of population, six-year staggered terms with about a third up for election every two years). Election cycle: House elections every two years alongside either a presidential election or a 'midterm' election (midterms often go against the President's party, a recurring pattern reflecting mid-term dissatisfaction); Senate elections staggered so only about a third of seats are contested at a time. Powers given to Congress in the Constitution: enumerated powers (Article I, Section 8) include taxation and spending, regulating interstate/foreign commerce, coining money, declaring war, raising and funding the armed forces, and 'necessary and proper' implied powers. Exclusive powers: the House alone can initiate revenue/tax bills and initiate impeachment (bringing charges by simple majority); the Senate alone confirms presidential nominations (executive appointments and federal judges, by simple majority) and ratifies treaties (two-thirds) and tries impeachment cases (conviction needs two-thirds, removing the office-holder). Concurrent powers: both chambers must pass identical legislation (including budget/appropriations bills) for it to go to the President; both can propose constitutional amendments (two-thirds each).

#### CONG.2 Representation: congressional elections, incumbency, and factors affecting voting behaviour in Congress (parties/caucuses, constituency, interest groups, lobbyists)
**Representation.** Congressional elections and incumbency: incumbents (sitting members seeking re-election) win at very high rates (often 85-95% in the House) due to advantages in name recognition, fundraising, constituency service, and (in many states) favourably drawn districts; this incumbency advantage is a major factor limiting genuine electoral competition and turnover in Congress. Factors affecting voting behaviour within Congress: parties and caucuses (party leadership, whips and ideological caucuses, e.g. the conservative Freedom Caucus among House Republicans or the Congressional Progressive Caucus among House Democrats, pressure members toward a party line, though party discipline is weaker than in the UK's whipped parliamentary system since members are not dependent on the executive for office); constituency (members often vote to reflect their district/state's interests, especially on issues salient to local industries or demographics, sometimes crossing party lines); interest groups and lobbyists (provide campaign funding, expertise and grassroots pressure that can shape a member's vote, especially on issues with concentrated, well-organised interests).

#### CONG.3 The legislative function: the legislative process, differences between the chambers, and the policy significance of Congress
**The legislative function.** Legislative process: a bill is introduced in either chamber, referred to committee (where most bills die without action - the 'committee graveyard'), if reported out it goes to the floor for debate/amendment and a vote, then must pass the other chamber in identical form (often via a conference committee reconciling different versions) before going to the President, who can sign it, veto it (Congress can override by two-thirds in both chambers, a high bar rarely met), or (via a 'pocket veto') let it die if Congress adjourns within ten days without signing. Strengths: extensive committee expertise, opportunities for negotiation and amendment, checks against hasty legislation. Weaknesses: slow, multiple veto points (including the Senate filibuster, requiring 60 votes to end debate on most legislation) make passing major legislation difficult, especially under divided government, contributing to gridlock. Differences between chambers: House rules allow the majority to move legislation relatively efficiently (limited debate via the Rules Committee); the Senate's traditions (unlimited debate, the filibuster, unanimous consent agreements needed for routine business, individual senators' power to place 'holds') give individual senators, and especially the minority party, far more power to obstruct. Policy significance: despite gridlock, Congress remains the source of major legislation (e.g. large-scale spending bills, significant reform Acts) when unified government or sufficient bipartisan agreement exists.

#### CONG.4 Oversight: factors influencing Congress-presidency relations, and Congress as a check on the presidency and Supreme Court
**Oversight.** Factors influencing Congress-presidency relations: divided government (different parties controlling Congress and the presidency) sharply increases friction and oversight intensity; unified government (same party controlling both) tends to produce more cooperative relations and easier legislative success for the President's agenda, though intra-party splits can still cause friction. Congress's role as a check: committees (especially in the chamber controlled by the party opposed to the President) conduct oversight hearings and investigations into executive branch conduct; the Senate's confirmation power over nominees and judges gives leverage over executive/judicial appointments; the power of the purse (appropriations) lets Congress withhold funding from disfavoured programmes; impeachment (House) and trial (Senate) provide an extreme check on a president or federal official for 'high crimes and misdemeanors', though conviction requiring a two-thirds Senate majority has never yet resulted in a president's removal despite several impeachments in modern history. Extent of institutional effectiveness: oversight is generally most vigorous under divided government and can genuinely constrain or embarrass an administration, but is limited by partisan loyalty (a same-party Congress is often reluctant to seriously check 'its own' president) and by the President's own resources (executive privilege claims, agency control) to resist scrutiny.

#### CONG.5 Interpretations and debates: Congress's changing role/powers, adequacy of representation, changing party significance, and effectiveness of its constitutional powers
**Interpretations and debates about Congress.** Changing roles and powers: some argue Congress has ceded power to the presidency over the 20th-21st centuries (delegating broad regulatory authority to executive agencies, allowing an expansive use of executive orders and national-emergency powers, and largely deferring on war powers despite its constitutional war-declaring power), producing an 'imperial presidency' at Congress's expense (see PRES.4); others argue Congress retains decisive power whenever it chooses to use it (the purse, confirmation, oversight, and its ability to simply not act on presidential priorities). Adequacy of Congress's representative role: critics point to low approval ratings, safe/gerrymandered districts reducing real electoral accountability, and the dominance of wealthy donors/PACs in campaign finance as undermining genuine representation; defenders point to strong constituency service, responsiveness to local interests, and regular elections as maintaining real accountability. Changing significance of parties: growing partisan polarisation (fewer ideologically moderate/cross-over members than in past decades) has made Congress more predictable along party lines but also more prone to gridlock, especially combined with the Senate filibuster. Significance and effectiveness of constitutional powers: Congress's formal powers (legislation, spending, war, confirmation, impeachment) remain extensive on paper, but their practical effectiveness varies significantly with party control and unity.
