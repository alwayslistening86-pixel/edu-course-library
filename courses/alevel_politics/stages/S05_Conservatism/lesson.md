# S05_Conservatism - Lesson: Core ideas, tensions and thinkers of conservatism

## Goal
The learner explains conservatism's core ideas and principles, the tensions between traditional, one-nation and New Right conservatism, and the key ideas of Hobbes, Burke, Oakeshott, Rand and Nozick.

## Syllabus items taught here
- CON.1 - Conservatism: core ideas and principles - pragmatism, tradition, human imperfection, organic society/state, paternalism, libertarianism (neo-liberalism)
- CON.2 - Tensions within conservatism: traditional conservatism, one-nation conservatism, and the New Right's fusion of neo-liberalism and neo-conservatism
- CON.3 - Conservative thinkers: Hobbes, Burke, Oakeshott, Rand, Nozick

## How to teach this
Ask: how can a single ideology contain both people who want to preserve the welfare state and people who want to shrink it drastically? Teach each area as: what the institution/process/idea is, how it works or what it claims, the debates and tensions around it, and (for the ideologies) which thinkers exemplify it. Push the learner to build a bank of specific, up-to-date examples (named Acts, cases, elections, presidents, policies) they can deploy in an essay, not just abstract description - Edexcel rewards analysis and evaluation (AO2/AO3, 65% of the marks) over bare knowledge (AO1, 35%). Current UK and US politics changes constantly (the sitting Prime Minister and Cabinet, the composition of Congress and the Supreme Court, current party leaders, ongoing court cases and legislation): this course names office-holders and events current as of when it was built, but check a reliable current-affairs source (e.g. instituteforgovernment.org.uk, parliament.uk, congress.gov, supremecourt.gov) before an exam for what has changed since. Source-based questions (Section A of Papers 1 and 2) need the learner to use the source's own detail plus their own knowledge, not just paraphrase it.

#### CON.1 Conservatism: core ideas and principles - pragmatism, tradition, human imperfection, organic society/state, paternalism, libertarianism (neo-liberalism)
**Conservatism: core ideas and principles.** Pragmatism: a flexible, non-ideological approach, judging policy by 'what works' rather than abstract theory; links to traditional and one-nation conservatism's suspicion of grand schemes. Tradition: the accumulated wisdom of past generations, providing stability, continuity and a sense of belonging/security; underpins support for gradual, 'organic' change rather than revolution. Human imperfection: humans are psychologically, morally and intellectually flawed, so cannot be trusted to remake society from first principles or govern themselves without authority/tradition to restrain them. Organic society/state: society is a living whole, more than the sum of its individual parts, which underpins belief in authority, hierarchy and social cohesion (rejecting atomistic individualism). Paternalism: benign authority exercised from above in the people's interest; traditional conservatives read this as authoritarian ('the state knows best'), one-nation conservatives as a moral obligation on the wealthy to help the less fortunate (noblesse oblige); New Right conservatives reject paternalism as undermining self-reliance. Libertarianism (specifically neo-liberalism): upholds liberty and free choice, mainly in the economy, favouring free markets, low taxation and minimal state intervention.

#### CON.2 Tensions within conservatism: traditional conservatism, one-nation conservatism, and the New Right's fusion of neo-liberalism and neo-conservatism
**Tensions within conservatism.** Traditional conservatism: committed to hierarchic and paternalistic values, cautious about change, respects established institutions (monarchy, church, aristocracy) as sources of stability. One-nation conservatism: originating with Disraeli's response to industrial-era inequality, updates traditional conservatism to accept the need for social reform and paternalist welfare provision to bind the nation together and avoid the "two nations" of rich and poor, avoiding revolution by adapting. New Right: a fusion, dominant from the Thatcher/Reagan era, of neo-liberal economics (free markets, privatisation, deregulation, atomistic individualism, minimal economic intervention) and neo-conservative social values (concern about social fragmentation and permissiveness, tough on law and order and traditional morality) - a tension in itself, since neo-liberal economic freedom can undermine the traditional social order neo-conservatism wants to protect.

#### CON.3 Conservative thinkers: Hobbes, Burke, Oakeshott, Rand, Nozick
**Conservative thinkers.** Thomas Hobbes (1588-1679): argued a strong, ordered sovereign authority is needed to prevent the chaotic 'state of nature' (life 'solitary, poor, nasty, brutish and short'); order should balance humans' need for freedom against their need for security; human nature is needy, vulnerable and easily led astray. Edmund Burke (1729-1797): political change should be approached with great caution and happen organically, building on what exists rather than sweeping it away (his critique of the French Revolution); tradition and empiricism (practices tested and passed down over generations) deserve respect over abstract rationalist schemes. Michael Oakeshott (1901-1990): society is inherently unpredictable and humans imperfect, so politics should be pragmatic ('the pursuit of intimations') rather than driven by grand ideological blueprints. Ayn Rand (1905-1982): objectivism champions rational self-interest as a virtue; supports a pure, laissez-faire capitalist economy and rejects altruism/collectivism as moral failures - closer to libertarian New Right thinking than traditional conservatism. Robert Nozick (1938-2002): libertarianism grounded in the Kantian idea that individuals cannot be used merely as a means to others' ends; self-ownership means individuals own their bodies, talents and labour, justifying minimal state intervention (only a 'night-watchman' state) and strong property rights.
