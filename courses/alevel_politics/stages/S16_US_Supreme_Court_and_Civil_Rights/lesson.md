# S16_US_Supreme_Court_and_Civil_Rights - Lesson: Nature and role; the appointment process; the Court and public policy; civil liberties; race and rights; interpretations and debates

## Goal
The learner explains the nature and role of the Supreme Court, its appointment process, its impact on public policy, the protection of civil liberties and rights, race and rights in contemporary US politics, and interpretations/debates including originalism versus the Living Constitution.

## Syllabus items taught here
- SCOTUS.1 - The nature and role of the Supreme Court: the Constitution, judicial independence, judicial review (Marbury v Madison 1803, Fletcher v Peck 1810)
- SCOTUS.2 - The appointment process: strengths/weaknesses, factors in presidential nomination, current composition and ideological balance
- SCOTUS.3 - The Supreme Court and public policy: impact with examples post-2005; judicial activism vs restraint; Living Constitution vs originalism
- SCOTUS.4 - Protection of civil liberties and rights: the Constitution, Bill of Rights, later amendments, and Supreme Court rulings
- SCOTUS.5 - Race and rights in contemporary US politics: racial rights campaigns' methods/influence/effectiveness on voting rights, affirmative action and representation
- SCOTUS.6 - Interpretations and debates: political vs judicial nature of the Court, effectiveness of rights protection, checks and balances, successes/failures of equality measures

## How to teach this
Ask: nine unelected justices, serving for life, can overturn what an elected Congress and President have done - is that a safeguard for the Constitution, or a democratic problem? Teach each area as: what the institution/process/idea is, how it works or what it claims, the debates and tensions around it, and (for the ideologies) which thinkers exemplify it. Push the learner to build a bank of specific, up-to-date examples (named Acts, cases, elections, presidents, policies) they can deploy in an essay, not just abstract description - Edexcel rewards analysis and evaluation (AO2/AO3, 65% of the marks) over bare knowledge (AO1, 35%). Current UK and US politics changes constantly (the sitting Prime Minister and Cabinet, the composition of Congress and the Supreme Court, current party leaders, ongoing court cases and legislation): this course names office-holders and events current as of when it was built, but check a reliable current-affairs source (e.g. instituteforgovernment.org.uk, parliament.uk, congress.gov, supremecourt.gov) before an exam for what has changed since. Source-based questions (Section A of Papers 1 and 2) need the learner to use the source's own detail plus their own knowledge, not just paraphrase it.

#### SCOTUS.1 The nature and role of the Supreme Court: the Constitution, judicial independence, judicial review (Marbury v Madison 1803, Fletcher v Peck 1810)
**The nature and role of the Supreme Court.** The US Constitution (Article III) establishes 'one supreme Court' but leaves most detail (including its size, currently nine justices) to Congress. Independent nature: justices have life tenure ('during good Behaviour', removable only by impeachment) and salary protection, designed to insulate them from political pressure and majority opinion, letting the Court check the elected branches. Judicial review: the power to rule federal or state laws, or executive actions, unconstitutional and therefore void - not explicit in the Constitution's text but established by the Court itself in Marbury v Madison (1803), which asserted the Court's authority to interpret the Constitution and strike down conflicting legislation, and extended to state laws in Fletcher v Peck (1810), the first case striking down a state law as unconstitutional; judicial review remains the foundation of the Court's power over both Congress and the President.

#### SCOTUS.2 The appointment process: strengths/weaknesses, factors in presidential nomination, current composition and ideological balance
**The appointment process.** Process: the President nominates a candidate for a Supreme Court vacancy; the Senate Judiciary Committee holds hearings and questions the nominee; the full Senate votes, with confirmation requiring only a simple majority (since a 2017 rules change removing the filibuster for Supreme Court nominations). Strengths: combines presidential initiative with senatorial check, intended to produce broadly qualified, vetted nominees. Weaknesses: increasingly partisan and politicised, especially since the 1980s, with nomination fights (e.g. contentious, high-profile confirmation battles) now central to national political conflict; a president and Senate of different parties can produce prolonged vacancies or blocked nominations (e.g. a nominee not receiving a hearing/vote before an election). Factors influencing a president's choice of nominee: judicial philosophy/likely voting pattern (presidents seek nominees whose approach aligns with their own party's priorities), age (younger nominees can serve, and shape the Court, for decades), demographic representation, and 'confirmability' given the Senate's makeup. Current composition and ideological balance: the Court's ideological balance shifts as vacancies arise and are filled by presidents of different parties; justices are informally categorised (not always precisely) as more 'conservative' or 'liberal' in judicial philosophy and voting patterns, and the current balance should be checked against the Court's up-to-date membership before an exam, since it changes whenever a justice retires, dies, or is replaced.

#### SCOTUS.3 The Supreme Court and public policy: impact with examples post-2005; judicial activism vs restraint; Living Constitution vs originalism
**The Supreme Court and public policy.** Impact on public policy: the Court's rulings can reshape entire areas of national policy - for example Citizens United v FEC (2010) transformed campaign finance by ruling that restricting independent political spending by corporations and unions violated the First Amendment, enabling Super PACs (see USDEM.2); more recent landmark rulings (post-2005) have reshaped policy on issues including abortion (Dobbs v Jackson Women's Health Organization, 2022, overturned Roe v Wade's 1973 constitutional right to abortion, returning the issue to the states) and affirmative action in university admissions (Students for Fair Admissions v Harvard, 2023, restricted race-conscious admissions policies). Judicial activism versus judicial restraint: judicial activism holds that judges should interpret the Constitution to address contemporary needs/injustices, sometimes overturning precedent or striking down laws readily; judicial restraint holds that judges should defer heavily to the elected branches and existing precedent (stare decisis), intervening only in the clearest cases - critics of activism argue it lets unelected judges substitute their own policy preferences for democratic decision-making, while critics of excessive restraint argue it can leave injustices unaddressed. Living Constitution versus originalism: Living Constitution interpretation reads the Constitution's principles as evolving with changing social values and circumstances; originalism (including 'strict construction') reads the text according to its original meaning/intent at ratification, arguing this constrains judicial discretion and preserves democratic legitimacy by limiting judges to applying, not rewriting, the law - this philosophical divide underlies many of the Court's most contested rulings.

#### SCOTUS.4 Protection of civil liberties and rights: the Constitution, Bill of Rights, later amendments, and Supreme Court rulings
**Protection of civil liberties and rights.** Rights protected by the original Constitution are limited (e.g. habeas corpus, protection against bills of attainder); the Bill of Rights (the first ten amendments, ratified 1791) added core protections including free speech/religion/press/assembly (First Amendment), the right to bear arms (Second Amendment), protection against unreasonable search and seizure (Fourth Amendment), and due process/rights of the accused (Fifth and Sixth Amendments). Later amendments extended rights further: the Thirteenth (1865, abolishing slavery), Fourteenth (1868, equal protection and due process against state governments, later used to apply most of the Bill of Rights to the states via 'incorporation') and Fifteenth (1870, voting rights regardless of race) Reconstruction amendments; the Nineteenth (1920, women's suffrage) and Twenty-Sixth (1971, voting age 18). Supreme Court rulings have further defined the practical scope of these rights over time (e.g. interpreting free speech, privacy, and equal protection in cases affecting everyday rights), meaning the Court, not just the constitutional text, substantially determines how rights actually apply in practice.

#### SCOTUS.5 Race and rights in contemporary US politics: racial rights campaigns' methods/influence/effectiveness on voting rights, affirmative action and representation
**Race and rights in contemporary US politics.** Voting rights: the Voting Rights Act 1965 banned racially discriminatory voting practices (e.g. literacy tests) and required federal 'preclearance' of voting-law changes in areas with a history of discrimination; the Supreme Court's Shelby County v Holder (2013) ruling struck down the formula determining which areas needed preclearance, effectively ending that federal oversight and leading, critics argue, to new state-level restrictions (voter ID laws, polling place closures, purges of voter rolls) with a disproportionate impact on minority voters, while supporters argue such measures protect election integrity. Affirmative action: race-conscious admissions and employment policies, designed to address historic and ongoing racial inequality, have been progressively restricted by the Supreme Court, culminating in Students for Fair Admissions v Harvard (2023) effectively ending race-conscious university admissions nationally. Representation: continuing debate over racial gerrymandering (drawing electoral districts to dilute or concentrate minority voting power) and its effect on minority representation in Congress and state legislatures. Effectiveness: racial rights campaigns (from the civil rights movement onward) achieved landmark legal change, but persistent disparities in voting access, wealth, incarceration and political representation lead to continuing debate over how much further legal/policy change is needed versus how much has already been achieved.

#### SCOTUS.6 Interpretations and debates: political vs judicial nature of the Court, effectiveness of rights protection, checks and balances, successes/failures of equality measures
**Interpretations and debates: the Supreme Court and civil rights.** Political versus judicial nature: critics argue the Court has become an effectively political institution, with rulings often predictable along the ideological lines of the appointing president's party, undermining claims of neutral legal reasoning; defenders argue justices still genuinely apply legal reasoning/precedent even when outcomes correlate with ideology, and that judicial philosophy (originalism vs Living Constitution) is a legitimate interpretive disagreement, not raw politics. Living Constitution versus originalism: remains the Court's central interpretive fault line, shaping outcomes on issues from abortion to gun rights to the scope of federal power. Effectiveness of rights protection: the Court has both expanded rights (e.g. extending due process and equal protection over the 20th century) and, more recently, narrowed some previously-established rights (e.g. Dobbs overturning Roe), showing rights protection is not simply linear or permanent but can shift with the Court's composition. Extent of powers/checks and balances: the Court has no enforcement power of its own (depending on the executive to enforce rulings) and Congress/the amendment process can in theory override some rulings, but in practice the Court's decisions on constitutional questions are extremely difficult to reverse, giving it substantial practical finality. Successes and failures on equality: successes include the end of legal segregation and formal voting-rights protections; failures/ongoing debates include the erosion of Voting Rights Act enforcement (Shelby County) and affirmative action (Students for Fair Admissions), and continuing disputes over immigration reform's constitutional and policy dimensions.
