# S01_Democracy_and_Participation - Lesson: Direct and representative democracy; the widening franchise; pressure groups and other influences; rights in context

## Goal
The learner explains direct and representative democracy and the case for reform, the widening of the franchise and its remaining debates, how pressure groups and other influences work, and the development and tensions of the UK's rights-based culture.

## Syllabus items taught here
- DP1.1 - Current systems of representative and direct democracy: features, similarities/differences, advantages/disadvantages and the case for reform
- DP1.2 - The widening franchise: 1832 Great Reform Act, 1918/1928/1969 Representation of the People Acts, the suffragists/suffragettes, and a current movement to extend the franchise
- DP1.3 - Pressure groups and other influences: how methods and influence vary, two case-study pressure groups, and think tanks, lobbyists and corporations
- DP1.4 - Rights in context: Magna Carta to the Human Rights Act 1998 and Equality Act 2010, tensions in the UK's rights-based culture, and two civil liberty pressure groups

## How to teach this
Ask: if you could hold a binding national referendum on any one issue tomorrow, what would it be, and why doesn't the UK just do that for everything? Teach each area as: what the institution/process/idea is, how it works or what it claims, the debates and tensions around it, and (for the ideologies) which thinkers exemplify it. Push the learner to build a bank of specific, up-to-date examples (named Acts, cases, elections, presidents, policies) they can deploy in an essay, not just abstract description - Edexcel rewards analysis and evaluation (AO2/AO3, 65% of the marks) over bare knowledge (AO1, 35%). Current UK and US politics changes constantly (the sitting Prime Minister and Cabinet, the composition of Congress and the Supreme Court, current party leaders, ongoing court cases and legislation): this course names office-holders and events current as of when it was built, but check a reliable current-affairs source (e.g. instituteforgovernment.org.uk, parliament.uk, congress.gov, supremecourt.gov) before an exam for what has changed since. Source-based questions (Section A of Papers 1 and 2) need the learner to use the source's own detail plus their own knowledge, not just paraphrase it.

#### DP1.1 Current systems of representative and direct democracy: features, similarities/differences, advantages/disadvantages and the case for reform
**Democracy and participation.** Representative democracy: citizens elect representatives (MPs, councillors) to make decisions on their behalf; dominant model in the UK, justified by practicality in a large, complex state and by giving elected officials accountability at the next election. Direct democracy: citizens decide directly (referendums, e-petitions, citizens' assemblies); origins in ancient Athens; in the UK used sparingly and only advisory in law (e.g. Parliament remains legally sovereign even after a referendum). Similarities: both claim legitimacy from popular consent. Differences: representative democracy relies on trust and periodic accountability, can filter out extreme or uninformed views, but risks a 'democratic deficit' (elected representatives drifting from public opinion between elections) and low participation; direct democracy gives a clear, unmediated mandate on a single issue and can boost engagement (e.g. high turnout for the 2014 Scottish independence referendum, the 2016 EU referendum) but can be manipulated by simplistic campaigning, produce 'tyranny of the majority', and struggles with complex, multi-part questions. Case for reform: proposals include more binding referendums, citizens' assemblies (used by some UK councils on climate policy), and lowering the voting age to 16 (as already used in Scottish and Welsh devolved elections).

#### DP1.2 The widening franchise: 1832 Great Reform Act, 1918/1928/1969 Representation of the People Acts, the suffragists/suffragettes, and a current movement to extend the franchise
**The widening franchise.** The 1832 Great Reform Act: modestly extended the property-based franchise and redistributed some rotten-borough seats, but left the large majority - including all women - without a vote. The Representation of the People Act 1918: gave the vote to all men over 21 and (for the first time) women over 30 who met a property qualification, roughly trebling the electorate; the Representation of the People (Equal Franchise) Act 1928 gave women the vote on the same terms as men (over 21). The Representation of the People Act 1969 lowered the voting age from 21 to 18 for all UK elections. The suffragists (National Union of Women's Suffrage Societies, led by Millicent Fawcett) campaigned constitutionally through lobbying and petitions; the suffragettes (Women's Social and Political Union, led by Emmeline Pankhurst) used direct action, civil disobedience and (controversially) violence/property damage, gaining more publicity but also arguably delaying reform by alienating some opinion; historians debate their relative contribution versus the effect of women's wartime service (1914-18). Current franchise-extension movements include campaigns (e.g. the 'Votes at 16' coalition) to lower the UK-wide voting age to 16, following Scotland and Wales.

#### DP1.3 Pressure groups and other influences: how methods and influence vary, two case-study pressure groups, and think tanks, lobbyists and corporations
**Pressure groups and other influences.** Pressure groups seek to influence government/policy without seeking office themselves (unlike parties). Methods vary with resources and access: insider groups (e.g. the National Farmers' Union) work directly with government through consultation and expert advice; outsider groups (e.g. many environmental and protest groups) use public campaigning, media pressure, demonstrations and sometimes direct action because they lack privileged access. Influence depends on resources (money, membership, expertise), public sympathy, and how far their aims align with the government's priorities. Case studies (two contrasting groups, e.g. the British Medical Association as an insider professional group with regular government access, and Extinction Rebellion as an outsider protest group using disruptive direct action to force media and political attention) illustrate how methods and effectiveness vary. Other collective influences: think tanks (e.g. the Institute for Fiscal Studies, the Adam Smith Institute) research and promote policy ideas; lobbyists are paid professionals who represent client interests to government and Parliament; corporations lobby directly and through trade bodies, raising concerns about undue business influence on policy.

#### DP1.4 Rights in context: Magna Carta to the Human Rights Act 1998 and Equality Act 2010, tensions in the UK's rights-based culture, and two civil liberty pressure groups
**Rights in context.** Magna Carta (1215) established the principle that even the monarch is subject to law, an early foundation of rights-based government. The Human Rights Act 1998 incorporated the European Convention on Human Rights into UK law, letting UK courts rule on rights issues without appealing to Strasbourg; the Equality Act 2010 consolidated anti-discrimination law around protected characteristics (age, disability, race, sex, sexual orientation, religion, etc.). Debates on the UK's rights culture: without a codified, entrenched bill of rights, rights can in principle be overridden by a later ordinary Act of Parliament (parliamentary sovereignty), unlike in the USA; tension between individual rights (e.g. free expression) and collective rights (e.g. a community's right to be free from hate speech) is a recurring theme, as is tension between security and liberty (e.g. anti-terror powers). Civil liberty pressure groups (e.g. Liberty, which campaigns on issues like state surveillance and detention powers, and Big Brother Watch, which focuses on privacy and data rights) litigate, lobby and campaign to defend and extend rights protections.
