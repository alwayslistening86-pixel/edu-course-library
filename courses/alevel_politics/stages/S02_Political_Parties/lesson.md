# S02_Political_Parties - Lesson: Party functions and funding; the established parties; minor parties; parties in context

## Goal
The learner explains the functions and funding of political parties, the origins and development of the three established parties, the importance of minor parties, and the development of a multi-party system and factors affecting party success.

## Syllabus items taught here
- PP2.1 - Political parties: functions and features in UK representative democracy; party funding and funding-reform debates
- PP2.2 - Established parties: the origins and development of the Conservative, Labour and Liberal Democrat parties and how this shaped their policies
- PP2.3 - Emerging and minor UK parties: their importance and the ideas and policies of two minor parties
- PP2.4 - UK parties in context: the development of a multi-party system, and factors (including leadership and the media) affecting party success

## How to teach this
Ask: why do the Conservative and Labour parties still dominate UK elections despite smaller parties often winning millions of votes? Teach each area as: what the institution/process/idea is, how it works or what it claims, the debates and tensions around it, and (for the ideologies) which thinkers exemplify it. Push the learner to build a bank of specific, up-to-date examples (named Acts, cases, elections, presidents, policies) they can deploy in an essay, not just abstract description - Edexcel rewards analysis and evaluation (AO2/AO3, 65% of the marks) over bare knowledge (AO1, 35%). Current UK and US politics changes constantly (the sitting Prime Minister and Cabinet, the composition of Congress and the Supreme Court, current party leaders, ongoing court cases and legislation): this course names office-holders and events current as of when it was built, but check a reliable current-affairs source (e.g. instituteforgovernment.org.uk, parliament.uk, congress.gov, supremecourt.gov) before an exam for what has changed since. Source-based questions (Section A of Papers 1 and 2) need the learner to use the source's own detail plus their own knowledge, not just paraphrase it.

#### PP2.1 Political parties: functions and features in UK representative democracy; party funding and funding-reform debates
**Political parties: functions and funding.** Functions: recruiting and training leaders/candidates; providing a choice of programmes (manifestos) that give the winning party a 'mandate' to govern; representing and aggregating diverse interests into a coherent platform; organising government/opposition in Parliament; educating the electorate on policy issues. Funding: parties are funded by membership subscriptions, donations from individuals/businesses/trade unions, and (limited) state funding (Short Money for opposition parties in the Commons, Cranborne Money for Lords opposition parties, and Policy Development Grants). Debates: large individual/corporate donations raise 'cash for influence' concerns (e.g. donors linked to peerages or policy access); trade union funding for Labour and wealthy individual donors for the Conservatives raise similar concerns from the other side; proposals for a cap on donations or greater state funding are debated but state funding is itself controversial (taxpayers funding parties they may not support) and previous reform attempts (e.g. the Committee on Standards in Public Life's recommendations) have stalled.

#### PP2.2 Established parties: the origins and development of the Conservative, Labour and Liberal Democrat parties and how this shaped their policies
**The established parties' origins and development.** The Conservative Party: traces to 18th/19th-century Tories, formally organised under Robert Peel in the 1830s; historically the party of property, order and gradual change, later embracing one-nation paternalism (Disraeli) and then, from the late 1970s, Thatcherite New Right free-market economics; today combines economic liberalism with varying degrees of social conservatism and (post-2016) a more populist, Brexit-shaped politics on immigration and sovereignty. The Labour Party: founded 1900 (as the Labour Representation Committee) to represent organised labour and the trade union movement in Parliament; historically committed to public ownership and the welfare state (Clause IV, the 1945 Attlee government's NHS and nationalisations); 'New Labour' under Tony Blair (from 1994) moved toward the market-friendly 'Third Way', accepting much of the Thatcher settlement while retaining redistributive aims; under Keir Starmer (leader from 2020, Prime Minister from July 2024) the party has positioned itself as fiscally cautious and pro-growth. The Liberal Democrats: formed 1988 from the merger of the Liberal Party (heir to 19th-century classical liberalism, in long-term decline after Labour's rise) and the SDP (a 1981 breakaway from Labour's right); combine social liberalism, pro-European and constitutional-reform positions (e.g. support for electoral reform); governed in coalition with the Conservatives 2010-15, which damaged their support at the 2015 election.

#### PP2.3 Emerging and minor UK parties: their importance and the ideas and policies of two minor parties
**Emerging and minor UK parties.** Minor parties matter even without winning many seats: they can shift the political agenda (e.g. UKIP/the Brexit Party pushing the Conservatives toward a EU referendum and a harder line on immigration), win concentrated regional support (e.g. the SNP dominating Scottish Westminster seats under FPTP despite a minority of the UK-wide vote, Plaid Cymru in Wales), or represent a distinct ideological niche (e.g. the Green Party on environmental policy, Reform UK on the right of the Conservatives). Two example minor parties: the Green Party of England and Wales - core policies of environmental sustainability, wealth redistribution and proportional representation, historically constrained by FPTP to a handful of seats despite a larger vote share; Reform UK - founded from the Brexit Party, campaigns on immigration control, tax cuts and scepticism of net-zero policy, achieved a significant vote share and won seats at the 2024 general election, illustrating how a new populist-right party can break through.

#### PP2.4 UK parties in context: the development of a multi-party system, and factors (including leadership and the media) affecting party success
**UK parties in context.** The UK has moved from a largely two-party system (Labour/Conservative dominance for most of the 20th century) toward a more multi-party system, especially outside England: the SNP dominates Scotland, Plaid Cymru contests Wales, Northern Ireland has its own party system (DUP, Sinn Féin, Alliance, etc.), and UKIP/Brexit Party/Reform UK and the Greens have all won significant vote shares in England. Implications for government: FPTP still tends to convert a plurality of the vote into a single-party majority (helping the two largest parties), but rising support for smaller parties increases the risk of a hung parliament (as in 2010 and 2017) and coalition or confidence-and-supply arrangements. Factors affecting party success: leadership (a leader's perceived competence, likeability and media performance, e.g. contrasting leadership ratings shaping election outcomes); the state of the economy and 'governing competency'; media coverage and endorsement (historically important, e.g. tabloid backing, though social media has diversified how voters get information); party unity versus visible internal division (which the media amplifies); and the clarity/appeal of the manifesto offer.
