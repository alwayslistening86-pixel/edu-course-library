# S10_PM_and_Executive - Lesson: Structure and powers of the Executive; ministerial responsibility; the Prime Minister and Cabinet

## Goal
The learner explains the structure, role and powers of the Executive, the concept of ministerial responsibility, and the factors governing the Prime Minister-Cabinet relationship, illustrated by one pre-1997 and one post-1997 Prime Minister.

## Syllabus items taught here
- GOV3.1 - The structure, role and powers of the Executive: PM, Cabinet, junior ministers, departments; proposing legislation/budget/policy; royal prerogative and secondary legislative powers
- GOV3.2 - Ministerial responsibility: individual and collective responsibility
- GOV3.3 - The Prime Minister and Cabinet: factors governing ministerial selection and the PM-Cabinet relationship; the power of one pre-1997 and one post-1997 PM to dictate events and policy

## How to teach this
Ask: the Prime Minister isn't directly elected by the whole country and has no written job description in law - so where does all that power actually come from? Teach each area as: what the institution/process/idea is, how it works or what it claims, the debates and tensions around it, and (for the ideologies) which thinkers exemplify it. Push the learner to build a bank of specific, up-to-date examples (named Acts, cases, elections, presidents, policies) they can deploy in an essay, not just abstract description - Edexcel rewards analysis and evaluation (AO2/AO3, 65% of the marks) over bare knowledge (AO1, 35%). Current UK and US politics changes constantly (the sitting Prime Minister and Cabinet, the composition of Congress and the Supreme Court, current party leaders, ongoing court cases and legislation): this course names office-holders and events current as of when it was built, but check a reliable current-affairs source (e.g. instituteforgovernment.org.uk, parliament.uk, congress.gov, supremecourt.gov) before an exam for what has changed since. Source-based questions (Section A of Papers 1 and 2) need the learner to use the source's own detail plus their own knowledge, not just paraphrase it.

#### GOV3.1 The structure, role and powers of the Executive: PM, Cabinet, junior ministers, departments; proposing legislation/budget/policy; royal prerogative and secondary legislative powers
**Structure, role and powers of the Executive.** Structure: the Prime Minister (head of government, first among Cabinet equals, chosen as leader of the party able to command Commons confidence); the Cabinet (senior ministers, roughly 20-25, heading major departments, meeting collectively to agree government policy); junior ministers (Ministers of State, Parliamentary Under-Secretaries) supporting Cabinet ministers within departments; government departments (the civil service machinery implementing policy, e.g. HM Treasury, the Home Office, staffed by permanent, politically neutral civil servants). Main roles: proposing legislation (most bills originate with government, using its Commons majority to pass them); proposing the budget (the Chancellor's Budget statement, setting taxation and spending); making policy decisions within the law and budget set. Main powers: royal prerogative powers (historic monarchical powers now exercised by the PM/government in the monarch's name, e.g. appointing/dismissing ministers, making treaties, deploying armed forces, granting honours - though some, like declaring war, are now subject to a convention of seeking a Commons vote); initiation of legislation (setting the legislative agenda via the King's Speech); secondary legislative power (making detailed regulations under powers delegated by an Act of Parliament, allowing government to update law without full new primary legislation, though this raises accountability concerns given reduced parliamentary scrutiny of secondary legislation).

#### GOV3.2 Ministerial responsibility: individual and collective responsibility
**Ministerial responsibility.** Individual ministerial responsibility: each minister is personally accountable to Parliament for their department's conduct and their own actions; convention holds a minister should resign for serious personal misconduct or a serious departmental failure, though in practice resignations are inconsistent and depend heavily on prime ministerial and media/political pressure rather than being automatic. Collective ministerial responsibility: Cabinet decisions bind all ministers publicly, who must support government policy in public (or resign) even if they disagreed in private Cabinet discussion, preserving a united public front and government authority; can be suspended by the PM for specific issues (e.g. a 'free vote' or an agreed 'agreement to differ', historically used rarely, such as during the 1975 EEC referendum), and breaches (public ministerial dissent) usually lead to dismissal or resignation.

#### GOV3.3 The Prime Minister and Cabinet: factors governing ministerial selection and the PM-Cabinet relationship; the power of one pre-1997 and one post-1997 PM to dictate events and policy
**The Prime Minister and Cabinet.** Factors governing ministerial selection: balancing competence, party unity (representing different party factions/wings), loyalty, and sometimes demographic/regional balance; a PM must also manage rivals by bringing potential critics into Cabinet ('keep your enemies closer') or by rewarding loyalty. Factors affecting the PM-Cabinet relationship: a PM's electoral mandate/majority size, personal authority and media dominance can shift power toward 'prime ministerial' or even 'presidential' style government (bypassing full Cabinet discussion via inner circles/special advisers), while a small majority, party splits, or personal unpopularity can force more genuine collective Cabinet government and greater dependence on senior colleagues. Illustrative PMs: Margaret Thatcher (1979-90, pre-1997) - increasingly dominant and unilateral style especially after 1983's landslide, side-lining Cabinet on key issues (e.g. the poll tax, economic policy), but ultimately removed by her own party/Cabinet in 1990 when support collapsed, showing limits on even a dominant PM's control; and (post-1997) either Tony Blair (1997-2007) - large majorities and personal popularity enabled 'sofa government' bypassing formal Cabinet process especially on Iraq, though later constrained by falling popularity and Gordon Brown's rival power base, or a more recent PM such as Boris Johnson (2019-22) - large 2019 majority and personal 'Vote Leave' mandate gave initial dominance, but was ultimately forced to resign in 2022 after a wave of ministerial resignations and loss of party/Cabinet confidence over conduct scandals, again showing that even a large majority does not guarantee lasting control if a PM loses their party's confidence.
