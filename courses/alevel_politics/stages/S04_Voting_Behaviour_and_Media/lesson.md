# S04_Voting_Behaviour_and_Media - Lesson: Three key general elections; the influence of the media

## Goal
The learner explains three key general elections and the factors behind their outcomes (party policy/manifestos/leadership/campaigns, class/partisan factors, demographic factors) and the influence of the media on politics.

## Syllabus items taught here
- VB4.1 - Case studies of three general elections (1945-92, 1997, and one since 1997): outcomes, party policy/manifestos/leadership/campaigns, class and partisan factors, and demographic factors
- VB4.2 - The influence of the media on politics between and during elections, including media bias and the role of opinion polls

## How to teach this
Ask: was Labour's 1997 landslide really about Tony Blair, or would almost any Labour leader have won that year? Teach each area as: what the institution/process/idea is, how it works or what it claims, the debates and tensions around it, and (for the ideologies) which thinkers exemplify it. Push the learner to build a bank of specific, up-to-date examples (named Acts, cases, elections, presidents, policies) they can deploy in an essay, not just abstract description - Edexcel rewards analysis and evaluation (AO2/AO3, 65% of the marks) over bare knowledge (AO1, 35%). Current UK and US politics changes constantly (the sitting Prime Minister and Cabinet, the composition of Congress and the Supreme Court, current party leaders, ongoing court cases and legislation): this course names office-holders and events current as of when it was built, but check a reliable current-affairs source (e.g. instituteforgovernment.org.uk, parliament.uk, congress.gov, supremecourt.gov) before an exam for what has changed since. Source-based questions (Section A of Papers 1 and 2) need the learner to use the source's own detail plus their own knowledge, not just paraphrase it.

#### VB4.1 Case studies of three general elections (1945-92, 1997, and one since 1997): outcomes, party policy/manifestos/leadership/campaigns, class and partisan factors, and demographic factors
**Three key general elections.** 1979 (from the 1945-92 period): Margaret Thatcher's Conservatives defeated Labour amid the 'Winter of Discontent' strikes, high inflation and a sense of national decline; class-based voting was still strong (most working-class voters backed Labour, most middle-class voters the Conservatives) but Thatcher won significant working-class 'Basildon Man' support, an early sign of class dealignment; governing competency (Labour seen as unable to control the unions/economy) and Thatcher's clear ideological offer were decisive. 1997: Tony Blair's 'New Labour' won a landslide (418 seats) after 18 years of Conservative government; factors included Conservative 'sleaze' scandals and internal division over Europe, the lingering damage of 1992's 'Black Wednesday' to the Conservatives' economic reputation, Blair's centrist rebranding (dropping Clause IV) reassuring middle-class 'Mondeo Man' voters, a slick, professionalised campaign, and continued partisan/class dealignment making voters more willing to switch. 2019 (since 1997): Boris Johnson's Conservatives won a majority of 80 on a 'Get Brexit Done' campaign that broke through Labour's traditional 'red wall' seats in the Midlands and North, showing how a single dominant issue (Brexit) could override historic class/partisan loyalty; Labour under Jeremy Corbyn suffered from a perceived lack of clarity on Brexit and leadership unpopularity. Across these elections: class dealignment and partisan dealignment (weaker automatic loyalty to a 'class party') have grown since the 1970s; valence factors (perceived governing competence, leadership) and short-term campaign factors have become relatively more important than long-term class identity; gender, age, ethnicity and region increasingly shape voting (e.g. a marked age gap in 2017/2019, with younger voters favouring Labour and older voters the Conservatives; graduates favouring Labour/Lib Dems, non-graduates the Conservatives/Reform).

#### VB4.2 The influence of the media on politics between and during elections, including media bias and the role of opinion polls
**The influence of the media.** Newspapers historically claimed to swing elections (e.g. The Sun's 1992 'It's The Sun Wot Won It' headline after backing the Conservatives), though political scientists debate whether media coverage shapes opinion or mainly reflects/reinforces existing views (partisan readers choosing like-minded papers). Broadcast media (BBC, ITV) are legally required to be impartial, giving televised leaders' debates (since 2010) significant influence on campaign momentum. Social media has transformed campaigning: direct party messaging, micro-targeted advertising and viral content (both official and from ordinary users) now shape campaigns alongside traditional outlets, raising concerns about echo chambers, disinformation and unregulated spending on online ads. Opinion polls influence coverage and narrative (a 'horse race' framing) and can affect turnout/tactical voting (though famous polling failures, e.g. underestimating Conservative support in 2015, show their limits); media bias (perceived pro-Conservative bias in most national newspapers, and periodic accusations of bias against the BBC from multiple sides) remains a persistent debate about fairness in UK elections.
