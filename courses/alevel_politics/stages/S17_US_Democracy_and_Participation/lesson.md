# S17_US_Democracy_and_Participation - Lesson: US elections; campaign finance; party ideas; party coalitions; interest groups; interpretations and debates

## Goal
The learner explains US electoral processes, campaign finance, the key ideas of the Democratic and Republican parties and their internal divisions and voter coalitions, interest groups, and interpretations/debates about US democracy and participation.

## Syllabus items taught here
- USDEM.1 - US elections: constitutional requirements, invisible primary, primaries/caucuses, National Party Conventions, the Electoral College, incumbency, congressional elections
- USDEM.2 - Campaign finance: current legislation and the implications of Supreme Court rulings (McCain-Feingold 2002, Citizens United v FEC 2010)
- USDEM.3 - Democratic and Republican ideas: attitudes to social/moral issues and crime, economic intervention, social welfare, and immigration
- USDEM.4 - Party conflicts and coalitions: factions within each party, and voter coalitions (race, religion, gender, education) in a post-2000 presidential election
- USDEM.5 - Interest groups in the USA: influence, methods and power of a single-interest, professional or policy group
- USDEM.6 - Interpretations and debates: the Electoral College and reform, campaign-finance reform, incumbency, and the role/influence of interest groups

## How to teach this
Ask: a candidate can win the presidency while getting fewer votes nationwide than their opponent - how does the Electoral College make that possible, and why hasn't it been reformed? Teach each area as: what the institution/process/idea is, how it works or what it claims, the debates and tensions around it, and (for the ideologies) which thinkers exemplify it. Push the learner to build a bank of specific, up-to-date examples (named Acts, cases, elections, presidents, policies) they can deploy in an essay, not just abstract description - Edexcel rewards analysis and evaluation (AO2/AO3, 65% of the marks) over bare knowledge (AO1, 35%). Current UK and US politics changes constantly (the sitting Prime Minister and Cabinet, the composition of Congress and the Supreme Court, current party leaders, ongoing court cases and legislation): this course names office-holders and events current as of when it was built, but check a reliable current-affairs source (e.g. instituteforgovernment.org.uk, parliament.uk, congress.gov, supremecourt.gov) before an exam for what has changed since. Source-based questions (Section A of Papers 1 and 2) need the learner to use the source's own detail plus their own knowledge, not just paraphrase it.

#### USDEM.1 US elections: constitutional requirements, invisible primary, primaries/caucuses, National Party Conventions, the Electoral College, incumbency, congressional elections
**US elections.** Presidential elections: constitutional requirements (natural-born citizen, at least 35, 14 years' US residency); the 'invisible primary' (the informal pre-primary period of fundraising, endorsements and positioning before voting starts); primaries (most states, direct voter elections to choose delegates pledged to a candidate) and caucuses (a minority of states, local meetings to select delegates, e.g. historically Iowa's early caucus); National Party Conventions (formally nominate the candidate and adopt a party platform, now largely ceremonial given primaries/caucuses decide the outcome in advance); the Electoral College (538 electors, one per member of Congress plus 3 for Washington DC, allocated by state - all but two states use winner-takes-all, so a candidate can win the presidency while losing the national popular vote, as in 2000 and 2016); this system has shaped the resulting party system by concentrating campaign effort on a relatively small number of competitive 'swing states' while safe states receive little attention. Incumbency: an incumbent president seeking a second term typically enjoys major advantages (name recognition, fundraising, the ability to shape news coverage through the office itself, and - if seeking re-election - a largely uncontested primary), though a struggling economy or unpopular record can outweigh these advantages. Congressional elections and incumbency: covered under CONG.2 (very high re-election rates driven by name recognition, fundraising and often favourable district boundaries).

#### USDEM.2 Campaign finance: current legislation and the implications of Supreme Court rulings (McCain-Feingold 2002, Citizens United v FEC 2010)
**Campaign finance.** Legislation: the Federal Election Campaign Act (1971, amended 1974) established disclosure requirements and contribution limits and created the Federal Election Commission; the Bipartisan Campaign Reform Act (McCain-Feingold, 2002) restricted 'soft money' (unregulated donations to parties for issue advocacy) and regulated 'issue ads' close to an election. Implications of Supreme Court rulings: Citizens United v FEC (2010) ruled that restricting independent political spending by corporations, unions and other groups violated First Amendment free-speech rights, enabling the rise of Super PACs (Political Action Committees that can raise and spend unlimited money on independent political advertising, provided they do not formally coordinate with a candidate's campaign) alongside traditional PACs (which face contribution limits when giving directly to candidates); this has dramatically increased the scale of outside spending in US elections and intensified debate over the influence of wealthy donors and organised interests on campaigns and, by extension, policy.

#### USDEM.3 Democratic and Republican ideas: attitudes to social/moral issues and crime, economic intervention, social welfare, and immigration
**Democratic and Republican ideas.** Democrats: generally more progressive/liberal on social and moral issues (e.g. LGBT+ rights, abortion rights post-Dobbs debates, criminal-justice reform); favour greater government intervention in the national economy (regulation, progressive taxation, expanded social programmes); support more extensive government provision of social welfare (healthcare access, safety-net programmes); generally more supportive of expanded legal immigration pathways and protections for undocumented immigrants already in the country. Republicans: generally more conservative on social and moral issues (e.g. more restrictive abortion positions post-Dobbs, traditional views on family/religion, tougher criminal-justice stances); favour more restricted government intervention in the economy (lower taxes, deregulation) while often still protecting American trade/jobs through measures like tariffs; accept some social welfare provision but emphasise personal responsibility and limiting its scope/cost; generally favour stricter immigration enforcement and border security. These are general tendencies, not universal positions - both parties contain internal disagreement (see USDEM.4).

#### USDEM.4 Party conflicts and coalitions: factions within each party, and voter coalitions (race, religion, gender, education) in a post-2000 presidential election
**Party conflicts and coalitions.** Current conflicts/tendencies within the Democratic Party: liberals/progressives (pushing for more ambitious economic and social reform), moderates (more centrist, cautious on the scale of economic intervention) and a smaller conservative-leaning wing (especially in some swing districts/states), creating real tension over how far left the party's agenda should go. Within the Republican Party: moderates (more traditional, establishment-oriented), social conservatives (prioritising religious/traditional-values issues), and fiscal conservatives (prioritising tax cuts, deregulation and reduced spending), with a populist, immigration-and-trade-focused wing that has grown markedly influential in recent elections, sometimes in tension with traditional fiscal/free-trade conservatism. Coalition of supporters: in a recent presidential election since 2000, voting patterns have shown race (non-white voters, especially Black voters, have voted heavily Democratic; white voters have leaned more Republican, though with regional/educational variation), religion (white evangelical Christians vote heavily Republican; religiously unaffiliated voters lean heavily Democratic), gender (a persistent 'gender gap', with women somewhat more likely to vote Democratic and men more likely Republican) and education (a growing 'diploma divide', with college-educated voters - especially white college graduates - trending more Democratic and non-college-educated voters, including a growing share of non-white working-class voters, trending more Republican than in earlier decades).

#### USDEM.5 Interest groups in the USA: influence, methods and power of a single-interest, professional or policy group
**Interest groups in the USA.** Types: single-interest groups (focused on one issue, e.g. gun rights or gun control organisations), professional groups (e.g. the American Medical Association representing doctors' interests) and policy groups (broader ideological/public-policy organisations). Significance, resources and tactics: well-resourced groups use lobbying (direct contact with legislators/staff), campaign contributions (via PACs, within legal limits) and independent spending (via Super PACs, unlimited since Citizens United), litigation (funding court challenges to advance policy goals), and grassroots mobilisation (member activism, public campaigns) to influence all three branches of government - Congress (lobbying members, providing technical expertise on complex legislation), the presidency (advocacy during rule-making, influencing executive-branch appointments) and the courts (filing amicus curiae 'friend of the court' briefs, funding strategic litigation to shape precedent, as seen in major rights-related Supreme Court cases). Example: the National Rifle Association (NRA) has for decades been one of the most influential single-interest groups, shaping gun-policy debates through lobbying, campaign spending and grassroots mobilisation of its membership, illustrating both the reach and the controversy such groups can generate.

#### USDEM.6 Interpretations and debates: the Electoral College and reform, campaign-finance reform, incumbency, and the role/influence of interest groups
**Interpretations and debates of US democracy and participation.** Electoral College: critics argue it is undemocratic (can elect a president who lost the popular vote, as in 2000 and 2016; concentrates campaign attention on a few swing states while most states are ignored) and campaign for reform or abolition (e.g. proposals for a National Popular Vote Interstate Compact); defenders argue it protects smaller/less-populous states' influence and preserves federalism in presidential selection, and note that formal abolition would require a near-impossible constitutional amendment. Campaign finance: critics argue Citizens United and Super PAC spending have given wealthy donors and organised interests outsized influence over elections and policy, undermining political equality; defenders argue independent spending is protected political speech under the First Amendment, and that reform faces the same difficulty of a constitutional-level free-speech question, making effective reform hard to achieve within current constitutional interpretation. Incumbency: seen by critics as entrenching an unresponsive political class and reducing genuine competition; by supporters as reflecting legitimate advantages of experience and demonstrated constituency service. Role and influence of interest groups: seen by critics as enabling wealthy, well-organised interests to dominate policy over the broader public interest ('interest-group liberalism'/pluralist stagnation); by supporters (classical pluralists) as a healthy expression of political participation and a genuine channel for diverse voices to be heard in a large, complex political system.
