# S19_Comparing_Executives_Judiciaries_and_Democracy - Lesson: Comparing UK/US executives, supreme courts and civil rights, and democracy and participation

## Goal
The learner compares UK and US executives, supreme courts and civil rights, and democracy and participation, using rational, cultural and structural approaches to explain the similarities and differences.

## Syllabus items taught here
- COMP.4 - Comparing UK and US executives: role and powers of the President vs the Prime Minister, and accountability to the legislature
- COMP.5 - Comparing UK and US supreme courts and civil rights: basis/extent of powers, judicial independence, effectiveness of rights protection, interest groups' role
- COMP.6 - Comparing UK and US democracy and participation: party systems, internal party unity, party policy profiles, campaign-finance debates, pressure/interest group power

## How to teach this
Ask: a UK Prime Minister can be removed by their own MPs within weeks; a US President serves a fixed four-year term whatever Congress thinks. Which system makes the executive more accountable - and which makes it more stable? Teach each area as: what the institution/process/idea is, how it works or what it claims, the debates and tensions around it, and (for the ideologies) which thinkers exemplify it. Push the learner to build a bank of specific, up-to-date examples (named Acts, cases, elections, presidents, policies) they can deploy in an essay, not just abstract description - Edexcel rewards analysis and evaluation (AO2/AO3, 65% of the marks) over bare knowledge (AO1, 35%). Current UK and US politics changes constantly (the sitting Prime Minister and Cabinet, the composition of Congress and the Supreme Court, current party leaders, ongoing court cases and legislation): this course names office-holders and events current as of when it was built, but check a reliable current-affairs source (e.g. instituteforgovernment.org.uk, parliament.uk, congress.gov, supremecourt.gov) before an exam for what has changed since. Source-based questions (Section A of Papers 1 and 2) need the learner to use the source's own detail plus their own knowledge, not just paraphrase it.

#### COMP.4 Comparing UK and US executives: role and powers of the President vs the Prime Minister, and accountability to the legislature
**Comparing UK and US executives.** Role and powers: the US President is both Head of State and Head of Government, directly (via the Electoral College) elected for a fixed four-year term (maximum two terms, Twenty-Second Amendment), independent of the legislature; the UK Prime Minister is Head of Government only (the monarch is Head of State), not directly elected by the whole country (chosen as the leader able to command Commons confidence), and serves with no fixed term, removable at any time by losing a confidence vote or their own party's support. This produces significant differences: the US President has guaranteed tenure (barring impeachment/conviction, a very high bar) regardless of Congress's wishes, giving stability but also potential for prolonged gridlock if unpopular yet unremovable; the UK PM's tenure is more precarious and directly tied to maintaining party/Commons support, giving more continuous accountability but also more instability (several UK PMs removed by their own parties in recent years, unthinkable for a sitting US president absent conviction on impeachment). Accountability to the legislature: the UK PM and government are continuously accountable to the Commons (PMQs, votes of confidence, ministerial questions) and can fall if they lose its support; the US President is not accountable to Congress in this direct way (cannot be removed by an ordinary no-confidence vote) but faces Congress's other checks (impeachment, appropriations, confirmation, oversight) as covered under CONG.4.

#### COMP.5 Comparing UK and US supreme courts and civil rights: basis/extent of powers, judicial independence, effectiveness of rights protection, interest groups' role
**Comparing UK and US supreme courts and civil rights.** Basis and extent of powers: the US Supreme Court's power of judicial review lets it strike down primary legislation as unconstitutional (established in Marbury v Madison); the UK Supreme Court, given parliamentary sovereignty, cannot strike down an Act of Parliament, only rule secondary/executive action unlawful (ultra vires) or declare primary legislation incompatible with the Human Rights Act (leaving Parliament free to act or not) - a fundamentally narrower formal power. Relative independence: both courts have structurally protected judicial independence, but the US Court's justices have life tenure (compared with a mandatory retirement age of 75 for UK Supreme Court justices) and US appointments are far more openly partisan/contested given their permanence and constitutional-strike-down power; UK judicial appointments are comparatively less politically contested, partly because the stakes (no power to void statute) are lower. Effectiveness of rights protection: the codified, entrenched US Bill of Rights and later amendments give US rights strong, court-enforceable constitutional status; UK rights protection rests more on the Human Rights Act (an ordinary statute, in principle repealable by a simple Commons majority) and common law, giving somewhat weaker formal entrenchment, though in practice both systems provide substantial rights protection in different ways. Interest groups' role in protecting rights: in the USA, interest groups (e.g. the American Civil Liberties Union) fund extensive strategic litigation aimed at shaping Supreme Court precedent, a distinctively powerful avenue given the Court's ability to void legislation; in the UK, civil liberties groups (e.g. Liberty) more often combine litigation with direct lobbying of Parliament, since UK courts cannot deliver the same kind of permanent, legislation-overriding victory.

#### COMP.6 Comparing UK and US democracy and participation: party systems, internal party unity, party policy profiles, campaign-finance debates, pressure/interest group power
**Comparing UK and US democracy and participation.** Party systems: the USA has an entrenched two-party system (Democrats and Republicans), reinforced by FPTP-style single-member-district elections for Congress and the Electoral College's winner-takes-all logic in most states, making third-party breakthroughs extremely rare; the UK, while historically dominated by two major parties under Westminster FPTP, has become more genuinely multi-party (SNP, Liberal Democrats, Greens, Reform UK) especially given devolved bodies' more proportional systems, as covered under PP2.4. Internal party unity: US parties are relatively loose coalitions with weaker central discipline over individual members of Congress (who are not dependent on party leadership for their political survival in the way a UK MP depends on the whip for career advancement and, ultimately, re-selection), producing more visible internal factions (e.g. the Freedom Caucus, moderate Democrats) able to defy their own party's leadership; UK parties are generally more centrally disciplined via the whip system, tied to the fused-powers need for government to maintain Commons confidence. Policy profiles: broadly, UK Labour and the US Democratic Party, and the UK Conservatives and the US Republican Party, occupy roughly comparable left/right positions on many economic and social issues, though the overall UK political spectrum sits somewhat left of the US spectrum on issues like healthcare provision and gun control, reflecting different political cultures. Campaign finance: UK party/campaign spending is tightly capped by law (with far lower overall election spending than in the USA); US campaign finance is comparatively unrestricted for independent spending post-Citizens United, producing vastly larger sums, a clear structural/legal difference with major consequences for the role of money in each system. Pressure/interest groups: both systems feature extensive group activity (lobbying, campaigning, litigation), but US groups generally have more numerous formal access points to influence (separated branches, federal and state levels, an elected judiciary role via litigation strategy) than the more centralised UK system, where a single sovereign Westminster Parliament and a whipped executive concentrate the targets for effective lobbying.
