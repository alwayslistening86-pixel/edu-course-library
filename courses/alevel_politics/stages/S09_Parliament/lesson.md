# S09_Parliament - Lesson: Commons and Lords: structure, comparative powers, the legislative process, and Parliament-Executive interaction

## Goal
The learner explains the structure and role of the Commons and Lords, their comparative powers, the legislative process, and how Parliament interacts with the Executive.

## Syllabus items taught here
- GOV2.1 - The structure and role of the Commons and Lords: membership, types of peer, and the extent to which their functions are fulfilled
- GOV2.2 - The comparative powers of the Commons and Lords: exclusive Commons powers, the Lords' main powers, and debates on their relative power
- GOV2.3 - The legislative process: the stages a bill goes through, and Commons-Lords interaction including the Salisbury Convention
- GOV2.4 - Parliament and the Executive: backbenchers and parliamentary privilege, select committees, public bill committees, the Official Opposition, and ministerial question time/PMQs

## How to teach this
Ask: if the House of Lords is unelected, why does it still get to delay and amend government legislation? Teach each area as: what the institution/process/idea is, how it works or what it claims, the debates and tensions around it, and (for the ideologies) which thinkers exemplify it. Push the learner to build a bank of specific, up-to-date examples (named Acts, cases, elections, presidents, policies) they can deploy in an essay, not just abstract description - Edexcel rewards analysis and evaluation (AO2/AO3, 65% of the marks) over bare knowledge (AO1, 35%). Current UK and US politics changes constantly (the sitting Prime Minister and Cabinet, the composition of Congress and the Supreme Court, current party leaders, ongoing court cases and legislation): this course names office-holders and events current as of when it was built, but check a reliable current-affairs source (e.g. instituteforgovernment.org.uk, parliament.uk, congress.gov, supremecourt.gov) before an exam for what has changed since. Source-based questions (Section A of Papers 1 and 2) need the learner to use the source's own detail plus their own knowledge, not just paraphrase it.

#### GOV2.1 The structure and role of the Commons and Lords: membership, types of peer, and the extent to which their functions are fulfilled
**Structure and role of the Commons and Lords.** House of Commons: 650 MPs elected by FPTP in single-member constituencies at a general election (or by-election); the primary chamber, source of government (the largest party/coalition able to command confidence forms government) and the only chamber that can remove a government via a vote of no confidence. House of Lords: unelected; members include life peers (appointed, mostly for distinguished public service or party service, the large majority of the current membership), a reduced number of hereditary peers (retained under a 1999 compromise, elected by other hereditary peers when a vacancy arises), and Lords Spiritual (senior Church of England bishops). Main functions: legislating (scrutinising and amending bills), holding government to account (questions, committees), representing constituents (Commons only, via MPs' casework) and deliberating on public policy. Extent functions are fulfilled: the Commons' scrutiny is limited by government's usual majority and whipping discipline; the Lords, lacking direct electoral legitimacy, provides valuable revising/expert scrutiny (many peers bring professional expertise) but faces persistent legitimacy questions given its unelected, part-appointed nature and its size (larger than the Commons).

#### GOV2.2 The comparative powers of the Commons and Lords: exclusive Commons powers, the Lords' main powers, and debates on their relative power
**Comparative powers of the Commons and Lords.** Exclusive Commons powers: only the Commons can bring down a government via a vote of no confidence; under the Parliament Acts (1911, 1949) the Lords cannot block a Commons-passed bill indefinitely (only delay it, typically about a year for most bills) and cannot amend or reject 'money bills' (budgetary legislation) at all. Main Lords powers: can revise, amend and delay legislation (forcing the Commons to reconsider, 'ping-pong' between the chambers), and can scrutinise/block secondary legislation in ways the Commons rarely does. Debates on relative power: some argue the Lords is now the more effective scrutiny chamber (less whipped, more independent-minded, more expert), even inflicting significant government defeats requiring concessions; others argue its lack of a democratic mandate means it should defer to the elected Commons (the 'Salisbury Convention', see GOV2.3) and that ultimate power clearly rests with the Commons given the Parliament Acts and the Lords' inability to block money bills or indefinitely obstruct manifesto commitments.

#### GOV2.3 The legislative process: the stages a bill goes through, and Commons-Lords interaction including the Salisbury Convention
**The legislative process.** A bill typically passes: First Reading (formal introduction, no debate); Second Reading (main debate on principles, a vote); Committee Stage (detailed line-by-line scrutiny and amendment, usually by a Public Bill Committee in the Commons, or by the whole House in the Lords); Report Stage (further amendments considered by the whole House); Third Reading (final vote on the bill as amended); then the process repeats in the other House, with any disagreements resolved through 'ping-pong' (bill shuttling between Houses until agreement or the Parliament Acts are invoked); finally Royal Assent (a formality, not refused since 1708). Commons-Lords interaction: the Salisbury Convention holds that the unelected Lords should not block at Second Reading or 'wreck' a bill implementing a manifesto commitment of the elected government, respecting the Commons' democratic mandate, though the Lords may still amend such bills; this convention is itself sometimes contested when applied to coalition or minority governments without a clear single manifesto mandate.

#### GOV2.4 Parliament and the Executive: backbenchers and parliamentary privilege, select committees, public bill committees, the Official Opposition, and ministerial question time/PMQs
**Parliament and the Executive.** Backbenchers (MPs/peers without ministerial or shadow-ministerial roles) hold government to account through debates, questions, and by voting against their own party (a 'rebellion') on issues of conscience or constituency concern; parliamentary privilege protects free speech in proceedings from defamation action, letting MPs raise sensitive matters. Select committees (one per government department, e.g. the Treasury Select Committee, plus cross-cutting committees like Public Accounts) scrutinise policy, spending and administration, call ministers/officials/experts to give evidence, and publish reports government must formally respond to (though committees cannot force policy change); their chairs are elected by the whole House (since 2010), increasing their independence from party control; Public Bill Committees, by contrast, scrutinise the text of a specific bill during its Committee Stage and are more subject to party whipping given their composition reflects Commons party strength. The Official Opposition (the largest non-government party) forms a Shadow Cabinet mirroring government departments, leads scrutiny in debates, and can use Opposition Day debates to force votes on chosen topics (though these are rarely binding on government). Ministerial question time and Prime Minister's Questions (PMQs, weekly) provide direct, public accountability, though critics argue PMQs is more theatrical point-scoring than substantive scrutiny, while defenders note it forces the PM to defend government record and respond to current events unscripted.
