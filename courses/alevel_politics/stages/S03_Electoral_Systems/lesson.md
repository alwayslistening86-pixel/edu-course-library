# S03_Electoral_Systems - Lesson: FPTP, AMS, STV and CPLS; referendums; electoral system analysis

## Goal
The learner explains the UK's electoral systems (FPTP, AMS, STV, CPLS) and their advantages/disadvantages, the use of referendums since 1997, and debates on why different systems are used and their impact on government, representation and voter choice.

## Syllabus items taught here
- ES3.1 - Electoral systems: FPTP, AMS, STV and the Closed Proportional List System (CPLS), their advantages/disadvantages, and FPTP compared with a devolved system
- ES3.2 - Referendums: how they have been used in the UK since 1997 and their impact; the case for and against referendums
- ES3.3 - Electoral system analysis: why different systems are used in the UK, and their impact on government formation, party representation and voter choice

## How to teach this
Ask: why does the UK use one voting system for Westminster and different ones for Scotland, Wales, London and the European Parliament (when it existed)? Teach each area as: what the institution/process/idea is, how it works or what it claims, the debates and tensions around it, and (for the ideologies) which thinkers exemplify it. Push the learner to build a bank of specific, up-to-date examples (named Acts, cases, elections, presidents, policies) they can deploy in an essay, not just abstract description - Edexcel rewards analysis and evaluation (AO2/AO3, 65% of the marks) over bare knowledge (AO1, 35%). Current UK and US politics changes constantly (the sitting Prime Minister and Cabinet, the composition of Congress and the Supreme Court, current party leaders, ongoing court cases and legislation): this course names office-holders and events current as of when it was built, but check a reliable current-affairs source (e.g. instituteforgovernment.org.uk, parliament.uk, congress.gov, supremecourt.gov) before an exam for what has changed since. Source-based questions (Section A of Papers 1 and 2) need the learner to use the source's own detail plus their own knowledge, not just paraphrase it.

#### ES3.1 Electoral systems: FPTP, AMS, STV and the Closed Proportional List System (CPLS), their advantages/disadvantages, and FPTP compared with a devolved system
**Electoral systems.** First-past-the-post (FPTP): used for UK general elections; single-member constituencies, the candidate with the most votes wins (a plurality, not necessarily a majority). Advantages: simple, produces a clear constituency link, usually delivers strong single-party government able to implement its manifesto. Disadvantages: disproportional (seats won can differ sharply from vote share), wastes votes in safe seats, disadvantages smaller parties with spread-out support (e.g. the Greens historically) while helping regionally concentrated ones (e.g. the SNP), and can produce landslide majorities from a minority of the national vote. Additional Member System (AMS): used for the Scottish Parliament and Welsh Senedd; voters get two votes, one for a constituency MSP/MS (FPTP) and one for a regional list, with list seats allocated to correct disproportionality; more proportional than FPTP while keeping a constituency link, but creates two classes of representative. Single Transferable Vote (STV): used for Northern Ireland Assembly and local elections there; multi-member constituencies, voters rank candidates, a quota determines election, surplus/eliminated votes transfer; highly proportional and gives voters a wide choice, but constituencies are large (weaker individual MP-constituent link) and counting is complex. Closed Proportional List System (CPLS): used for Great Britain's European Parliament elections (while the UK was an EU member) and now for functions such as the London Assembly's additional/list element; voters choose a party, seats allocated by vote share from a party-ordered list; highly proportional but removes voter choice over which individual is elected and weakens the constituency link entirely.

#### ES3.2 Referendums: how they have been used in the UK since 1997 and their impact; the case for and against referendums
**Referendums since 1997.** Used more frequently in the UK since Labour's 1997-2010 constitutional reform programme: 1997 devolution referendums (Scotland and Wales); 1998 Good Friday Agreement referendum (Northern Ireland, and the Republic of Ireland); 2011 AV referendum (rejected changing Westminster's voting system); 2014 Scottish independence referendum (No, 55.3%-44.7%, on a high turnout of 84.6%); 2016 EU membership referendum (Leave, 51.9%-48.1%), which triggered years of subsequent political and constitutional upheaval. Impact: referendums have settled (at least temporarily) major constitutional questions with strong legitimacy from direct public consent, but the 2016 result exposed how a single binary referendum can produce deep, lasting political division without resolving the practical detail of implementation. Case for referendums: enhances legitimacy and participation for fundamental, irreversible constitutional change; educates and engages the public. Case against: reduces complex, multi-faceted issues to a simple binary; campaigns can be dominated by misinformation or emotive appeals; undermines representative democracy's deliberative, expert-informed decision-making; can produce a result Parliament then struggles to implement (as with Brexit).

#### ES3.3 Electoral system analysis: why different systems are used in the UK, and their impact on government formation, party representation and voter choice
**Electoral system analysis.** Why different systems are used: the choice of a more proportional system for devolved bodies (AMS in Scotland/Wales, STV in Northern Ireland) partly reflects a deliberate wish to avoid one-party dominance and to build power-sharing/consensus, especially important in divided Northern Ireland; Westminster retains FPTP partly through inertia and because it benefits the two largest parties who control any reform process (demonstrated by the 2011 AV referendum's defeat). Impact on government: FPTP tends to produce single-party majority government, giving voters a clear choice of governing party and enabling decisive policy delivery, but can also produce landslide majorities on a minority vote share (e.g. 1983, 2019, 2024) or, when the vote fragments enough, a hung parliament (2010, 2017) forcing coalition or confidence-and-supply deals. More proportional systems (AMS, STV) more often produce coalition or minority government, requiring negotiation and compromise. Impact on representation: proportional systems give smaller parties (Greens, Reform, nationalist parties) a fairer seat share relative to votes; FPTP under-represents parties with spread-out support. Impact on voter choice: FPTP can encourage tactical voting (voting to stop a disliked candidate rather than for a preferred one); STV lets voters rank preferences across parties and candidates, reducing wasted votes.
