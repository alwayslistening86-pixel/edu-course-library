# S15_US_Presidency - Lesson: Formal and informal presidential power; relationships and limitations; interpretations and debates

## Goal
The learner explains the formal and informal sources of presidential power since 2000, the presidency's relationships with Congress and the Supreme Court, limitations on presidential power, and interpretations/debates including the imperial/imperilled presidency.

## Syllabus items taught here
- PRES.1 - Formal presidential powers (Head of State/Government) since 2000
- PRES.2 - Informal presidential powers since 2000: electoral mandate, executive orders, national events, cabinet, powers of persuasion, EXOP (NSC, OMB, WHO)
- PRES.3 - Presidential relationships (Congress, Supreme Court) and limitations on presidential power (term stage, Congress/Court/Constitution, election cycle, divided government) since 2000
- PRES.4 - Interpretations and debates: effectiveness in achieving aims, imperial/imperilled presidency, accountability to Congress, foreign-policy power

## How to teach this
Ask: the Constitution gives the President remarkably few explicit powers compared with Congress - so how has the presidency become the branch most people think of as 'in charge'? Teach each area as: what the institution/process/idea is, how it works or what it claims, the debates and tensions around it, and (for the ideologies) which thinkers exemplify it. Push the learner to build a bank of specific, up-to-date examples (named Acts, cases, elections, presidents, policies) they can deploy in an essay, not just abstract description - Edexcel rewards analysis and evaluation (AO2/AO3, 65% of the marks) over bare knowledge (AO1, 35%). Current UK and US politics changes constantly (the sitting Prime Minister and Cabinet, the composition of Congress and the Supreme Court, current party leaders, ongoing court cases and legislation): this course names office-holders and events current as of when it was built, but check a reliable current-affairs source (e.g. instituteforgovernment.org.uk, parliament.uk, congress.gov, supremecourt.gov) before an exam for what has changed since. Source-based questions (Section A of Papers 1 and 2) need the learner to use the source's own detail plus their own knowledge, not just paraphrase it.

#### PRES.1 Formal presidential powers (Head of State/Government) since 2000
**Formal presidential powers.** As Head of State, the President represents the nation ceremonially and in foreign affairs (receiving ambassadors, conducting diplomacy, serving as Commander-in-Chief of the armed forces, negotiating treaties subject to Senate ratification). As Head of Government, the President leads the executive branch (appointing - with Senate confirmation - Cabinet secretaries, agency heads and federal judges/justices), proposes legislation and an annual budget to Congress, and can veto congressional legislation (Congress can override by two-thirds in both chambers). Significance since 2000: presidents from George W. Bush through to the present have used these formal powers with varying success depending on party control of Congress - a president enjoying unified government (same-party majorities in both chambers, e.g. the opening years of a new administration) can secure significant legislative wins (e.g. major tax or spending Acts), while a president facing divided government (opposition control of one or both chambers, common for much of this period) relies far more on non-legislative formal powers such as the veto and appointment power, or turns to informal powers instead.

#### PRES.2 Informal presidential powers since 2000: electoral mandate, executive orders, national events, cabinet, powers of persuasion, EXOP (NSC, OMB, WHO)
**Informal presidential powers.** Electoral mandate: a decisive election win (especially with unified government) gives a president political capital/legitimacy to push an ambitious agenda; a narrow or contested win weakens this. Executive orders: formal directives to federal agencies, used to implement, adjust or bypass congressional gridlock on policy within existing legal authority (e.g. on immigration enforcement priorities, federal agency regulation, or emergency response) - do not require Congress but can be reversed by a successor's own executive order or struck down by courts, so are often less durable than legislation. National events: crises (economic, security, public health) can dramatically boost or damage a president's authority and public standing ('rally round the flag' effects, or blame when handled poorly). The cabinet: can extend presidential reach and expertise into departments, though cabinet influence varies with a president's personal governing style. Powers of persuasion: the ability to bargain, build coalitions and use the 'bully pulpit' (public communication, media appearances, social media) to pressure Congress and shape public opinion, an idea associated with presidential scholar Richard Neustadt's view that presidential power is fundamentally the 'power to persuade'. Executive Office of the President (EXOP): includes the National Security Council (NSC, coordinating foreign/defence policy advice), the Office of Management and Budget (OMB, shaping the federal budget and reviewing agency regulations) and the White House Office (WHO, the President's closest personal staff/advisers) - together giving the President significant institutional capacity independent of the wider federal bureaucracy or Congress.

#### PRES.3 Presidential relationships (Congress, Supreme Court) and limitations on presidential power (term stage, Congress/Court/Constitution, election cycle, divided government) since 2000
**Presidential relationships and limitations since 2000.** Relationship with Congress: cooperative and productive under unified government with strong party discipline, but frequently adversarial/gridlocked under divided government, requiring negotiation, veto threats, or reliance on executive action instead. Relationship with the Supreme Court: presidents shape the Court long-term through nominations (subject to Senate confirmation), but sitting justices - once confirmed, with life tenure - can rule against the president's own administration's actions (as courts have done to presidents of both parties on issues like immigration policy, national-emergency declarations, and executive-privilege claims), showing the Court's independence even from an appointing president. Limitations on presidential power: changing nature of power over a term (a president's influence and popularity typically peak early - a 'honeymoon period' - and often decline, especially after a difficult midterm election which frequently costs the president's party seats in Congress); Congress, the Supreme Court and the Constitution as structural checks (as covered under CONG.4 and USC.2); the election cycle (a president's ability to act is shaped by proximity to the next election and, for a second-term president, 'lame duck' status once re-election is no longer possible); divided government (as above) is probably the single most significant recurring limitation on legislative achievement since 2000, with several presidents in this period facing a Congress at least partly controlled by the opposing party for much of their time in office.

#### PRES.4 Interpretations and debates: effectiveness in achieving aims, imperial/imperilled presidency, accountability to Congress, foreign-policy power
**Interpretations and debates of the US presidency.** How effectively presidents have achieved their aims: varies sharply with unified versus divided government, a president's political skill/popularity, and unforeseen events (economic crises, wars, pandemics) that can derail or reshape an agenda regardless of a president's own plans. The imperial presidency: a term (Arthur Schlesinger Jr.) describing a presidency that has accumulated excessive, unchecked power, especially in foreign policy/national security (unilateral military action without a formal congressional declaration of war, expansive use of executive orders and emergency powers, extensive claims of executive privilege) - critics argue this trend has continued or intensified into the 21st century across administrations of both parties. The imperilled presidency: by contrast, some scholars argue presidents are often weaker in practice than 'imperial' framing suggests, hemmed in by Congress (especially under divided government), the courts, the media, and a fragmented, polarised political environment that makes sustained legislative achievement difficult - both framings can apply to the same presidency in different policy areas. Presidential accountability to Congress: formally strong (oversight, confirmation, appropriations, impeachment) but practically inconsistent, heavily dependent on which party controls Congress. Role and power in foreign policy: the President dominates day-to-day foreign policy and military deployment in practice (as Commander-in-Chief and chief diplomat) far more than Congress's formal war-declaring power would suggest, a long-standing and continuing source of executive-legislative tension.
