# S11_Relations_Between_Branches - Lesson: The Supreme Court; Executive-Parliament relations; the EU's impact; the location of sovereignty

## Goal
The learner explains the Supreme Court's role and influence, the Executive-Parliament relationship, the EU's aims/role/impact on UK government, and the location of sovereignty in the UK.

## Syllabus items taught here
- GOV4.1 - The Supreme Court: role and composition, judicial neutrality and independence, and its influence via ultra vires and judicial review
- GOV4.2 - The Executive-Parliament relationship: Parliament's effectiveness in holding the Executive to account, and the Executive's attempts to dominate Parliament
- GOV4.3 - The EU's aims, role and impact on UK government: the 'four freedoms', its role in policymaking, and the impact of at least two EU policies before Brexit
- GOV4.4 - The location of sovereignty: legal vs political sovereignty, and how it has moved between branches and to/from the EU and devolved bodies

## How to teach this
Ask: if judges aren't elected, how can the Supreme Court strike down or block government actions without being 'undemocratic'? Teach each area as: what the institution/process/idea is, how it works or what it claims, the debates and tensions around it, and (for the ideologies) which thinkers exemplify it. Push the learner to build a bank of specific, up-to-date examples (named Acts, cases, elections, presidents, policies) they can deploy in an essay, not just abstract description - Edexcel rewards analysis and evaluation (AO2/AO3, 65% of the marks) over bare knowledge (AO1, 35%). Current UK and US politics changes constantly (the sitting Prime Minister and Cabinet, the composition of Congress and the Supreme Court, current party leaders, ongoing court cases and legislation): this course names office-holders and events current as of when it was built, but check a reliable current-affairs source (e.g. instituteforgovernment.org.uk, parliament.uk, congress.gov, supremecourt.gov) before an exam for what has changed since. Source-based questions (Section A of Papers 1 and 2) need the learner to use the source's own detail plus their own knowledge, not just paraphrase it.

#### GOV4.1 The Supreme Court: role and composition, judicial neutrality and independence, and its influence via ultra vires and judicial review
**The Supreme Court.** Role and composition: created by the Constitutional Reform Act 2005 and operating since 2009, replacing the Law Lords (formerly part of the House of Lords) to strengthen the separation of powers; 12 Justices, the UK's final court of appeal for civil cases (and criminal cases from England, Wales and Northern Ireland, though not Scotland's separate system for criminal matters), also hearing cases on devolution disputes. Key operating principles: judicial neutrality (judges should be politically impartial, deciding cases on legal merit not personal/political preference) and judicial independence (judges protected from political pressure/removal, appointed via an independent process, salaries protected) - both are strong in the UK by international comparison, though critics note appointment and background diversity concerns. Degree of influence: the Court cannot strike down primary legislation (unlike the US Supreme Court, given parliamentary sovereignty), but can rule executive/secondary-legislation actions unlawful via judicial review, using the doctrine of ultra vires (acting beyond legal authority) to check government - for example the 2019 Miller II/Cherry ruling that Boris Johnson's prorogation of Parliament was unlawful, a landmark assertion of judicial power to constrain executive action, though notably still an application of ordinary legal principle rather than striking down an Act of Parliament.

#### GOV4.2 The Executive-Parliament relationship: Parliament's effectiveness in holding the Executive to account, and the Executive's attempts to dominate Parliament
**The Executive-Parliament relationship.** Parliament's effectiveness in holding the Executive to account: strengthened by select committees, backbench rebellions, and (in a hung parliament or with a small majority) genuine leverage over legislation; weakened by government's usual Commons majority and party whipping discipline, and by the Salisbury Convention limiting Lords resistance to manifesto bills. The Executive's attempts to dominate Parliament: use of secondary legislation to bypass full primary-legislation scrutiny; strict party discipline (whips) and patronage (ministerial jobs) to secure backbench compliance; control of the parliamentary timetable (though backbench business time has increased since 2010 reforms); in extreme cases, controversial use of prerogative power against Parliament itself (as in the 2019 prorogation, ruled unlawful). Extent the balance has changed: many argue Parliament has become more assertive since the 2010s (more rebellions, a more independent Lords, EVEL-era and Brexit-era votes constraining government, the Fixed-term Parliaments Act 2011 briefly removing the PM's power to call snap elections at will before its 2022 repeal), while others argue a government with a solid Commons majority (e.g. after 2019 or 2024) can still dominate Parliament for most practical purposes.

#### GOV4.3 The EU's aims, role and impact on UK government: the 'four freedoms', its role in policymaking, and the impact of at least two EU policies before Brexit
**The EU's impact on UK government (while a member, 1973-2020).** Aims of the EU: the 'four freedoms' of the single market (free movement of goods, services, capital and people between member states); social policy (minimum standards, e.g. working time, employment rights); political and economic union (shared institutions, a customs union, and for eurozone members a single currency, though the UK retained the pound and opted out of areas like Schengen). Role in policymaking: EU law took precedence over conflicting UK law while a member (via the European Communities Act 1972), with EU directives/regulations directly shaping UK policy in areas like trade, competition, environment and employment; UK ministers negotiated in the EU Council, and UK MEPs sat in the European Parliament. Impact: at least two example policy areas - employment/social policy (EU-derived rights such as paid holiday entitlement, agency worker rights and the Working Time Directive became embedded in UK law) and environmental/single-market regulation (EU standards shaped UK product, environmental and competition regulation) - illustrate how deeply EU membership constrained and shaped domestic UK policy, which the 2016 Brexit referendum and 2020 withdrawal then reversed, returning full formal legal authority over these areas to Westminster (though UK-EU trade agreements still constrain some choices in practice).

#### GOV4.4 The location of sovereignty: legal vs political sovereignty, and how it has moved between branches and to/from the EU and devolved bodies
**The location of sovereignty.** Legal sovereignty: the ultimate, supreme legal authority to make or unmake any law - in UK constitutional theory this rests with Parliament (the Crown-in-Parliament), which can override any other body's decisions and cannot bind its successors. Political sovereignty: where power in practice actually lies, given political constraints - arguably with the electorate (who can remove a government), with the Prime Minister/Executive (who usually controls the Commons agenda via a majority), or diffused among the UK's other actors given devolution and (formerly) EU membership. Movement of sovereignty: devolution has moved practical decision-making power (though not ultimate legal sovereignty, since Westminster could in theory revoke devolution) to Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland; EU membership pooled/shared legal sovereignty with EU institutions in areas of EU competence, which Brexit reversed, returning that legal authority to Westminster; referendums (though legally only advisory) have in practice constrained Parliament's freedom of action (e.g. Parliament ultimately implementing the 2016 Leave result despite a Remain-majority Commons). Where sovereignty now lies: most constitutional lawyers still locate ultimate legal sovereignty at Westminster, but note it is exercised within significant political constraints from the electorate, devolved bodies, the courts (via judicial review) and international commitments (e.g. trade agreements), making 'sovereignty' as much a political as a strictly legal question today.
