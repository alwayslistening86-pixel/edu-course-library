# S08_The_Constitution - Lesson: Nature and sources of the UK constitution; change since 1997; devolution; debates on further reform

## Goal
The learner explains the nature and sources of the UK constitution, how it has changed since 1997, the role and powers of devolved bodies, and debates on further reform.

## Syllabus items taught here
- GOV1.1 - The nature and sources of the UK constitution: key historical documents, uncodified/unentrenched/unitary character, and the five sources of the constitution
- GOV1.2 - How the constitution has changed since 1997: Labour 1997-2010, the Coalition 2010-15, and reforms since 2015 including further devolution to Scotland
- GOV1.3 - The role and powers of devolved bodies: England, the Scottish Parliament/Government, the Welsh Senedd/Government, the Northern Ireland Assembly/Executive
- GOV1.4 - Debates on further constitutional reform: extending the 1997-present reforms, further devolution in England, and whether the constitution should be codified and entrenched

## How to teach this
Ask: if there's no single document called 'The UK Constitution', how do we even know what it says? Teach each area as: what the institution/process/idea is, how it works or what it claims, the debates and tensions around it, and (for the ideologies) which thinkers exemplify it. Push the learner to build a bank of specific, up-to-date examples (named Acts, cases, elections, presidents, policies) they can deploy in an essay, not just abstract description - Edexcel rewards analysis and evaluation (AO2/AO3, 65% of the marks) over bare knowledge (AO1, 35%). Current UK and US politics changes constantly (the sitting Prime Minister and Cabinet, the composition of Congress and the Supreme Court, current party leaders, ongoing court cases and legislation): this course names office-holders and events current as of when it was built, but check a reliable current-affairs source (e.g. instituteforgovernment.org.uk, parliament.uk, congress.gov, supremecourt.gov) before an exam for what has changed since. Source-based questions (Section A of Papers 1 and 2) need the learner to use the source's own detail plus their own knowledge, not just paraphrase it.

#### GOV1.1 The nature and sources of the UK constitution: key historical documents, uncodified/unentrenched/unitary character, and the five sources of the constitution
**The nature and sources of the UK constitution.** Historical development through key documents: Magna Carta (1215, limits on royal power); Bill of Rights (1689, established parliamentary sovereignty over the monarch after the Glorious Revolution); Act of Settlement (1701, regulated succession, established judicial independence); Acts of Union (1707, joined England and Scotland into Great Britain); Parliament Acts (1911 and 1949, limited the Lords' power to block Commons legislation); the European Communities Act (1972, took the UK into the EEC/EU, later repealed by the EU Withdrawal Act 2018 following Brexit). Nature of the constitution: unentrenched (can be changed by ordinary Act of Parliament, no special majority needed - contrast the USA's entrenched Constitution), uncodified (not gathered in a single document, unlike almost all other democracies), and unitary (ultimate power constitutionally rests at Westminster, even where devolved), built on the 'twin pillars' of parliamentary sovereignty (Parliament can make or unmake any law) and the rule of law (everyone, including government, is subject to and accountable under the law). Five sources: statute law (Acts of Parliament, the highest-status source); common law (judge-made law through precedent); conventions (unwritten rules followed by practice, not legally enforceable, e.g. the monarch always granting royal assent); authoritative works (academic/expert texts used to interpret uncertain areas, e.g. Erskine May on parliamentary procedure); treaties (international agreements, including historically EU law while a member).

#### GOV1.2 How the constitution has changed since 1997: Labour 1997-2010, the Coalition 2010-15, and reforms since 2015 including further devolution to Scotland
**Constitutional change since 1997.** Under Labour (1997-2010): House of Lords reform (House of Lords Act 1999 removed most hereditary peers); electoral reform (introducing AMS/STV/CPLS for devolved bodies and European elections, though a 2011 AV referendum for Westminster failed); devolution to Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland (1998 Acts, following 1997/1998 referendums); the Human Rights Act 1998 (incorporating the ECHR into UK law); creation of the Supreme Court (Constitutional Reform Act 2005, separating the UK's highest court from the House of Lords, effective 2009, to strengthen judicial independence). Under the Coalition (2010-15): Fixed-term Parliaments Act 2011 (removed the PM's power to call an election at will, later repealed in 2022 restoring that prerogative); further devolution to Wales (Wales Act 2014). Since 2015: further devolution to Scotland following the 2014 independence referendum (Scotland Act 2016, devolving further tax and welfare powers); Brexit (the 2016 referendum and subsequent EU Withdrawal Act 2018 and Trade and Cooperation Agreement, a fundamental change returning sovereignty from EU institutions to Westminster); continuing devolution debates, including English devolution to metro mayors and combined authorities.

#### GOV1.3 The role and powers of devolved bodies: England, the Scottish Parliament/Government, the Welsh Senedd/Government, the Northern Ireland Assembly/Executive
**Devolved bodies and their impact.** Devolution in England: asymmetric and partial, with directly elected metro mayors (e.g. for Greater Manchester, London) and combined authorities given some powers (transport, some economic development) but no England-wide devolved legislature, leaving the 'West Lothian Question' (English MPs cannot vote on devolved Scottish/Welsh/NI matters, but Scottish/Welsh/NI MPs historically could vote on English-only matters) contested; English Votes for English Laws (EVEL, introduced 2015) attempted a partial fix at Westminster but was abolished in 2021. Scottish Parliament and Government: elected by AMS, has extensive devolved powers (health, education, justice, and since 2016 significant tax/welfare powers) but not full independence (foreign policy, defence, and the core of the constitution remain reserved to Westminster); the SNP-led government's long-term goal of independence keeps devolution's boundaries a live issue. Welsh Senedd and Government: initially more limited (executive devolution) than Scotland, but powers have grown (Wales Act 2017 moved to a reserved-powers model matching Scotland's approach, with further primary legislative and some tax powers). Northern Ireland Assembly and Executive: power-sharing structure (mandatory coalition between unionist and nationalist parties) established by the 1998 Good Friday Agreement, has been repeatedly suspended (most recently over post-Brexit trading arrangements/the Northern Ireland Protocol) illustrating the fragility of devolved power-sharing in a divided society. Impact of devolution overall: increased policy divergence across the UK (e.g. different NHS charging, university tuition and social care policies in Scotland/Wales), raised questions about UK-wide equity and the future of the Union, and shifted political power without removing Westminster's ultimate unitary legal sovereignty.

#### GOV1.4 Debates on further constitutional reform: extending the 1997-present reforms, further devolution in England, and whether the constitution should be codified and entrenched
**Debates on further reform.** Extending 1997-present reforms: further Lords reform (a fully or partly elected chamber remains proposed but unenacted since the removal of most hereditaries); further devolution (fiscal autonomy debates in Scotland, calls for an English parliament or more powers for combined authorities). Devolution in England: advocates argue further devolution (more powers to mayors/combined authorities, or a full English parliament) would address the West Lothian imbalance and bring decision-making closer to communities; opponents worry about fragmenting England's unified administration and creating confusing, unequal levels of provision. Codification and entrenchment: advocates of a codified, entrenched constitution (possibly with an entrenched bill of rights) argue it would provide clearer, more stable protection for rights and institutions, resistant to erosion by a government with a Commons majority, and would be easier for citizens to understand; opponents argue the current flexible, evolutionary system allows adaptation without the rigidity/difficulty of formal amendment (contrast the US Constitution's rare, difficult Article V amendments), and that parliamentary sovereignty provides democratic accountability that judicial enforcement of an entrenched document could undermine (a 'democratic override' argument).
