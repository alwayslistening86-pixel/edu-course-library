# S12_Feminism - Lesson: Ideas and principles, types, and thinkers of feminism

## Goal
The learner explains feminism's core ideas and principles, the different types of feminism, and the key ideas of Gilman, de Beauvoir, Millett, Rowbotham and hooks.

## Syllabus items taught here
- FEM.1 - Feminism: ideas and principles - sex and gender, patriarchy, the personal is political, equality feminism/difference feminism, intersectionality
- FEM.2 - Types of feminism: liberal, socialist, radical, post-modern
- FEM.3 - Feminist thinkers: Gilman, de Beauvoir, Millett, Rowbotham, hooks

## How to teach this
Ask: why might a working-class Black woman and a wealthy white woman disagree about what 'feminism' should prioritise? Teach each area as: what the institution/process/idea is, how it works or what it claims, the debates and tensions around it, and (for the ideologies) which thinkers exemplify it. Push the learner to build a bank of specific, up-to-date examples (named Acts, cases, elections, presidents, policies) they can deploy in an essay, not just abstract description - Edexcel rewards analysis and evaluation (AO2/AO3, 65% of the marks) over bare knowledge (AO1, 35%). Current UK and US politics changes constantly (the sitting Prime Minister and Cabinet, the composition of Congress and the Supreme Court, current party leaders, ongoing court cases and legislation): this course names office-holders and events current as of when it was built, but check a reliable current-affairs source (e.g. instituteforgovernment.org.uk, parliament.uk, congress.gov, supremecourt.gov) before an exam for what has changed since. Source-based questions (Section A of Papers 1 and 2) need the learner to use the source's own detail plus their own knowledge, not just paraphrase it.

#### FEM.1 Feminism: ideas and principles - sex and gender, patriarchy, the personal is political, equality feminism/difference feminism, intersectionality
**Feminism: ideas and principles.** Sex and gender: sex refers to biological differences between men and women; gender refers to the social roles society ascribes to men and women - feminists argue this distinction matters because it shows much of what is assumed 'natural' about gender roles is actually socially constructed and can therefore be changed. Patriarchy: society, the state and the economy are characterised by systematic, institutionalised, pervasive gender oppression favouring men; different feminist traditions understand patriarchy's causes and depth differently (e.g. rooted in capitalism for socialist feminists, in male violence/control of sexuality for radical feminists). The personal is political: all relationships, in society and in private, between men and women are shaped by power and dominance; radical feminists especially see this as central (the family and personal relationships are sites of patriarchal oppression, not separate from 'politics'), while some liberal feminists are more cautious about extending 'political' analysis fully into private life. Equality feminism and difference feminism: equality feminists argue men and women are fundamentally alike and should be treated identically/equally; difference feminists argue men and women have genuinely different natures (e.g. more relational, caring female values) which should be valued, not erased, by aiming for sameness with men. Intersectionality: Black and working-class women's experience of patriarchy differs from that of white, middle-class women, since race and class interact with gender to shape oppression differently - a significant, newer strand reshaping wider feminist thinking.

#### FEM.2 Types of feminism: liberal, socialist, radical, post-modern
**Types of feminism.** Liberal feminism: sees individualism as the basis of gender equality; seeks equal legal/political rights and equal opportunity within existing liberal-democratic and capitalist structures (e.g. equal pay legislation, anti-discrimination law), working through reform rather than revolution. Socialist feminism: believes gender inequality stems from economics, arguing capitalism creates and relies on patriarchy (e.g. women's unpaid domestic labour supporting the capitalist workforce, or women as a low-paid 'reserve army of labour'); true gender equality requires transforming the economic system, not just legal reform. Radical feminism: believes gender inequality (patriarchy) is the most fundamental division in society, predating and underlying other oppressions (including class); calls for more fundamental social/cultural change, sometimes including challenging the traditional family and gender roles at their root, not just legal equality. Post-modern feminism: argues patriarchy manifests differently depending on a woman's race, class, sexuality etc., rejecting a single, universal account of 'women's oppression' in favour of diverse, situated experiences - closely linked to intersectionality.

#### FEM.3 Feminist thinkers: Gilman, de Beauvoir, Millett, Rowbotham, hooks
**Feminist thinkers.** Charlotte Perkins Gilman (1860-1935): argued sex and domestic economics go hand in hand - women's dependence on marriage for survival ties their sexuality/body to pleasing husbands economically; societal pressure compels young girls to conform to future domesticity/motherhood through gendered upbringing (toys, clothes marketed to them). Simone de Beauvoir (1908-1986): distinguished sex from gender - 'one is not born, but rather becomes, a woman' (gender as socially produced); 'otherness' - men are treated as the societal norm/default and women as deviant from that norm, a foundational existentialist-feminist analysis. Kate Millett (1934-2017): argued the family is patriarchy's chief institution, and undoing the traditional family structure was key to genuine sexual revolution; showed how patriarchal culture produced art and literature that degraded women (Sexual Politics). Sheila Rowbotham (1943- ): a socialist feminist - capitalism forces women to sell their labour to survive while also using their labour to support family; the family is not just a tool disciplining/subjecting women to capitalism but also a refuge for men from capitalist alienation, a nuanced view of the family's role under capitalism. bell hooks (1952-2021): brought the experiences and perspectives of women of colour into the mainstream feminist movement; argued mainstream feminism had focused mostly on white, college-educated, middle/upper-class women's concerns, neglecting the different, intersecting oppressions facing women of colour - a foundational intersectional-feminist voice.

## Explicitly not here
Anarchism, ecologism, multiculturalism and nationalism are not covered in this course (Feminism is the non-core idea taught).
