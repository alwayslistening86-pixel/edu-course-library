# FLK1_Business_Law_and_Practice - Lesson: Business Law and Practice

## Goal
The learner identifies the correct business vehicle, spots governance and authority issues, recognises insolvency triggers and procedures, and issue-spots the tax consequences of a business transaction, at day-one-competence standard, and answers SQE1-style single-best-answer questions on all of it.

## How to teach this
Open with a scenario: three friends have run an unincorporated design business together for two years with no paperwork. A big client will only sign if the business is "a proper company"; one friend wants to bring in an aunt as an investor; another worries about personal liability; a third wants to know who can sign contracts without asking the others. Have the learner work out, before any teaching, which of BLP.1-BLP.10 each worry actually raises - that diagnostic habit is the single most useful day-one skill this subject tests, and it is exactly how SQE1 SBA questions are constructed: a fact pattern, then five plausible-looking legal conclusions of which only one is correct.

#### BLP.1 Business vehicles, legal personality and limited liability
Four common structures: sole trader and partnership (no separate legal personality, unlimited personal liability for the owner(s)); LLP and company (separate legal personality, liability limited to capital contributed or the amount unpaid on shares). Salomon v A Salomon & Co Ltd [1897] establishes that a validly incorporated company is a distinct legal person from its shareholders - the doctrinal reason incorporating solves a personal-liability worry. Two or more people trading together with a view to profit are automatically a partnership under s.1 Partnership Act 1890, whether or not they intended that label, with each partner unlimited and jointly/severally liable. Teach the learner to classify a fact pattern into one of the four structures before analysing anything else.

#### BLP.2 Company formation: constitutional documents and Companies House requirements
Incorporating a company requires a memorandum of association (a statement of intent to form the company), articles of association (the company's internal rulebook - the default Model Articles apply unless amended), and Companies House form IN01 (registered office, proposed officers, statement of capital, People with Significant Control information), plus the registration fee. On registration Companies House issues a certificate of incorporation, and the company exists as a separate legal person from that moment (s.16 Companies Act 2006). Teach the learner that "a proper company" in a client's mouth usually means exactly this: separate legal personality from the moment of incorporation, not merely a trading name.

#### BLP.3 Financing a business: debt and equity finance, types of security
Equity finance means issuing shares for investment; existing shareholders often hold pre-emption rights (s.561 Companies Act 2006), so new shares must generally be offered to them first unless disapplied. Debt finance (loans, overdrafts, bonds) makes the lender a creditor, not an owner - no voting rights, but repaid ahead of shareholders on insolvency. Security for debt is either a fixed charge (over a specific, identifiable asset; the company cannot deal with it without the lender's consent) or a floating charge (over a changing class of assets; the company can deal with them in the ordinary course of business until the charge crystallises, e.g. on default or insolvency). Charges must be registered at Companies House within 21 days (s.859A CA 2006) or are void against a liquidator, administrator or creditor.

#### BLP.4 Corporate governance: directors' and shareholders' rights, decision-making, minority protection
Directors run the company day to day and owe statutory duties under ss.171-177 CA 2006: act within powers, promote the success of the company, exercise independent judgment, exercise reasonable care/skill/diligence, avoid conflicts of interest, not accept benefits from third parties, and declare interest in a proposed transaction. Shareholders exercise control mainly through voting: an ordinary resolution (simple majority, over 50%) covers routine matters like appointing a director; a special resolution (75%) covers major changes like amending the articles, changing the company name, or disapplying pre-emption rights. Private companies may use written resolutions instead of a physical meeting. Minority shareholders have statutory protection (e.g. unfair prejudice petitions) against majority abuse of these powers.

#### BLP.5 Partnership decision-making and authority
Absent a partnership agreement, the Partnership Act 1890 supplies default rules: profits and losses are shared equally (s.24(1)), ordinary decisions are by majority, but changing the nature of the business requires unanimity (s.24(8)). Any partner can bind the firm in the ordinary course of business (s.5) - apparent authority protects a third party dealing in good faith, even if that partner in fact lacked authority, unless the third party knew of the restriction. Teach the learner that a written partnership agreement matters precisely because these default rules may not match what the partners actually intended, and s.5 is what usually answers "who can sign" questions.

#### BLP.6 Insolvency procedures: CVA, IVA, bankruptcy, administration, liquidation
Individuals use bankruptcy (a trustee in bankruptcy takes control of the individual's assets) or an individual voluntary arrangement (a formal agreement with creditors to repay some debt over time, avoiding bankruptcy). Companies use a company voluntary arrangement (a binding agreement with creditors, the company continues trading), administration (an administrator takes control to rescue the company or achieve a better result than liquidation, with a moratorium preventing creditor action), or liquidation/winding up (the company's life ends; assets are sold and distributed to creditors in strict statutory order, then the company is dissolved). Teach the learner to match the procedure to whether the client is an individual or a company, and whether rescue or an orderly end is the goal.

#### BLP.7 Insolvency claw-back and priority of distribution to creditors
An insolvency practitioner can claw back assets for creditors by challenging preferences (a creditor put in a better position than others shortly before insolvency), transactions at an undervalue, fraudulent trading, wrongful trading (a director who knew or should have known insolvent liquidation was unavoidable and failed to minimise creditor loss), or by setting aside a floating charge created shortly before insolvency for no new value. On liquidation, the statutory order of priority is (simplified): fixed charge holders, then the insolvency practitioner's costs, then preferential creditors (e.g. certain employee claims), then floating charge holders, then unsecured creditors, and shareholders last, only if anything remains.

#### BLP.8 Income Tax and Capital Gains Tax for business
Income Tax is paid by sole traders and partners on business profits via self-assessment, and by employees/directors on salary via PAYE; the charge depends on the chargeable person and the type of income, with main reliefs, exemptions and anti-avoidance provisions to be issue-spotted rather than calculated in full. Capital Gains Tax is paid by individuals (sole traders, partners, shareholders) - not companies - on gains from disposing of a chargeable asset (e.g. selling shares or business premises), again with allowable deductions and reliefs relevant to spot. Teach the learner the day-one standard here: identify which tax applies to which taxpayer and transaction, not produce a full calculation.

#### BLP.9 Corporation Tax and tax treatment of distributions
Companies pay Corporation Tax on their income profits and chargeable gains together (they do not pay Income Tax or Capital Gains Tax on trading profits or asset disposals) - calculated with allowable deductions, main reliefs and exemptions, and subject to anti-avoidance legislation. Distributions to shareholders (dividends) have their own tax treatment, taxed on the shareholder as income rather than being deductible for the company. Teach the learner the recurring SBA distinction: an individual sole trader/partner pays Income Tax and CGT; a company pays Corporation Tax on the equivalent profits and gains - mixing these up is a classic distractor.

#### BLP.10 VAT and Inheritance Tax business property relief
VAT is charged on most goods and services once a business's taxable turnover exceeds the registration threshold: registered businesses charge output tax on sales and can usually reclaim input tax on their own purchases, with returns, payment and record-keeping obligations, and correct issue of VAT invoices. Inheritance Tax Business Property Relief can reduce the IHT value of qualifying business assets (e.g. shares in a trading company) by up to 100%, relevant whenever a business owner dies or makes a lifetime gift of business assets - a day-one spotting point connecting this subject directly to Wills and the Administration of Estates (WILLS.7).

## Explicitly not here
Detailed tax calculation and advanced tax planning are outside day-one competence; the standard here is issue-spotting which tax applies, not computing a liability.
