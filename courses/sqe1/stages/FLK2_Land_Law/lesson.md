# FLK2_Land_Law - Lesson: Land Law

## Goal
The learner correctly classifies land interests as legal or equitable, applies registration/notice rules to decide whether they bind a purchaser, and analyses co-ownership, easements, covenants, mortgages and leases, at day-one-competence standard, and answers SQE1-style single-best-answer questions on all of it.

## Syllabus items taught here
- LAND.1 - Registered and unregistered title, interests overriding registration: learn and apply: registered and unregistered title
- LAND.2 - Co-ownership: joint tenancy, tenancy in common, severance, TOLATA disputes: learn and apply: co-ownership
- LAND.3 - Easements: creation and passing of benefit and burden: learn and apply: easements
- LAND.4 - Freehold covenants: learn and apply: freehold covenants
- LAND.5 - Mortgages: enforceability, priority, powers and duties: learn and apply: mortgages
- LAND.6 - Leases: essential characteristics, lease/licence distinction: learn and apply: lease/licence distinction
- LAND.7 - Leasehold covenants: privity, enforceability, alienation, remedies: learn and apply: leasehold covenants

## How to teach this
Open with a scenario: two friends buy a house together with unequal contributions, one grants the neighbour a right of way across the garden, and the house is mortgaged - now one friend wants to sell up. Ask the learner to identify, before teaching, every distinct proprietary interest in play (the co-ownership, the easement, the mortgage) and whether each would bind a buyer - land law is tested by asking exactly this: given this interest, does it bind a purchaser, and why.

#### LAND.1 Registered and unregistered title, interests overriding registration
Registered title (the majority of land) is proved by the Land Registry's register (proprietorship, property, charges); most third-party interests must be protected by an entry (a notice, for interests such as restrictive covenants and easements, or a restriction, for interests such as a beneficiary's equitable interest under a trust of land) to bind a purchaser, but some interests override registration without any entry (e.g. a legal easement, or the interest of a person in actual occupation under Sch.3 para.2 Land Registration Act 2002, subject to exceptions). Unregistered title relies on deeds and the doctrine of notice for equitable interests not registrable as land charges, and on the Land Charges Register for registrable land charges. Teach the learner to identify the title system first, then ask what protects the interest in question.

#### LAND.2 Co-ownership: joint tenancy, tenancy in common, severance, TOLATA disputes
Joint tenants hold as a single notional owner with the right of survivorship (on death, the share passes automatically to the surviving joint tenant(s), not by will); tenants in common hold distinct, quantifiable shares which pass under a will or intestacy. The legal estate can only ever be held as a joint tenancy (max four legal owners); the equitable interest can be joint tenancy or tenancy in common. A joint tenancy in equity can be severed (converted to a tenancy in common) by written notice, an act operating on one's own share (e.g. selling it), mutual agreement, or a course of dealing. Disputes over sale, occupation or the size of shares are resolved under ss.14-15 Trusts of Land and Appointment of Trustees Act 1996, which lists factors the court considers (the purpose of the trust, the interests of any minor occupying, secured creditors). Teach the learner to check severance before assuming survivorship applies.

#### LAND.3 Easements: creation and passing of benefit and burden
An easement (e.g. a right of way, right of light) must satisfy the Re Ellenborough Park characteristics: a dominant and servient tenement, the right must accommodate the dominant land, the tenements must be owned/occupied by different people, and the right must be capable of forming the subject matter of a grant. Easements are created expressly (by deed), impliedly (necessity, common intention, or the rule in Wheeldon v Burrows), by prescription (long use, at least 20 years, as of right), or under s.62 Law of Property Act 1925 on a conveyance. The benefit passes automatically with the dominant land; whether the burden binds a purchaser of the servient land depends on the title system and whether it is protected (registered: notice, unless overriding; unregistered: legal easements bind everyone, equitable ones need registration as a land charge or notice under the doctrine). Teach the learner to check the Re Ellenborough Park criteria before analysing how the easement was created.

#### LAND.4 Freehold covenants
A covenant is a promise in a deed; the original covenantor remains liable in contract regardless of who later owns the land. Whether the burden of a restrictive covenant passes to a successor in title depends on whether it "touches and concerns" the land, was intended to bind successors, and (unregistered land) is registered as a Class D(ii) land charge, or (registered land) protected by notice - positive covenants (requiring action, e.g. to repair a fence) do not run with the burden at law and typically need indirect enforcement mechanisms (e.g. a chain of indemnity covenants, or the benefit/burden principle). The benefit of a covenant can pass by express assignment, annexation, or a building scheme. Teach the learner to check restrictive versus positive before deciding whether the burden can bind a successor at all.

#### LAND.5 Mortgages: enforceability, priority, powers and duties
A legal mortgage over registered land is created by a charge by deed expressed as a legal mortgage, registered against the title; priority between competing mortgages is normally determined by date of registration (registered land) or date of creation for legal mortgages / registration as a land charge for equitable ones (unregistered land). Once the legal date for redemption has passed and default occurs, a mortgagee's remedies include possession, sale (power of sale under s.101 Law of Property Act 1925, arising once the mortgage money is due and exercisable once one of the statutory conditions is met), appointing a receiver, and foreclosure (rare in practice); a mortgagee exercising a power of sale owes a duty to obtain the best price reasonably obtainable. Teach the learner to check whether the power of sale has actually arisen and become exercisable before assuming the lender can sell.

#### LAND.6 Leases: essential characteristics, lease/licence distinction
A lease requires exclusive possession, for a certain term (a fixed maximum duration, ascertainable at the outset, or periodic), typically (though not essentially) at a rent; a licence is merely permission to be on land, creating no proprietary interest and not binding third parties. Street v Mountford established that if the essential characteristics (exclusive possession, certain term, usually rent) are present, the arrangement is a lease regardless of the label the parties give it, unless there is no intention to create legal relations or a genuine service occupancy/lodger arrangement negatives exclusive possession. Teach the learner: label is not decisive, substance is - look for exclusive possession first.

#### LAND.7 Leasehold covenants: privity, enforceability, alienation, remedies
Under old leases (pre-1996), privity of contract binds the original landlord and tenant to each other for the whole term even after assignment (subject to authorised guarantee agreements); privity of estate binds whoever currently holds the reversion and the lease to each other, so covenants that "touch and concern" the land are enforceable between current landlord and current tenant. For new leases (post-1995, under the Landlord and Tenant (Covenants) Act 1995), the original tenant is normally released from liability on a lawful assignment. Alienation covenants (assignment, underletting, charging) may be absolute (cannot be done) or qualified (needs consent, not to be unreasonably withheld under s.19(1)(a) LTA 1927 where applicable). Remedies for breach include forfeiture (subject to relief and, for rent arrears, a formal demand or waiver rules), damages, and (for repair) specific performance in limited cases. Teach the learner to identify old versus new lease first, since that changes who remains liable after assignment.

## Explicitly not here
Detailed conveyancing mechanics for registering these interests (covered procedurally in Property Law and Practice) and full drafting of covenants are outside this stage; the focus here is the substantive land law rules on creation, classification and enforceability.
