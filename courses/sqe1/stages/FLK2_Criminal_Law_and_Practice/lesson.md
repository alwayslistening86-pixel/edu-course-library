# FLK2_Criminal_Law_and_Practice - Lesson: Criminal Law and Practice

## Goal
The learner advises a suspect at the police station, manages a case through bail, first hearings, case management and trial, applies the rules of criminal evidence, and advises on sentencing and appeals (including youth court process), at day-one-competence standard, and answers SQE1-style single-best-answer questions on all of it.

## How to teach this
Open with a scenario: a 17-year-old is arrested and taken to the police station for an alleged assault, denies it, and there is a disputed identification by a witness who only glimpsed the incident briefly at night. Walk the learner through the entire criminal process this triggers - police station rights, charge decision, bail, first hearing, evidence rules for the identification and any later confession, trial, sentencing (given the client's age), and any appeal - so each item lands inside one continuous case rather than as an isolated rule.

#### CRIMPRAC.1 Police station rights and PACE Code C/D procedures
A suspect at the police station has the right to free and independent legal advice (which can be waived, but only after being clearly informed of it), the right to have someone informed of their arrest, and protection from detention beyond the statutory limits (24 hours for most offences without authorisation to extend, up to 36 or 96 hours for indictable offences with the right authorisation, subject to periodic review). Code D governs identification procedures (video identification is normally the default where identity is disputed and the suspect is known). A vulnerable suspect (e.g. a juvenile, or someone with a mental health condition) must have an appropriate adult present throughout, including during interview. Teach the learner to check custody time limits and the right to legal advice as the first two things in any police station scenario.

#### CRIMPRAC.2 Bail
Police bail and court bail both start from a presumption in favour of bail (Bail Act 1976), which can be rebutted where there are substantial grounds to believe the defendant would fail to surrender, commit further offences, or interfere with witnesses/obstruct justice - factors include the nature and seriousness of the offence, the defendant's character and community ties, and their record of answering bail before. Conditions (e.g. residence, curfew, reporting, surety) can be attached instead of outright refusal where they would address the specific risk identified. A refusal of bail can be appealed (to a Crown Court judge, or ultimately the High Court). Teach the learner to identify which specific statutory exception is engaged before concluding bail should be refused, not just that the offence is serious.

#### CRIMPRAC.3 First hearings, plea before venue and allocation
At the first hearing in the magistrates' court, an either-way offence goes through plea before venue: if the defendant indicates a guilty plea, the magistrates proceed to sentence (or commit to the Crown Court for sentence if their powers are insufficient); if not guilty is indicated, the court decides allocation (magistrates' or Crown Court), considering the offence's seriousness and their sentencing powers, but the defendant has the right to elect Crown Court trial for an either-way offence even if the magistrates would accept jurisdiction. Summary-only offences stay in the magistrates' court; indictable-only offences are sent straight to the Crown Court. Teach the learner to classify the offence type first, since that alone determines which of these routes applies.

#### CRIMPRAC.4 Case management and disclosure
The Criminal Procedure Rules require active case management by the court (identifying real issues, fixing a timetable, encouraging early guilty pleas where appropriate) from the first hearing onward. The prosecution must give initial disclosure of unused material that might reasonably undermine the prosecution case or assist the defence (Criminal Procedure and Investigations Act 1996), triggering a duty on the defence to serve a defence statement (compulsory in the Crown Court, and it can trigger further prosecution disclosure), with unjustified failures capable of leading to adverse comment or inferences. Teach the learner: disclosure is a continuing, staged duty, not a single one-off event.

#### CRIMPRAC.5 Identification evidence and inferences from silence
Where identification evidence is disputed and is the sole or main basis of the case, the Turnbull guidelines require the court to warn the jury/itself of the special need for caution and to examine the quality of the identification (duration of observation, distance, lighting, any prior knowledge of the suspect); weak, unsupported identification evidence may lead to the case being withdrawn from the jury. Under ss.34-37 Criminal Justice and Public Order Act 1994, an adverse inference can be drawn from a suspect's failure to mention facts when questioned (if later relied on at trial), failure to account for objects/marks/substances, or failure to account for presence at a scene, but only where a proper caution was given and legal advice was available. Teach the learner to check the quality-of-evidence factors for identification, and the specific statutory conditions before any inference from silence is drawn.

#### CRIMPRAC.6 Hearsay and confession evidence
Hearsay (an out-of-court statement relied on for the truth of its contents) is generally inadmissible unless it falls within a statutory gateway (Criminal Justice Act 2003) - e.g. a witness is unavailable for a qualifying reason, it is business/other document evidence, all parties agree, or admission is in the interests of justice. A confession is admissible unless obtained by oppression, or in circumstances likely to render it unreliable (s.76 PACE 1984), in which case the prosecution must prove beyond reasonable doubt it was not so obtained, or the court may exclude it; it can also be excluded under s.78 if admitting it would have such an adverse effect on fairness that it ought to be excluded. Teach the learner to check the gateway for hearsay, and oppression/unreliability specifically for a disputed confession, before assuming either is automatically in or out.

#### CRIMPRAC.7 Bad character evidence and exclusion under s.78 PACE
Bad character of a non-defendant is admissible only through one of three gateways (s.100 CJA 2003): important explanatory evidence, substantial probative value on a matter in issue of substantial importance, or all parties agree. Bad character of a defendant is admissible only through one of seven gateways (s.101 CJA 2003) - commonly: an agreement between the parties, evidence the defendant has adduced themselves, evidence relevant to an important matter in issue between defence and prosecution (including propensity), correcting a false impression given by the defendant, or an attack on another person's character by the defendant. Any prosecution evidence, including bad character, can additionally be excluded under s.78 PACE if its admission would have such an adverse effect on the fairness of proceedings that it ought not to be admitted. Teach the learner to identify the specific gateway relied on, not just assert "it's relevant".

#### CRIMPRAC.8 Trial procedure (magistrates' and Crown Court)
The prosecution bears the burden of proving guilt beyond reasonable doubt (or "so that the jury/magistrates are sure"), save for limited reverse-burden exceptions; the trial follows a set sequence - prosecution opening (Crown Court), prosecution evidence and cross-examination, a submission of no case to answer if appropriate, defence evidence, closing speeches, summing up (Crown Court) or the magistrates retiring, then verdict. A defendant's good character (no previous convictions and, ideally, positive good character evidence) entitles them to directions on both credibility and propensity. Welsh-language cases in Wales may proceed bilingually with interpretation, a point to flag procedurally rather than substantively. Teach the learner the fixed sequence, since procedural questions are commonly tested by asking "what happens next".

#### CRIMPRAC.9 Sentencing
Sentencing follows the relevant sentencing guideline: the court assesses the offence's seriousness by culpability and harm to place it within a starting point and range, then adjusts for aggravating and mitigating factors, a guilty plea reduction (up to one third, tapering the earlier the plea), and totality where sentencing for multiple offences (concurrent sentences run together; consecutive sentences run one after another, for unrelated or particularly serious combined offending). Sentence types range from discharges and fines through community orders to suspended and immediate custodial sentences, the last reserved for offences so serious that only custody is justified. A Newton hearing resolves a factual dispute affecting sentence where the defendant pleads guilty but disputes the prosecution's version of events. Teach the learner the guideline's two-stage structure (seriousness, then adjustment) before naming a likely sentence.

#### CRIMPRAC.10 Appeals
From the magistrates' court, a defendant can appeal to the Crown Court against conviction and/or sentence (a full rehearing), or appeal by way of case stated to the High Court on a point of law. From the Crown Court, a defendant appeals to the Court of Appeal (Criminal Division) against conviction (requiring leave, generally where the conviction is unsafe) or sentence (where it was wrong in principle or manifestly excessive), with the Court of Appeal able to quash a conviction, order a retrial, or vary a sentence (including, exceptionally, increasing it via an Attorney General reference for unduly lenient sentences). Teach the learner to identify which court the case started in before naming the correct appeal route.

#### CRIMPRAC.11 Youth court process
Defendants aged 10-17 are normally dealt with in the youth court, which has different procedures (less formal, no public gallery as of right, reporting restrictions) and its own sentencing framework (e.g. referral orders for a first guilty plea to an imprisonable offence, youth rehabilitation orders, detention and training orders) geared to welfare and rehabilitation rather than adult sentencing ranges. Grave crimes (offences that could attract a long sentence for an adult) and cases involving a young person jointly charged with an adult can be sent to the Crown Court instead. Teach the learner to check age at the date of the offence first, then whether a grave-crime or joint-charge exception sends the case out of the youth court.

## Explicitly not here
Detailed procedural forms and court-specific administrative paperwork are outside day-one competence; the standard here is applying the correct rule and sequence to a fact pattern, not completing a specific court form.
