# FLK2_Criminal_Liability - Lesson: Criminal Liability

## Goal
The learner proves the actus reus and mens rea of the specified offences, applies general and partial defences and rules on secondary participation and attempt, at day-one-competence standard, and answers SQE1-style single-best-answer questions on all of it.

## How to teach this
Open with a scenario: during an argument, one person pushes another who falls, hits their head, and later dies in hospital; the pusher had been drinking heavily and claims they only meant to make the other person "back off". Ask the learner to work through, before teaching, every offence potentially committed and every defence potentially available - that is the exam's method: give the facts, then test which offence is actually made out and whether any defence displaces it.

#### CRIMLIAB.1 Actus reus and mens rea
Every offence requires an actus reus (the guilty act - conduct, a result, or an omission where a duty to act exists, e.g. a contractual duty, a duty from a dangerous situation the defendant created, or a special relationship) coinciding in time with the required mens rea (the guilty mind), unless a continuing-act or single-transaction analysis bridges a gap between them (Fagan v MPC; Thabo Meli). Mens rea states include intention (direct - it was the defendant's purpose - or oblique, where the consequence was virtually certain and the defendant appreciated that) and recklessness (the defendant foresaw an unjustified risk and took it anyway - Cunningham/subjective recklessness, the test used for the specified offences). Teach the learner to prove actus reus and mens rea separately, element by element, before reaching for a defence.

#### CRIMLIAB.2 General defences: intoxication, self-defence
Voluntary intoxication is no defence to a basic intent offence (one that can be committed recklessly, e.g. s.20/47 OAPA) but can negate the mens rea of a specific intent offence (one requiring intention only, e.g. s.18 OAPA, murder) if it prevented the defendant forming that intention; involuntary intoxication is a defence if it negated mens rea. Self-defence (including defence of another or property, and prevention of crime) requires the defendant to have honestly believed force was necessary (even if mistaken, provided the mistake was genuine - though a mistake induced by voluntary intoxication does not assist) and the force used to have been reasonable in the circumstances as the defendant believed them to be. Teach the learner to check basic versus specific intent before applying intoxication, and honesty-of-belief before reasonableness for self-defence.

#### CRIMLIAB.3 Partial defences: loss of control, diminished responsibility
These partial defences reduce murder to voluntary manslaughter only - they are not available to other offences. Loss of control (Coroners and Justice Act 2009, s.54) requires a loss of self-control triggered by a qualifying trigger (fear of serious violence, or things said/done amounting to circumstances of an extremely grave character causing the defendant a justifiable sense of being seriously wronged - excluding sexual infidelity alone), where a person of the defendant's age and sex, with a normal degree of tolerance and self-restraint, might have reacted similarly. Diminished responsibility (Homicide Act 1957 s.2, as amended) requires an abnormality of mental functioning from a recognised medical condition, substantially impairing the defendant's ability to understand their conduct, form rational judgment, or exercise self-control, and providing an explanation for the killing. Teach the learner: these only ever reduce murder to manslaughter, they never produce a full acquittal.

#### CRIMLIAB.4 Secondary liability and attempt
A secondary party (accessory) who assists or encourages the principal offender, intending to assist/encourage and with knowledge of the essential matters constituting the principal's offence, is liable to the same extent as the principal. Criminal Attempts Act 1981 s.1 makes it an offence to do an act that is more than merely preparatory to the commission of an offence, with intention as to every element of the full offence (even where the full offence could be committed recklessly as to some element). Teach the learner to identify the principal offence first, then ask separately whether the defendant is liable as principal, secondary party, or only for an attempt.

#### CRIMLIAB.5 Offences against the person: assault/battery, s.47/20/18 OAPA 1861
Common assault is causing the victim to apprehend immediate unlawful force; battery is the application of unlawful force. s.47 OAPA (assault occasioning actual bodily harm) requires an assault or battery causing ABH (more than trifling harm), with the mens rea only of the underlying assault/battery, not of causing the harm itself. s.20 OAPA (malicious wounding/inflicting GBH) requires a wound (breaking both layers of skin) or really serious harm, with intention or subjective recklessness as to some harm (not necessarily serious harm). s.18 OAPA (wounding/causing GBH with intent) requires the same injury but intention to cause really serious harm (or to resist arrest). Teach the learner this is a ladder of increasing seriousness in both harm and mens rea - check both before naming the offence.

#### CRIMLIAB.6 Theft, robbery and burglary (Theft Act 1968)
Theft (s.1) is the dishonest appropriation of property belonging to another with intention to permanently deprive - dishonesty is assessed objectively against what an ordinary person would consider honest, given the defendant's actual knowledge and belief (Ivey). Robbery (s.8) is theft using or threatening force immediately before or at the time of the theft, in order to steal. Burglary (s.9) is entering a building (or part of one) as a trespasser with intent to steal, cause GBH, or cause criminal damage (s.9(1)(a)), or having entered as a trespasser, going on to steal or attempt to, or inflict/attempt GBH (s.9(1)(b)). Teach the learner to check each element of theft first, since robbery and both forms of burglary all build on it.

#### CRIMLIAB.7 Criminal damage and arson
Simple criminal damage (Criminal Damage Act 1971 s.1(1)) is destroying or damaging property belonging to another, without lawful excuse, intending to or being reckless as to the damage. Aggravated criminal damage (s.1(2)) additionally requires intention or recklessness as to endangering life by the damage (the danger need not actually materialise). Arson (s.1(3)) is criminal damage by fire, and always carries a higher maximum sentence; arson can also be aggravated if life was intended or recklessly endangered. Lawful excuse includes an honest (even if mistaken) belief the owner would have consented, or damage done to protect property honestly believed to be in immediate need of protection by reasonable means. Teach the learner to check simple versus aggravated (life endangered) and by fire versus not, as three separate binary questions.

#### CRIMLIAB.8 Homicide: murder and manslaughter
Murder is the unlawful killing of a human being under the King's/Queen's Peace with malice aforethought - intention to kill or to cause grievous bodily harm (GBH is enough; death need not be intended). Voluntary manslaughter is murder reduced by a successful partial defence (loss of control or diminished responsibility). Involuntary manslaughter covers an unlawful killing without intent to kill or cause GBH: unlawful act (constructive) manslaughter (an unlawful and objectively dangerous act causing death) or gross negligence manslaughter (a duty of care, breach, a risk of death that was reasonably foreseeable, and a breach so bad as to be characterised as criminal). Teach the learner to establish the unlawful killing first, then work out which category and, if murder, whether a partial defence applies.

#### CRIMLIAB.9 Fraud offences
Fraud Act 2006 creates a single offence of fraud committable in three ways: by false representation (s.2 - dishonestly making a false representation, knowing it is or might be untrue/misleading, intending to make a gain or cause/expose another to loss - no actual gain or loss need occur); by failing to disclose information (s.3 - dishonestly failing to disclose information one is under a legal duty to disclose, with the same intent); and by abuse of position (s.4 - dishonestly abusing a position in which one is expected to safeguard another's financial interests, with the same intent). Dishonesty uses the same objective Ivey test as theft. Teach the learner to identify which of the three routes the facts fit before analysing the elements of that route specifically.
