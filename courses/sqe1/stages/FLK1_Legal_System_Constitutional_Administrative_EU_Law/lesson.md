# FLK1_Legal_System_Constitutional_Administrative_EU_Law - Lesson: Legal System of England and Wales, Constitutional and Administrative Law, and EU Law

## Goal
The learner explains the court hierarchy, precedent and statutory interpretation, the UK's constitutional framework, and the grounds and remedies of judicial review, and applies the Human Rights Act at day-one-competence standard.

## Syllabus items taught here
- LS.1 - Courts: judicial function, hierarchy, jurisdiction, rights of audience: learn and apply: courts and their hierarchy
- LS.2 - The doctrine of precedent: learn and apply: the doctrine of precedent
- LS.3 - Primary legislation structure and statutory interpretation: learn and apply: statutory interpretation
- LS.4 - Core constitutional institutions: parliamentary sovereignty, devolution, the Crown, conventions, prerogative: learn and apply: core constitutional institutions
- LS.5 - Separation of powers, rule of law and public order law: learn and apply: separation of powers and public order law
- LS.6 - Judicial review: grounds, process, remedies, standing, time limits: learn and apply: judicial review
- LS.7 - Human Rights Act 1998 and ECHR Convention rights: learn and apply: human rights act and echr
- LS.8 - The place of EU law in the UK constitution: learn and apply: eu law in the uk constitution

## How to teach this
Open with: a public body refuses a licence application without giving reasons or allowing the applicant to respond, relying on a power granted by a statute whose wording is genuinely ambiguous. Have the learner identify every distinct legal question buried in that one sentence - which court would hear a challenge, how would the statute be interpreted, what ground of judicial review is engaged, and whether a Convention right is also in play - before teaching each item.

#### LS.1 Courts: judicial function, hierarchy, jurisdiction, rights of audience
The court hierarchy runs (for civil and criminal matters respectively) from magistrates'/county court through the Crown Court/High Court to the Court of Appeal and the Supreme Court, each with its own jurisdiction and judicial function (trial of fact, appellate review of law, or both). Rights of audience (who may exercise before which court) differ between solicitors, solicitor-advocates and barristers depending on the court and type of hearing. Teach the learner to place a given dispute correctly in the hierarchy before analysing precedent, since which court decided a prior case determines whether it binds the court now deciding the new one.

#### LS.2 The doctrine of precedent
A court is bound by the ratio decidendi (the reasoning necessary to the decision, as opposed to obiter dicta, remarks not necessary to it) of a court above it in the hierarchy, and generally by its own past decisions, subject to recognised exceptions (e.g. the Court of Appeal's own limited exceptions in Young v Bristol Aeroplane Co [1944], and the Supreme Court's freedom to depart from its own past decisions under the 1966 Practice Statement). Teach the learner to distinguish ratio from obiter in a short extract, and to identify whether a cited case is genuinely binding, merely persuasive, or distinguishable on its facts.

#### LS.3 Primary legislation structure and statutory interpretation
An Act of Parliament (and, separately, an Act of Senedd Cymru) has a standard structure - long title, sections, subsections, schedules - that a solicitor must be able to navigate. Where a statute's wording is unclear, courts apply interpretive approaches: the literal rule (ordinary meaning of the words, even if the result seems harsh), the golden rule (departing from the literal meaning only to avoid an absurd result), the mischief rule (looking to the defect in the prior law the statute was meant to remedy), and the modern purposive approach (giving effect to Parliament's underlying purpose). Presumptions (e.g. against retrospective effect, against ousting the courts' jurisdiction) and aids to construction (e.g. Hansard, under Pepper v Hart [1993], in limited circumstances) supplement these approaches. Legislation made by Senedd Cymru and by Westminster applies differently to England and to Wales depending on the devolved competence engaged.

#### LS.4 Core constitutional institutions: parliamentary sovereignty, devolution, the Crown, conventions, prerogative
Parliamentary sovereignty (Parliament can make or unmake any law, and no Parliament can bind its successor) is the traditional cornerstone of the UK constitution, qualified in practice by devolution (the devolved institutions in Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland exercise powers granted by Westminster, which retains ultimate legal authority but by convention does not normally legislate on devolved matters without consent) and by central government's accountability to Parliament. The Crown/monarch retains formal constitutional functions exercised in practice on ministerial advice; constitutional conventions (unwritten but binding-in-practice rules) and prerogative powers (residual executive powers not requiring statutory authority) both operate alongside, and are constrained by, statute and convention. Teach the learner that prerogative power can be displaced or limited by legislation covering the same ground.

#### LS.5 Separation of powers, rule of law and public order law
The separation of powers divides legislative (Parliament), executive (government) and judicial (courts) functions, with UK practice showing significant overlap (e.g. ministers sit in Parliament) tempered by conventions and judicial independence safeguards. The rule of law requires that state power be exercised according to publicly known, prospective law, applied equally, with access to independent courts. Public order law regulates processions (advance notice requirements, conditions that may be imposed) and assemblies (conditions imposable to prevent serious public disorder, damage or disruption), and breach of the peace is a common law power allowing intervention (including arrest) to prevent an imminent breach, distinct from a specific statutory public order offence.

#### LS.6 Judicial review: grounds, process, remedies, standing, time limits
Judicial review is the supervisory (not appellate) review by the courts of the lawfulness of a public body's decision-making process, available only where a claimant has sufficient standing (a sufficient interest in the matter), within strict time limits (promptly, and in any event normally within three months), against decisions that are amenable to review (broadly, decisions exercising a public function). The recognised grounds are illegality (acting outside the power granted, or misapplying/misunderstanding it), irrationality (a decision so unreasonable no reasonable decision-maker could have made it - Wednesbury unreasonableness), procedural impropriety (failing to follow a fair procedure, e.g. a right to be heard, or a decision-maker's own published procedure), and legitimate expectation (a public body departing, without good reason and without a fair process, from a clear promise or established practice on which the claimant reasonably relied). Remedies are discretionary: quashing order, prohibiting order, mandatory order, declaration, injunction, and/or damages in limited circumstances.

#### LS.7 Human Rights Act 1998 and ECHR Convention rights
The Human Rights Act 1998 gives further effect in UK law to rights under the European Convention on Human Rights (the "Convention rights" listed in Schedule 1). Section 2 requires UK courts to take account of Strasbourg case law; section 3 requires legislation to be read and given effect, so far as possible, compatibly with Convention rights; section 4 allows a court to make a declaration of incompatibility where primary legislation cannot be read compatibly (which does not itself invalidate the legislation); section 6 makes it unlawful for a public authority to act incompatibly with a Convention right; section 7 allows a victim of such an act to bring proceedings; section 8 allows the court to grant relief or a remedy it considers just and appropriate, including damages; section 10 provides a fast-track procedure (a remedial order) to amend legislation found incompatible. Teach the learner to identify which specific Convention right and which specific HRA section is engaged by a fact pattern, since the exam rewards precision here.

#### LS.8 The place of EU law in the UK constitution
Following the UK's withdrawal from the EU, retained EU law continues to have effect in the UK constitution in a modified form, subject to ongoing statutory changes to its status and to domestic courts' powers to depart from retained EU case law in defined circumstances. Teach the learner that this is a live, still-shifting area of constitutional law and that the day-one competence standard here is recognising that EU-derived law can still be relevant to a transaction or dispute post-Brexit, not mastering the full detail of the retained-law regime.

## Explicitly not here
Detailed public international law and EU institutional law beyond the UK constitutional position of retained EU law are outside this subject's day-one-competence standard.
