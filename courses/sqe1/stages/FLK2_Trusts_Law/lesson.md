# FLK2_Trusts_Law - Lesson: Trusts Law

## Goal
The learner tests whether an express, resulting or constructive trust has been validly created, analyses beneficial entitlement, trustees' duties and liability for breach, and applies equitable remedies including tracing, at day-one-competence standard, and answers SQE1-style single-best-answer questions on all of it.

## Syllabus items taught here
- TRUSTS.1 - The three certainties and express trust creation: learn and apply: the three certainties
- TRUSTS.2 - Constitution of trusts and exceptions: learn and apply: constitution of trusts
- TRUSTS.3 - Beneficial interests and the rule in Saunders v Vautier: learn and apply: beneficial interests and saunders v vautier
- TRUSTS.4 - Charitable and non-charitable purpose trusts: learn and apply: charitable and purpose trusts
- TRUSTS.5 - Resulting trusts: learn and apply: resulting trusts
- TRUSTS.6 - Common intention constructive trusts and proprietary estoppel: learn and apply: constructive trusts and proprietary estoppel
- TRUSTS.7 - Stranger liability: knowing receipt and dishonest assistance: learn and apply: stranger liability
- TRUSTS.8 - Trustees' fiduciary duties, powers and duty of care: learn and apply: trustees' duties and powers
- TRUSTS.9 - Breach of trust, liability and tracing: learn and apply: breach of trust and tracing

## How to teach this
Open with a scenario: a person tells a friend "I want you to hold my shares for my niece" but never transfers the shares before dying; separately, an unmarried couple bought a house in one partner's sole name, with both contributing to the mortgage. Ask the learner to work out, before teaching, whether either arrangement creates a trust and of what kind - the exam consistently tests the learner's ability to classify which of the several trust doctrines a fact pattern actually engages.

#### TRUSTS.1 The three certainties and express trust creation
A valid express trust requires the three certainties (Knight v Knight): certainty of intention (words or conduct showing a genuine intention to create a trust, not merely a moral obligation or a gift with a wish attached), certainty of subject matter (the trust property and each beneficiary's share must be clear, or ascertainable), and certainty of objects (for a fixed trust, a complete list of beneficiaries must be possible; for a discretionary trust, the "is or is not" test - can it be said with certainty whether any given person is or is not a member of the class). Teach the learner to test all three certainties in order before concluding a trust exists at all.

#### TRUSTS.2 Constitution of trusts and exceptions
A trust of existing property is only completely constituted once the correct legal method of transferring that type of property to the trustee has been used (e.g. a deed or registered transfer for land, delivery for chattels, a stock transfer form and registration for shares); equity will not assist a volunteer to perfect an imperfect gift or trust ("equity will not perfect an imperfect gift"). Recognised exceptions include the rule in Re Rose (equity treats the transfer as complete once the settlor has done everything in their power, even if a third party such as a registrar has not yet acted), the rule in Strong v Bird (an intended gift is perfected if the intended donee becomes executor and the intention continued to death), and Pennington v Waine / Choithram v Pagarani (unconscionability of the donor resiling). Teach the learner to check whether the trust property was actually and correctly transferred before assuming it is validly constituted.

#### TRUSTS.3 Beneficial interests and the rule in Saunders v Vautier
Beneficial interests may be vested (the beneficiary's entitlement is certain, even if possession is postponed) or contingent (dependent on a future event, e.g. reaching a specified age), and fixed (defined shares) or discretionary (trustees choose who benefits and how much, from within a class). Under the rule in Saunders v Vautier, where all beneficiaries are of full age, capacity and together absolutely entitled (i.e. between them they own the whole beneficial interest), they can collectively direct the trustees to terminate the trust and transfer the property to them, even against the trustees' wishes. Teach the learner to check whether every beneficiary is ascertained, of age, and together entitled to the whole interest before applying the rule.

#### TRUSTS.4 Charitable and non-charitable purpose trusts
A charitable trust must have a purpose falling within one of the statutory charitable heads (Charities Act 2011, e.g. the prevention/relief of poverty, advancement of education, religion) and satisfy the public benefit requirement; it is exempt from the certainty-of-objects and perpetuity rules that apply to private trusts, because it is enforced by the Charity Commission/Attorney General rather than by named beneficiaries. Non-charitable purpose trusts are generally void for lack of a human beneficiary to enforce them (the "beneficiary principle"), except for a small number of anomalous exceptions (e.g. trusts for specific animals, or monuments, within the perpetuity period) and trusts that can be reinterpreted as being for identifiable individuals (Re Denley). Teach the learner: is there a human beneficiary who can enforce this, or does it fall within a recognised exception, before validating a purpose trust.

#### TRUSTS.5 Resulting trusts
An automatic resulting trust arises where an express trust fails (wholly or partly) or does not exhaust the beneficial interest, so the undisposed-of property results back to the settlor (or their estate). A presumed resulting trust arises where property is purchased in the name of another, or transferred gratuitously, without evidence of intended gift - equity presumes the recipient holds it on trust for the person who provided the purchase money, in proportion to their contribution, rebuttable by evidence of a contrary intention (and displaced by the presumption of advancement in limited relationships, e.g. father to child). Teach the learner to distinguish a failed express trust (automatic) from an unexplained gratuitous transfer or purchase in another's name (presumed).

#### TRUSTS.6 Common intention constructive trusts and proprietary estoppel
A common intention constructive trust over a home (typically registered in one party's sole name) requires an express or inferred common intention that both parties should have a beneficial interest, and detrimental reliance on that common intention by the claimant (e.g. financial contributions, or conduct the court would not expect absent an expectation of a share); the size of the share is then quantified by looking at the whole course of dealing (Jones v Kernott). Proprietary estoppel instead requires a clear assurance by the landowner, reasonable reliance on it by the claimant, and resulting detriment, making it unconscionable for the landowner to go back on the assurance - the remedy is flexible, and need not simply mirror the assurance given. Teach the learner to check for an express or inferred common intention first; if there is none, consider estoppel's assurance-reliance-detriment test instead.

#### TRUSTS.7 Stranger liability: knowing receipt and dishonest assistance
A stranger to a trust who receives trust property transferred in breach of trust, and who has the requisite knowledge that the receipt was traceable to a breach (assessed by what makes it unconscionable to retain the benefit), is personally liable to account for it (knowing receipt). A stranger who assists a trustee's breach, dishonestly (assessed objectively, by the standard of an honest person in the stranger's position, on the facts as the stranger believed them to be - Barlow Clowes/Ivey), is personally liable even without ever receiving any trust property (dishonest assistance). Teach the learner these are two distinct routes to the same outcome - personal liability of a non-trustee - triggered by different facts (receiving property vs helping the breach happen).

#### TRUSTS.8 Trustees' fiduciary duties, powers and duty of care
Trustees owe fiduciary duties: not to profit from their position without authorisation, not to purchase trust property (the self-dealing rule) without consent, and to avoid conflicts between their personal interest and their duty to beneficiaries. They owe a statutory duty of care (Trustee Act 2000) when exercising powers such as investment (a duty to invest in accordance with standard investment criteria - suitability and diversification - and, generally, to take advice) and maintenance/advancement (statutory powers to apply income or capital for a minor or contingent beneficiary's benefit before their interest vests). Trustees are appointed, removed or retire under statutory or express provisions in the trust instrument. Teach the learner to identify which specific fiduciary duty or power a fact pattern engages before assessing breach.

#### TRUSTS.9 Breach of trust, liability and tracing
A trustee in breach of trust is personally liable to restore the trust fund to the position it would have been in absent the breach (equitable compensation), assessed with the benefit of hindsight rather than by the tort measure of foreseeability; the court may relieve a trustee who acted honestly and reasonably (s.61 Trustee Act 1925), and a limitation period (generally six years) applies subject to exceptions (e.g. fraud, or a trustee retaining trust property). Beneficiaries can also trace trust property in equity - following its value through mixed funds and substitutions (e.g. into a purchased asset) - to recover the property itself or a proprietary claim over its traceable proceeds, which is particularly valuable where the trustee is insolvent. Teach the learner: personal claim against the trustee, or proprietary claim by tracing the property - and why tracing matters most when the wrongdoer has no money left.

## Explicitly not here
Detailed perpetuity-period calculations and the full mechanics of tracing rules in mixed bank accounts (the specific tracing "rules" for competing claims) are outside day-one competence; the standard here is correctly classifying the trust and identifying that a personal or proprietary remedy is available, not computing a precise tracing outcome.
