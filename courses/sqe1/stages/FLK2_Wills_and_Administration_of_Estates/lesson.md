# FLK2_Wills_and_Administration_of_Estates - Lesson: Wills and the Administration of Estates

## Goal
The learner tests will validity and interpretation, applies the intestacy rules, obtains the right grant of representation, administers an estate correctly including tax, and spots 1975 Act claims, at day-one-competence standard, and answers SQE1-style single-best-answer questions on all of it.

## Syllabus items taught here
- WILLS.1 - Will validity: capacity, knowledge and approval, formalities, undue influence: learn and apply: will validity
- WILLS.2 - Revocation, alteration and interpretation of wills: learn and apply: revocation, alteration and interpretation of wills
- WILLS.3 - Intestacy rules and statutory trusts: learn and apply: intestacy rules
- WILLS.4 - Grant of representation: priority, application, types: learn and apply: grant of representation
- WILLS.5 - Personal representatives' duties in estate administration: learn and apply: personal representatives' duties
- WILLS.6 - Inheritance (Provision for Family and Dependants) Act 1975 claims: learn and apply: 1975 act claims
- WILLS.7 - Inheritance Tax on lifetime and death transfers: learn and apply: inheritance tax on lifetime and death transfers
- WILLS.8 - Income Tax and CGT liability during administration: learn and apply: income tax and cgt during administration

## How to teach this
Open with a scenario: a person dies leaving a handwritten will made shortly before death, naming a friend as executor, with some assets not mentioned in it and a estranged adult child who received nothing. Ask the learner to list every legal question this raises before teaching anything - validity, interpretation, what happens to unmentioned assets, who can apply for the grant, what tax is due, and whether the child has a claim - because that is exactly how this subject is tested: one fact pattern, several distinct legal issues layered together.

#### WILLS.1 Will validity: capacity, knowledge and approval, formalities, undue influence
A valid will requires testamentary capacity (the Banks v Goodfellow test: understanding the nature of the act, the extent of the property, and the claims of those who might expect to benefit, free of any disorder of the mind poisoning their judgment), knowledge and approval of the contents (presumed once capacity and due execution are shown, unless suspicious circumstances rebut it), and formal validity under s.9 Wills Act 1837 (in writing, signed by the testator, in the presence of two witnesses present at the same time, who each sign in the testator's presence). Undue influence (coercion overpowering the testator's free will, not just persuasion) makes a will invalid if proved, with the burden on the person alleging it. Teach the learner to check these four elements in order.

#### WILLS.2 Revocation, alteration and interpretation of wills
A will is revoked by a later valid will or codicil, by marriage or civil partnership (unless made in contemplation of it), by destruction with intention to revoke, or by a duly executed written declaration. Alterations made after execution are generally invalid unless themselves executed like a will, or the words are wholly illegible. Interpretation follows the testator's expressed intention read in light of the will as a whole and admissible surrounding circumstances; the courts can rectify a will for clerical error or failure to understand instructions, and can construe ambiguous wording. Teach the learner: check for a later document or a marriage before assuming an earlier will stands.

#### WILLS.3 Intestacy rules and statutory trusts
Where there is no valid will (or it fails to dispose of everything), the intestacy rules under the Administration of Estates Act 1925 (as amended) apply in strict order: a surviving spouse/civil partner takes the personal chattels, a statutory legacy, and a share of the remainder (with the balance, if any, held on statutory trusts for issue); if there is no spouse, the estate passes to issue on statutory trusts, then in a fixed order to parents, siblings, and other specified relatives, and finally to the Crown as bona vacantia if no one qualifies. Teach the learner to apply the hierarchy step by step rather than guessing who "should" inherit.

#### WILLS.4 Grant of representation: priority, application, types
A grant of probate is issued to the executor(s) named in a valid will; letters of administration (with will annexed) issue where there is a valid will but no proving executor, following the priority order in the Non-Contentious Probate Rules; letters of administration (simple) issue on intestacy, following the statutory order of entitlement matching the intestacy beneficiaries. The application requires the original will (if any), the death certificate, and an Inheritance Tax account/return where required, before the grant issues from the Probate Registry. Teach the learner to match the type of grant to whether there is a will, and to who has priority to apply.

#### WILLS.5 Personal representatives' duties in estate administration
Personal representatives must collect in the estate's assets, pay the deceased's debts, funeral and testamentary expenses and any Inheritance Tax due, in the correct statutory order if the estate is insolvent, before distributing the residue to those entitled. They owe fiduciary duties to act in the estate's best interests, must administer within a reasonable time (with the "executor's year" as a rough benchmark before beneficiaries can press for distribution), and may need to place statutory notices (s.27 Trustee Act 1925) to protect themselves against unknown claims. Teach the learner the correct order: assets in, debts and tax out, then distribute.

#### WILLS.6 Inheritance (Provision for Family and Dependants) Act 1975 claims
Certain categories of applicant (spouse/civil partner, former spouse who has not remarried, a child, a person treated as a child of the family, a cohabitant of two years or more, or a person maintained by the deceased) can apply within six months of the grant (extendable with permission) for reasonable financial provision from the estate if the will or intestacy rules fail to provide it. The court considers factors under s.3, including the applicant's needs and resources, the size of the estate, and (for a spouse/civil partner) what they might have received on divorce. Teach the learner to check eligibility category first, then the time limit, then the s.3 factors.

#### WILLS.7 Inheritance Tax on lifetime and death transfers
On death, IHT is charged at 40% on the estate's value above the nil rate band (currently £325,000, plus a residence nil rate band where a qualifying home passes to direct descendants), after deducting spouse/civil partner exemption, charity exemption and reliefs such as Business Property Relief. Lifetime gifts are potentially exempt transfers (PETs), becoming chargeable only if the donor dies within seven years, with taper relief reducing the tax on gifts made more than three years before death. Teach the learner to check the seven-year rule and available exemptions/reliefs before calculating any liability, since the day-one standard is issue-spotting, not full computation.

#### WILLS.8 Income Tax and CGT liability during administration
Personal representatives are subject to income tax at the basic rate on income arising during administration (e.g. rent, interest) and must account for CGT on gains made on disposing of estate assets during administration, with the assets treated as acquired by the PRs at their probate (death) value ("uplift" - no CGT is charged on the death itself). Beneficiaries are taxed on income or gains once assets or income are appropriated or distributed to them. Teach the learner the recurring distinction: no CGT on death itself, but CGT can arise if the PRs later sell an asset that has grown in value since the death valuation.

## Explicitly not here
Detailed IHT computation across multiple lifetime gifts and full drafting of will clauses are outside day-one competence; the standard here is correctly identifying validity, entitlement, the right grant, and which tax/relief applies, not producing a full calculation or a drafted will.
