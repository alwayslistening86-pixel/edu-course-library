# FLK1_Dispute_Resolution - Lesson: Dispute Resolution

## Goal
The learner takes a civil dispute from choosing a resolution route through issuing, interim applications, trial and enforcement, applying the Civil Procedure Rules at day-one-competence standard, and answers SQE1-style single-best-answer questions on all of it.

## How to teach this
Open with: a supplier is owed £45,000 by a customer who disputes the goods were satisfactory. Walk the learner through the entire lifecycle this stage covers - should they litigate at all, is the claim still in time, where do they issue, how do they serve, what if the customer ignores it, how do they get a quick win if the defence is hopeless, what happens at trial, and how do they actually get paid if they win - before teaching each rule, so every item lands in a real procedural sequence rather than as an isolated topic.

#### DR.1 Choosing a dispute resolution mechanism: negotiation, mediation, arbitration, litigation
A solicitor must be able to identify the characteristics of negotiation, mediation, arbitration and litigation that make each an appropriate mechanism for a given dispute: negotiation and mediation are consensual and confidential but not binding unless a settlement is reached; arbitration produces a binding, enforceable award via a process the parties have chosen (often faster and more private than court, but with limited rights of appeal); litigation is compulsory if the other party will not engage but is public, more expensive and slower. Teach the learner to weigh cost, speed, confidentiality, relationship preservation and enforceability for a given client fact pattern, since the exam tests this judgement, not just definitions.

#### DR.2 Limitation periods and pre-action protocols
Limitation periods must be calculated correctly for the cause of action: broadly six years from breach for a simple contract claim and six years from the date the damage occurs for most tort claims (with different periods for some claims, e.g. personal injury). Pre-action protocols (general Practice Direction on Pre-Action Conduct, plus claim-specific protocols) set out steps parties should take before issuing - exchanging information, considering ADR - and non-compliance can carry costs consequences even if the underlying claim succeeds. Teach the learner to check limitation first in any dispute scenario, since a claim issued out of time is normally simply lost regardless of its merits.

#### DR.3 Court jurisdiction, allocation and issuing/serving proceedings
Business is allocated between the High Court and the county court broadly by value and complexity, with specialist courts (e.g. the Business and Property Courts) having their own jurisdiction for particular claim types. A claim is issued by filing a claim form, which may add, remove or substitute parties as needed. Service within the jurisdiction follows set methods and deemed service dates; service outside the jurisdiction requires either the court's permission or use of an applicable international mechanism, and strict time limits govern how long after issue a claim form must be served, with alternative service available where normal methods are impracticable.

#### DR.4 Responding to a claim and statements of case
A defendant can admit the claim, acknowledge service and file a defence and/or counterclaim, dispute the court's jurisdiction, or do nothing and risk default judgment being entered against them (which can later be set aside only on specific grounds). Statements of case - the claim form, particulars of claim, defence, reply, and any Part 20 (additional) claim - must have the right purpose, structure and content for the claim in contract or tort being advanced, can be the subject of a request for further information if unclear, and can be amended (with permission required after certain stages). Teach the learner to check time limits for each responsive step, since missing them has real procedural consequences.

#### DR.5 Interim applications: summary judgment, interim payments, interim injunctions
Interim applications follow a standard application procedure (notice, evidence, hearing) but each type has its own test: summary judgment requires showing the other side has no real prospect of success on the claim or defence and there is no other compelling reason for trial; an interim payment requires showing the claimant would obtain judgment for a substantial sum; an interim injunction (applying American Cyanamid principles) requires a serious question to be tried, an assessment of the balance of convenience, and usually a cross-undertaking in damages. Teach the learner to match the fact pattern (a hopeless defence, urgent cash-flow need, or urgent risk of harm) to the correct application.

#### DR.6 Case management and track allocation
The overriding objective requires the court and parties to deal with cases justly and at proportionate cost, and underpins every case-management decision. Claims are allocated to the small claims, fast, intermediate or multi-track largely by value and complexity, each track carrying different procedural rules and cost consequences. The court gives case management directions appropriate to the allocated track, and can impose sanctions for non-compliance with orders, with relief from sanctions available only applying the recognised three-stage test (seriousness of breach, reason for it, all the circumstances). Costs and case management conferences deal with budgeting on the more complex tracks.

#### DR.7 Evidence: hearsay, expert evidence, witness evidence, burden and standard of proof
The claimant generally bears the burden of proving their case on the balance of probabilities (the civil standard). Relevance and admissibility govern what evidence the court will hear, including hearsay evidence (a statement not made in oral evidence at trial, admissible in civil proceedings subject to notice requirements and weight considerations). Expert evidence requires the court's permission, is governed by the expert's overriding duty to the court rather than the instructing party, and may involve a single joint expert or a discussion between experts to narrow issues. Witness evidence is typically given by witness statement (or affidavit where required), standing as the witness's evidence-in-chief at trial.

#### DR.8 Disclosure and privilege
Standard disclosure requires a party to disclose documents they rely on and documents adversely affecting their own or another party's case, or supporting another party's case, following a reasonable search; the court can order specific disclosure of particular documents, and in limited circumstances pre-action or non-party disclosure. Electronic disclosure follows the same principles applied to electronic documents. Privilege (legal advice privilege and litigation privilege) protects certain communications from disclosure even though otherwise relevant, and without prejudice communications (genuine settlement negotiations) are protected from being put before the court on the question of liability; privilege can be waived, intentionally or inadvertently.

#### DR.9 Trial procedure and judgment
Trial preparation includes summoning witnesses where needed, pre-trial checklists/listing questionnaires and hearings, and preparing trial bundles. Trial procedure covers courtroom etiquette, modes of address, and the distinction between leading questions (suggesting the answer, generally not permitted in examination-in-chief) and non-leading questions. The court's judgment determines the claim and has binding legal effect between the parties, forming the basis for any subsequent enforcement. Teach the learner that the procedural steps before trial (case management, disclosure, evidence) exist specifically to make the trial itself efficient and fair, not as isolated hoops.

#### DR.10 Costs, Part 36 offers, and appeals
Costs generally follow the event (the loser pays the winner's reasonable costs), managed through costs budgeting on applicable tracks, with inter-partes costs orders available at interim stages and at the end of a case, and non-party costs orders in limited circumstances. A Part 36 offer is a formal settlement offer carrying automatic costs consequences if not beaten at trial, creating a strong incentive to settle realistically; other, non-Part-36 offers can still be relevant to costs. Security for costs may be ordered against a claimant in limited circumstances. Appeals require permission (from the lower court or the appeal court), go to the correct destination court, and must identify valid grounds (an error of law, or a decision wrong or unjust because of a serious procedural irregularity).

#### DR.11 Enforcement of money judgments
Winning a judgment does not itself produce payment: the judgment creditor may need to obtain information from the judgment debtor about their means (e.g. an order to obtain information), then choose an appropriate enforcement method - such as a third party debt order (freezing money a third party owes the debtor), a charging order (secured against the debtor's property), an attachment of earnings order, or a writ/warrant of control (seizing goods). Teach the learner that enforcement is a distinct, separate stage from winning the claim, and the right method depends on what is known about the debtor's assets.

## Explicitly not here
The substantive law of the underlying claim (contract or tort) is covered in CON.1-CON.9 and TORT.1-TORT.8; this stage covers only the procedural route the claim takes through the civil courts.
