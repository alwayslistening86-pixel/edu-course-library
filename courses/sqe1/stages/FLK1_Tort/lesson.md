# FLK1_Tort - Lesson: Tort

## Goal
The learner runs a structured negligence analysis and correctly applies the specific liability regimes (employers', vicarious, occupiers', product, nuisance) to a realistic fact pattern at day-one-competence standard, and answers SQE1-style single-best-answer questions on all of it.

## How to teach this
Open with: a visitor slips on a wet floor in a shop with no warning sign, is injured, and the shop's cleaner (an agency worker, not a direct employee) had mopped the floor ten minutes earlier. Have the learner identify every possible claim and defendant before teaching each rule - the shop under occupiers' liability, the cleaner or their agency under negligence and vicarious liability - since spotting multiple overlapping causes of action in one fact pattern is exactly what the exam tests.

#### TORT.1 Negligence: duty of care and breach
A duty of care arises where harm is reasonably foreseeable, there is sufficient proximity between claimant and defendant, and it is fair, just and reasonable to impose a duty (Caparo Industries v Dickman [1990]); many relationships (e.g. driver-road user, doctor-patient, employer-employee) are established duty categories not requiring fresh analysis. Breach asks whether the defendant fell below the standard of the reasonable person in their position - the general standard for an ordinary defendant, or the standard of the ordinarily competent member of a profession for a professional defendant (Bolam v Friern Hospital Management Committee [1957]), weighing the foreseeability and magnitude of risk against the cost and practicability of precautions and the utility of the defendant's conduct.

#### TORT.2 Negligence: causation, remoteness and loss
Factual causation normally uses the "but for" test: but for the defendant's breach, would the claimant have suffered the loss? Where medical or scientific uncertainty makes strict but-for causation hard to prove, exceptional rules (e.g. material contribution to risk) may apply in narrow circumstances. Legal causation asks whether an intervening act breaks the chain of causation. Remoteness (The Wagon Mound [1961]) limits recovery to loss of a type that was reasonably foreseeable, even if its precise extent or manner was not. Teach the learner to run duty, breach, causation and remoteness as four separate, sequential checks, since a fact pattern commonly fails on only one.

#### TORT.3 Pure economic loss and psychiatric harm
Pure economic loss (financial loss not flowing from physical damage to the claimant's own person or property) is generally not recoverable in negligence, except in defined circumstances such as a negligent misstatement relied on where a special relationship of proximity exists (Hedley Byrne v Heller [1964]). Claims for psychiatric harm distinguish primary victims (those within the zone of physical danger) from secondary victims (witnesses to a shocking event, who additionally must show a close tie of love and affection with the primary victim, physical and temporal proximity to the event or its immediate aftermath, and that the harm was caused by direct perception, per Alcock v Chief Constable of South Yorkshire [1992]) - and a recognised psychiatric illness, not mere grief, must be shown either way.

#### TORT.4 Employers' primary liability and vicarious liability
Employers' primary liability is the employer's own direct duty to take reasonable care for employee safety (safe system of work, competent staff, adequate equipment, safe premises) - a personal, non-delegable duty distinct from vicarious liability. Vicarious liability makes an employer liable for torts committed by an employee (or someone in a relationship "akin to employment") where the tort is committed in the course of employment, applying a "close connection" test to acts that are wrongful departures from authorised conduct but sufficiently connected to it. Teach the learner to keep these two bases of liability analytically separate: one is the employer's own breach, the other is liability for someone else's tort.

#### TORT.5 Defences: volenti, contributory negligence, illegality
Volenti non fit injuria (consent to the risk) is a complete defence where the claimant freely and with full knowledge of the risk agreed to accept it - narrowly applied, and rarely succeeding against an employee in the course of their work. Contributory negligence is a partial defence: damages are reduced to the extent the court considers just and equitable having regard to the claimant's own share of responsibility for their loss (Law Reform (Contributory Negligence) Act 1945). The illegality defence (ex turpi causa) can bar a claim where it is founded on the claimant's own serious illegal conduct closely connected to the loss claimed.

#### TORT.6 Occupiers' liability (1957 and 1984 Acts)
The Occupiers' Liability Act 1957 imposes a duty on an occupier to take reasonable care to see that a visitor will be reasonably safe using the premises for the purpose for which they are invited or permitted to be there - the common duty of care - with special provision for child visitors (an occupier must be prepared for children to be less careful) and for visitors exercising a trade or calling (who may be expected to guard against risks ordinarily incident to that trade). The Occupiers' Liability Act 1984 imposes a narrower duty toward non-visitors (e.g. trespassers) only where the occupier knows or has reasonable grounds to believe a danger exists and that someone may come into its vicinity, and it is reasonable to expect some protection to be offered. Defences and exclusion of liability differ between the two Acts, with exclusion of the 1957 duty to a visitor limited by the Unfair Contract Terms Act 1977 where relevant.

#### TORT.7 Product liability: negligence and Consumer Protection Act 1987
A manufacturer owes a duty of care in negligence to the ultimate consumer of a product (Donoghue v Stevenson [1932] - the "neighbour principle"), requiring the claimant to prove fault. The Consumer Protection Act 1987 provides a separate, stricter route: a producer (and certain others in the supply chain) is liable for damage caused by a defect in a product without the claimant needing to prove fault, subject to defined defences (e.g. the "development risks" defence, where the state of scientific and technical knowledge at the time meant the defect could not have been discovered). Teach the learner to consider both routes, since the 1987 Act can succeed where fault cannot be proved.

#### TORT.8 Nuisance and the rule in Rylands v Fletcher
Private nuisance protects a claimant's use and enjoyment of land against an unlawful, indirect interference (e.g. noise, smell, vibration) that is unreasonable given its nature, duration, locality and any malice, and generally requires a proprietary interest in the affected land to sue. Public nuisance protects the public generally and can be claimed by an individual who suffers particular, greater damage than the public at large. The rule in Rylands v Fletcher imposes strict liability where a defendant brings onto their land, and keeps there, something likely to cause mischief if it escapes, and it does escape, causing foreseeable damage - a distinct, narrower doctrine from ordinary nuisance. Remedies for nuisance are damages and/or an injunction, subject to available defences.
