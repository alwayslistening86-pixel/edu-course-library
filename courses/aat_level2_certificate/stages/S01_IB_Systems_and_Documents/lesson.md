# S01_IB_Systems_and_Documents - Lesson: Business documents, prime-entry books, coding and bookkeeping systems

## Goal
The learner names the purpose of each core business document, explains the bookkeeping process from prime entry to financial statements, creates coding systems, and states the dual effect and the accounting equation.

## Syllabus items taught here
- IB1.1 - The purpose of business documents: petty cash voucher, invoice, credit note (including for PPD), remittance advice, statement of account, bank statement
- IB1.2 - The process of recording bookkeeping transactions: books of prime entry, the cash book and petty cash book, the ledgers, the trial balance, financial statements, accuracy and digital systems
- IB1.3 - Create and use coding systems: types of code and where to use them
- IB1.4 - Set bookkeeping systems: the dual effect (debits and credits) and the accounting equation

## How to teach this
Ask the learner: when a shop sells goods on credit, what piece of paper does the customer receive, and what happens if they later return some of the goods? Have the learner attempt every calculation (VAT, discounts, control-account reconciliations, bank reconciliations, FIFO/LIFO/AVCO, labour pay, overhead absorption, product costs, budget variances) with full workings before checking the model answer -- these are computer-marked numeric-entry items in the real AAT assessment, so exact figures matter. Every numeric example in this course was computed and verified in Python when the course was built. UK VAT is taken at the standard rate of 20% throughout unless an item states otherwise; check the current rate at gov.uk if it may have changed. AAT's assessments use a mix of multiple-choice, numeric gap-fill and journal/ledger-entry question tools; this course's items mirror the same calculation-and-entry style using clearly marked short-answer and multiple-choice items.

#### IB1.1 The purpose of business documents: petty cash voucher, invoice, credit note (including for PPD), remittance advice, statement of account, bank statement
The core business documents and their purpose: a **petty cash voucher** records a small cash payment (e.g. £5 for stamps) and who authorised it; an **invoice** is sent by a seller to a buyer requesting payment for goods/services supplied, showing what was sold, at what price, and any VAT; a **credit note** (including one issued for prompt payment discount, PPD) reduces an amount owed -- issued when goods are returned, an invoice was overcharged, or (at Level 2) when a customer takes up a prompt payment discount; a **remittance advice** is sent with (or before) a payment, telling the recipient which invoice(s) the payment covers; a **statement of account** is sent periodically by a supplier to a customer, listing all invoices, credit notes and payments on the account and the balance outstanding; a **bank statement** is issued by the bank showing all transactions through a bank account and the resulting balance.

#### IB1.2 The process of recording bookkeeping transactions: books of prime entry, the cash book and petty cash book, the ledgers, the trial balance, financial statements, accuracy and digital systems
The **books of prime entry** are where transactions are first recorded, from the original documents, before being summarised into the ledgers: the **sales daybook** (credit sales), **sales returns daybook** (credit notes issued to customers), **purchases daybook** (credit purchases), **purchases returns daybook** (credit notes received from suppliers), **discounts allowed daybook** and **discounts received daybook** (PPD taken by customers/given by suppliers). The **cash book** and **petty cash book** can be a book of prime entry only, or (in many small businesses) also form part of the double-entry system itself. The **ledgers** are: the **receivables ledger** (one account per credit customer), the **payables ledger** (one account per credit supplier) and the **general ledger** (holds every other account: assets, liabilities, income, expenses, plus the receivables and payables *control* accounts, which summarise the total owed by/to all customers/suppliers). The **trial balance** lists every general ledger account balance in a debit or credit column, to check total debits equal total credits before the **financial statements** -- the **statement of profit or loss** (income less expenses, over a period) and the **statement of financial position** (assets, liabilities and capital, at a point in time) -- are prepared. Accurate entry matters: in a manual system an error can go undetected and mislead decisions; in a digital system, one wrong entry can be repeated automatically (e.g. via a recurring entry) or corrupt an automated reconciliation. Digital systems can import transactions from bank records, csv files and third-party software, rather than every line being typed by hand.

#### IB1.3 Create and use coding systems: types of code and where to use them
A **coding system** gives every customer, supplier, product and general ledger account a unique reference, used in the books of prime entry, the receivables ledger and the payables ledger. Codes can be **alphabetical** (e.g. SMIT for Smith Ltd), **numerical** (e.g. 10234) or **alphanumerical** (e.g. SM102, combining both) -- alphabetical codes are easy to recognise, numerical codes sort and search efficiently and avoid ambiguity between similarly-named accounts, and alphanumerical codes can combine both benefits (e.g. grouping by customer initial, then a unique number).

#### IB1.4 Set bookkeeping systems: the dual effect (debits and credits) and the accounting equation
The **dual effect** is the principle that every transaction affects two accounts: a **debit** (an increase in an asset or expense, or a decrease in a liability, income or capital) and an equal and opposite **credit** (an increase in a liability, income or capital, or a decrease in an asset or expense) -- e.g. buying stock for cash debits Inventory (an asset increases) and credits the Bank/Cash account (an asset decreases). The **accounting equation** is Assets = Liabilities + Equity (capital); rearranged, Equity = Assets - Liabilities. *Worked example:* a business has assets of £18,500 and liabilities of £6,200; its equity (capital) is £18,500 - £6,200 = **£12,300**. Learners need to be able to classify items as **assets** (something the business owns or is owed, e.g. cash, inventory, receivables), **liabilities** (something the business owes, e.g. payables, a loan), **equity/capital** (the owner's stake, i.e. assets minus liabilities), **income/revenue** (amounts earned, e.g. sales) or **expenses/costs** (amounts spent running the business, e.g. rent, wages).
