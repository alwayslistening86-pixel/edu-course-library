## How to teach this
Ask: 'When you push a shopping trolley at steady speed, why do you still need to push? And which forces are acting on the trolley?'

#### 4.5.1.1 Scalar and vector quantities
Scalars have magnitude only (distance, speed, mass, energy, time); vectors have magnitude and direction (force, displacement, velocity, acceleration, momentum). Vectors are represented by arrows whose length shows magnitude and direction shows direction.

#### 4.5.1.2 Contact and non-contact forces
A force is a push or a pull that acts on an object due to an interaction with another. Contact forces need touching: friction, air resistance, tension, normal contact force. Non-contact forces act at a distance: gravity, electrostatic and magnetic forces. Forces come in pairs: the interaction between two objects.

#### 4.5.1.3 Gravity
Mass is the amount of matter in an object (kg) and is constant; weight is the force of gravity on the object (N) and depends on the gravitational field strength: weight = mass x gravitational field strength (W = m g), with g = 9.8 N/kg on Earth. A 5 kg mass weighs 49 N on Earth but only about 8 N on the Moon. Weight acts through the centre of mass; weight is measured using a calibrated spring balance (newtonmeter).

#### 4.5.1.4 Resultant forces
The resultant force is the single force that has the same effect as all the forces acting together. For forces along a line, add those in one direction and subtract those in the opposite direction: 12 N right and 5 N left gives 7 N right. A free-body diagram shows all forces. Higher tier: resolve a force into components at right angles, and find the resultant of two forces at an angle using a scale drawing (parallelogram of forces).

#### 4.5.2 Work done and energy transfer
Work done = force x distance moved along the line of action of the force (W = F s), with 1 joule equal to 1 newton-metre. Work done against friction transfers energy to thermal stores, which is why brakes heat up. Lifting a 20 N weight through 3 m does 60 J of work.

#### 4.5.3 Forces and elasticity
Stretching, compressing or bending an object needs more than one force. Elastic deformation: the object returns to its original shape when the forces are removed; inelastic deformation: it does not. For a spring within its limit of proportionality: force = spring constant x extension (F = k e), a straight-line force-extension graph. The work done stretching a spring (elastic potential energy) = 0.5 x spring constant x extension squared, which equals the area under the force-extension graph up to the limit.

#### 4.5.4 Moments, levers and gears
The moment of a force is the turning effect: moment = force x perpendicular distance from the pivot to the line of action (M = F d), in newton-metres. For an object in equilibrium the sum of clockwise moments equals the sum of anticlockwise moments (principle of moments). A 50 N force at 0.4 m balances a force of 100 N at 0.2 m. Levers and gears transmit rotational effects: a lever gives a force multiplier, and a larger gear turns more slowly but with a bigger moment.

#### RP6 Required practical 6: force and extension of a spring
Investigate the relationship between force and extension of a spring. Hang a spring from a clamp, measure its original length with a ruler, add masses (weights) in 100 g steps (weight = m g), measure the new length each time, and calculate extension = new length minus original length. Plot force against extension and find the gradient (spring constant). Read the ruler at eye level, use a fiducial marker, and do not exceed the limit of proportionality.
