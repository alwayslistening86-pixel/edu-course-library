# S03_Mains_Power_the_Grid_and_Static - Lesson: Mains electricity, power, the National Grid, static electricity and electric fields

## Goal
The learner distinguishes direct and alternating current, explains mains wiring and safety, calculates electrical power and energy, describes the National Grid, and explains static charge and electric fields.

## How to teach this
Ask: 'Why does a metal-cased kettle need an earth wire, and why are the National Grid cables carried at hundreds of thousands of volts?'

#### 4.2.3.1 Direct and alternating potential difference
Cells and batteries supply direct current (d.c.), which flows one way. Mains electricity is alternating current (a.c.): the potential difference changes direction continuously. In the UK mains is about 230 V with a frequency of 50 Hz (50 cycles per second).

#### 4.2.3.2 Mains electricity
Three-core cable: the live wire (brown) carries the alternating potential difference, at about 230 V; the neutral wire (blue) completes the circuit at about 0 V; the earth wire (green and yellow) is a safety wire that stops the casing becoming live, by carrying current to earth and blowing the fuse. Touching the live wire is dangerous because a current would flow through the body to earth. Fuses and circuit breakers are in the live wire.

#### 4.2.4.1 Power
Electrical power = potential difference x current (P = V I) and = current squared x resistance (P = I squared R), in watts. A 2300 W kettle on 230 V draws 10 A, so a 13 A fuse is suitable. Energy transferred = power x time (E = P t) = charge x potential difference (E = Q V).

#### 4.2.4.2 Energy transfers in everyday appliances
Appliances transfer energy from the mains to useful stores, by heating (kettle), motion (fan), light or sound. Power rating shows the energy transferred per second: a 60 W lamp uses 60 J each second. The work done by the charge depends on potential difference: the higher the pd, the more energy per coulomb. Compare appliances by power, time in use and cost.

#### 4.2.4.3 The National Grid
The National Grid connects power stations to consumers. Step-up transformers increase the potential difference to 400 000 V for transmission, which reduces the current for the same power, so less energy is wasted as heat in the cables (power loss = I squared R). Step-down transformers then decrease the potential difference to a safe 230 V for homes.

#### 4.2.5.1 Static charge
Rubbing two insulating materials transfers electrons from one surface to the other: the material gaining electrons becomes negatively charged, the one losing them positively charged. Like charges repel and unlike charges attract; the force is non-contact. Charged objects attract neutral insulators by induced charge. A spark occurs when a large potential difference builds up and charge jumps to earth; earthing removes the charge safely.

#### 4.2.5.2 Electric fields
A charged object creates an electric field around it, and a second charged object in the field experiences a force that acts without contact. The field is stronger closer to the charge. Field lines show the direction of the force on a positive charge: away from positive charges and towards negative ones. Sparks and the attraction of neutral objects are explained by fields.
