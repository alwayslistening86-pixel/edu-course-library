## How to teach this
Ask: 'Radio, microwaves, light and X-rays all seem very different. What do they have in common, and what makes them differ?'

#### 4.6.2.1 Types of electromagnetic waves
Electromagnetic (EM) waves are transverse waves that transfer energy from a source to an absorber, and all travel at the same speed in a vacuum or air, 3 x 10 to the power 8 m/s. The spectrum in order of increasing frequency (decreasing wavelength): radio waves, microwaves, infrared, visible light, ultraviolet, X-rays, gamma rays. The visible spectrum runs from red (longest wavelength) to violet (shortest).

#### 4.6.2.2 Properties of electromagnetic waves 1
Different substances may absorb, transmit, refract or reflect EM waves in ways that depend on wavelength. Radio waves can be produced by oscillations in electrical circuits, and when absorbed by a conductor they create an alternating current with the same frequency as the wave, which is how radio receivers work. Refraction of EM waves at boundaries between materials follows from a change in speed.

#### 4.6.2.3 Properties of electromagnetic waves 2
Changes in atoms and nuclei can generate EM waves over a range of frequencies: electrons changing energy levels emit UV or visible light, and changes in the nucleus emit gamma rays. Ultraviolet, X-rays and gamma rays are ionising radiation that can cause mutations and cancer; UV also causes sunburn and skin cancer. Radiation dose in sieverts measures the risk. Higher frequency means more harmful.

#### 4.6.2.4 Uses and applications of electromagnetic waves
Radio waves: broadcasting and communications; microwaves: satellite communications and cooking; infrared: heating, thermal imaging and remote controls; visible light: fibre-optic communication and photography; ultraviolet: security marking and fluorescent lamps; X-rays: medical imaging of bones; gamma rays: sterilising equipment and treating cancer. Each use depends on how the wave is absorbed, transmitted or reflected.

#### 4.6.2.5 Lenses
A convex (converging) lens brings parallel rays to a focus; a concave (diverging) lens spreads them. A real image can be projected onto a screen (rays actually meet); a virtual image is formed where rays only appear to come from, and cannot be projected. Magnification = image height / object height. A convex lens used as a magnifying glass gives a virtual, upright, magnified image if the object is closer than the focal length.

#### 4.6.2.6 Visible light
Coloured objects reflect their own colour and absorb others: a red object reflects red light. White objects reflect all colours, and black absorb all. Filters transmit certain colours and absorb others. Specular reflection from smooth surfaces gives clear images; diffuse reflection from rough surfaces scatters light. Transparent, translucent and opaque describe how light is transmitted.

#### 4.6.3.1 Emission and absorption of infrared radiation
All objects emit and absorb infrared radiation. The hotter an object, the more infrared it emits in a given time. A good absorber is also a good emitter; dark, matt surfaces are best at absorbing and emitting, while light, shiny surfaces reflect it. An object at constant temperature is absorbing and emitting infrared radiation at the same rate.

#### 4.6.3.2 Perfect black bodies and radiation (Higher tier)
A perfect black body absorbs all radiation that hits it and is the best possible emitter for its temperature. The intensity and distribution of wavelengths emitted depend on temperature: as temperature increases, the intensity increases and the peak shifts to shorter wavelengths. The temperature of the Earth depends on the balance between radiation absorbed from the Sun and radiation emitted, changed by the atmosphere.

#### RP9 Required practical 9: reflection and refraction of light
Investigate the reflection of light by different types of surface and the refraction of light by different substances. Use a ray box with a slit, a plane mirror or a glass block, mark incident and emergent rays on paper, draw the normal, and measure angles with a protractor. For reflection, plot angle of incidence against angle of reflection; for refraction in a glass block, note that the ray bends towards the normal on entering. Use a sharp pencil and narrow beam.

#### RP10 Required practical 10: infrared radiation and surfaces
Investigate how the amount of infrared radiation absorbed or radiated by a surface depends on its nature. Use a Leslie cube (each face a different surface) filled with hot water and an infrared detector or thermometer at a fixed distance from each face, or two cans of different colours. Control the distance, water temperature and detector. The matt black surface emits most.
