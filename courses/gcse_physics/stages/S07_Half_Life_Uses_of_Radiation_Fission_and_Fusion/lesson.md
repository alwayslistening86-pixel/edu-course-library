## How to teach this
Ask: 'A sample loses half its activity every 6 hours. After a day, how much is left? And why can we never say exactly when one particular nucleus will decay?'

#### 4.4.2.3 Half-lives and the random nature of radioactive decay
Radioactive decay is random: we cannot predict when a particular nucleus will decay, but for a large number the rate is predictable. The half-life is the time for the number of nuclei of an isotope in a sample to halve, or for the count rate to fall to half its initial value. A source with a count rate of 800 counts per minute and a half-life of 3 hours has 400 after 3 h, 200 after 6 h, 100 after 9 h. Higher tier: the net decline is the ratio of the final to initial activity, after n half-lives (1/2 to the power n).

#### 4.4.2.4 Radioactive contamination
Contamination is the unwanted presence of materials containing radioactive atoms on other materials; the hazard depends on the radiation type. Irradiation is exposure to radiation from a source without the source touching, and does not make the object radioactive. Contamination continues to irradiate until the source decays, so it is more dangerous if ingested. Precautions include gloves, tongs, protective clothing, limiting exposure time and shielding.

#### 4.4.3.1 Background radiation
Background radiation is around us all the time: natural sources include rocks (radon gas), cosmic rays from space and food; man-made sources include fallout from nuclear weapons testing and accidents, and medical procedures. Levels vary with location and occupation. Radiation dose (in sieverts) measures the risk of harm to human tissue; large doses can cause cell damage and cancer.

#### 4.4.3.2 Different half-lives of radioactive isotopes
Isotopes with a long half-life stay hazardous for a very long time but emit radiation at a low rate; those with a short half-life emit radiation at a high rate but decay away quickly. This affects medical uses and nuclear waste storage: waste with a long half-life must be stored safely for thousands of years.

#### 4.4.3.3 Uses of nuclear radiation
Medical: exploration of internal organs with tracers (gamma emitters with short half-lives), and treatment of cancer with radiotherapy, which is targeted to avoid healthy cells. Industry: monitoring the thickness of paper or metal foil with beta sources, and finding leaks in pipes. Safety and short half-lives reduce risk; benefits and risks must be weighed.

#### 4.4.4.1 Nuclear fission
Fission is the splitting of a large unstable nucleus, such as uranium-235 or plutonium-239, after it absorbs a neutron: it splits into two smaller nuclei of roughly equal size, emits two or three neutrons and releases energy as gamma radiation. The neutrons can trigger further fissions: a chain reaction. In a reactor, control rods absorb neutrons to keep the chain reaction under control and a moderator slows neutrons. In a nuclear weapon the chain reaction is uncontrolled.

#### 4.4.4.2 Nuclear fusion
Fusion is the joining of two light nuclei to form a heavier nucleus, releasing energy because the mass of the products is slightly less than the mass of the reactants, and the difference is transferred as energy. Fusion happens in stars at very high temperature and pressure. Controlled fusion on Earth is difficult because of the extreme conditions needed to overcome the electrostatic repulsion of the nuclei.
