# S10_Newtons_Laws_Stopping_Distance_and_Practical - Lesson: Newton's three laws, stopping distance and braking (required practical 7)

## Goal

## How to teach this
Ask: 'Why does a heavier lorry take longer to stop, and why do you lurch forward when a bus brakes suddenly?'

#### 4.5.6.2.1 Newton's First Law
If the resultant force on an object is zero, a stationary object stays stationary, and a moving object continues at constant speed in a straight line. So a constant velocity needs no resultant force. Inertia is the tendency of an object to continue in its state of rest or uniform motion; more mass means more inertia.

#### 4.5.6.2.2 Newton's Second Law
The acceleration of an object is proportional to the resultant force and inversely proportional to its mass: resultant force = mass x acceleration (F = m a). A 1200 kg car with a resultant force of 3000 N accelerates at 2.5 m/s2. Inertial mass measures how difficult it is to change an object's velocity: mass = force / acceleration.

#### 4.5.6.2.3 Newton's Third Law
Whenever two objects interact, the forces they exert on each other are equal in size and opposite in direction. The two forces act on different objects, so they do not cancel. A book on a table is pushed up by the table with a force equal to the book's push down on the table.

#### 4.5.6.3.1 Stopping distance
Stopping distance = thinking distance + braking distance. Thinking distance is the distance travelled during the driver's reaction time; braking distance is the distance travelled under the braking force. Both increase with speed; for example doubling speed roughly quadruples braking distance.

#### 4.5.6.3.2 Reaction time
A typical reaction time is 0.2 to 0.9 s. It can be increased by tiredness, alcohol, drugs and distractions such as a phone, which increases thinking distance. It is measured by the ruler drop test or computer tests.

#### 4.5.6.3.3 Factors affecting braking distance 1
Braking distance increases with speed, greater mass, wet or icy roads (less friction), and worn tyres or brakes. Less friction between tyre and road means a smaller braking force, so a longer distance for the same kinetic energy.

#### 4.5.6.3.4 Factors affecting braking distance 2 (Higher tier)
When a force brakes a vehicle, work done by the braking force equals the kinetic energy lost, transferred to thermal energy in the brakes. A larger deceleration needs a greater force: high forces can cause brakes to overheat or the driver to lose control. Because kinetic energy = 0.5 m v squared, doubling speed quadruples kinetic energy and the braking distance.

#### RP7 Required practical 7: force, mass and acceleration
Use a trolley pulled along a bench by a string over a pulley with hanging masses; measure acceleration with light gates or a ticker timer or by timing over a set distance. To vary the force, transfer masses from the trolley to the hanger to keep total mass constant; to vary the mass, add masses to the trolley while keeping the force constant. Plot acceleration against force (a straight line through the origin) and acceleration against 1/mass. Compensate for friction with a slightly tilted track.
