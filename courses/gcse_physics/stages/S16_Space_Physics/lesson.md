# S16_Space_Physics - Lesson: The Solar System, life cycle of a star, orbits and red-shift

## Goal
The learner describes the Solar System, the life cycle of stars, orbital motion of natural and artificial satellites, and red-shift as evidence for the Big Bang.

## Syllabus items taught here
- 4.8.1.1 - Our Solar System: learn and apply: our solar system
- 4.8.1.2 - The life cycle of a star: learn and apply: the life cycle of a star

- 4.8.2.1 - Red-shift: learn and apply: red-shift

## How to teach this
Ask: 'Where did the atoms in your body come from, and how do we know the universe had a beginning?'

#### 4.8.1.1 Our Solar System
Our Solar System is a small part of the Milky Way galaxy. It includes the Sun, eight planets and their natural satellites (moons), dwarf planets (such as Pluto), asteroids and comets. The Sun and planets formed from a cloud of dust and gas (a nebula) pulled together by gravity, with the Sun forming at the centre when fusion began. Artificial satellites are human-made and orbit the Earth for communications, weather and observation.

#### 4.8.1.2 The life cycle of a star
A star forms from a nebula as gravity pulls dust and gas into a protostar, and when hot enough fusion of hydrogen begins. In the main sequence the star is stable because the outward force from fusion balances the inward force of gravity. When hydrogen runs out, a star like the Sun expands into a red giant, then becomes a white dwarf and cools to a black dwarf. A star much more massive than the Sun becomes a red supergiant, then explodes as a supernova, leaving a neutron star or a black hole. Fusion inside stars makes elements up to iron; heavier elements form in supernovae and are scattered into new nebulae.

#### 4.8.1.3 Orbital motion, natural and artificial satellites
Gravity provides the force that keeps satellites and planets in orbit, always acting towards the centre. In a circular orbit the speed is constant but the direction changes, so the velocity is continuously changing. Higher tier: for a stable orbit of a given radius, there is only one speed; if the speed changes, the radius must change: a smaller orbit needs a higher speed, and a larger orbit a lower speed.

#### 4.8.2.1 Red-shift
Light from distant galaxies is red-shifted: the wavelengths are longer than expected, so the galaxies are moving away. The further away a galaxy, the greater the red-shift and the faster it recedes, which shows the universe is expanding. This supports the Big Bang theory: all matter began from a very small, hot, dense region that has been expanding for about 13.8 billion years. Cosmic microwave background radiation, as leftover radiation, is further evidence. Observations of galaxy rotation lead to dark matter and dark energy hypotheses.
