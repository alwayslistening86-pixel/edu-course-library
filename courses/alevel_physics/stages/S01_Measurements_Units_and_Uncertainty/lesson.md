## How to teach this
Ask for an estimate of the number of heartbeats in a lifetime, to the nearest power of ten, before any rules. Teach from the physics outwards: state the principle, derive or justify the equation, then do a worked example with units and sensible significant figures. Use AQA's data and formulae booklet values (e.g. g = 9.81 N kg^-1, e = 1.60 x 10^-19 C, h = 6.63 x 10^-34 J s, c = 3.00 x 10^8 m s^-1). Every numerical answer here was computed when the course was built.

#### 3.1.1 Use of SI units and their prefixes
SI base units: kg, m, s, A, K, mol (and cd). Derived units are built from them: N = kg m s^-2, J = kg m^2 s^-2, W = J s^-1, Pa = N m^-2, C = A s, V = J C^-1 = W A^-1. Prefixes: T 10^12, G 10^9, M 10^6, k 10^3, c 10^-2, m 10^-3, μ 10^-6, n 10^-9, p 10^-12, f 10^-15. Check an equation by comparing base units on both sides (homogeneity).

#### 3.1.2 Limitation of physical measurements
**Random errors** scatter readings either side of the true value (reduce by repeating and averaging); **systematic errors** shift all readings the same way (zero error, parallax, a miscalibrated instrument: reduce by calibration and technique). **Precision**: how close repeated readings are to each other; **repeatability** (same person, method, equipment) and **reproducibility** (different people or methods); **resolution**: the smallest change the instrument can show; **accuracy**: closeness to the true value. Uncertainty: absolute (±0.2 cm), fractional (0.2/5.0) or percentage (4%). The uncertainty of a reading is at least ± half the resolution; for repeated readings use half the range. Combining: adding or subtracting quantities → add absolute uncertainties; multiplying or dividing → add percentage uncertainties; raising to a power n → multiply the percentage uncertainty by n. *Example:* a cube's side is 2.0 ± 0.1 cm (5%), so its volume 8.0 cm^3 has a 15% uncertainty, ± 1.2 cm^3. On graphs, draw error bars and find the uncertainty in a gradient from the steepest and shallowest lines of best fit.

#### 3.1.3 Estimation of physical quantities
Estimate to one significant figure or an order of magnitude, stating assumptions. *Example:* the kinetic energy of a running person: 70 kg at about 8 m/s: (1/2)(70)(64) ≈ 2000 J, order 10^3 J. *Example:* the number of air molecules in a room of 5 m x 4 m x 3 m: about 60 m^3 x 2.5 x 10^25 per m^3 ≈ 10^27.

#### MS 0.1 Recognise and make use of appropriate units in calculations
Carry units through calculations and convert before substituting (e.g. 250 mA = 0.25 A, 3.0 cm^2 = 3.0 x 10^-4 m^2, 1 kWh = 3.6 x 10^6 J). Give the unit with every final answer.

#### MS 0.2 Recognise and use expressions in decimal and standard form
Standard form a x 10^n with 1 ≤ a < 10: the charge on an electron is 1.60 x 10^-19 C. Multiply by adding powers, divide by subtracting. Use the calculator's EXP or x10^x key rather than typing "x 10 ^".

#### MS 0.3 Use ratios, fractions and percentages
Ratios and percentages: efficiency = useful output / total input x 100%; percentage difference from an accepted value = |measured - accepted| / accepted x 100%. Scaling by ratio: if I ∝ 1/r^2, doubling r gives a quarter of the intensity.

#### MS 0.4 Estimate results
Before calculating, estimate the expected size of the answer; afterwards, check that the answer is physically sensible (a car cannot travel at 3000 m/s).

#### MS 0.5 Use calculators to find and use power, exponential and logarithmic functions
Use the calculator's power, exponential and log functions: e^(-t/RC), ln, log10, x^y. *Example:* e^(-2.5) = 0.0821.

#### MS 0.6 Use calculators to handle sin x, cos x and tan x in degrees or radians
Set the calculator to degrees or radians to match the question; in SHM and circular motion formulae angles are in radians (sin(ωt) with ω in rad/s).

#### MS 1.1 Use an appropriate number of significant figures
Give answers to the same number of significant figures as the least precise data (usually 2 or 3); never round intermediate values.

#### MS 1.2 Find arithmetic means
The mean of repeated readings, excluding anomalous values: (2.31 + 2.29 + 2.33 + 2.30)/4 = 2.31 s.

#### MS 1.4 Make order of magnitude calculations
An order of magnitude is the power of ten nearest the value: 4.7 x 10^5 is order 10^6 (since 4.7 > root 10 ≈ 3.16). Use orders of magnitude to compare scales (atomic radius ~10^-10 m, nuclear radius ~10^-15 m: a ratio of about 10^5).

#### MS 1.5 Identify uncertainties in measurements and use simple techniques to determine uncertainty when data are combined
Identify the largest source of uncertainty in an experiment (usually the smallest measured quantity relative to its resolution) and combine uncertainties as in 3.1.2. *Example:* ρ = RA/L with R ± 2%, diameter ± 1% (so A ± 2%) and L ± 0.5% gives ρ ± 4.5%.
