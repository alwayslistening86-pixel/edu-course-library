# S22_Practical_Skills_and_Data_Analysis - Lesson: Practical skills, graphs and data analysis

## Goal
The learner plans and evaluates experiments, identifies variables, presents and processes data, plots and interprets graphs (including linearising, gradients, intercepts and areas), handles uncertainties to reach justified conclusions, and applies algebra and graph sketching as Paper 3 Section A requires.

## How to teach this
Give the learner a table of messy data (with an anomaly) and ask what they would do before plotting it. Teach from the physics outwards: state the principle, derive or justify the equation, then do a worked example with units and sensible significant figures. Use AQA's data and formulae booklet values (e.g. g = 9.81 N kg^-1, e = 1.60 x 10^-19 C, h = 6.63 x 10^-34 J s, c = 3.00 x 10^8 m s^-1). Every numerical answer here was computed when the course was built.

#### PS1.1 Solve problems set in practical contexts
Solve problems set in unfamiliar practical contexts: identify the physics involved, decide which equations link the measured quantities, and plan the calculation. Paper 3 Section A sets such problems, often from the required practicals in new settings.

#### PS1.2 Apply scientific knowledge to practical contexts
Apply knowledge to practical contexts: e.g. explain why a thin wire is used in a resistivity experiment (larger resistance, easier to measure) or why a light gate is better than a stopwatch (removes reaction time).

#### PS2.1 Comment on experimental design and evaluate scientific methods
Comment on experimental design and evaluate methods: is the method valid (does it test the question)? Would a different technique reduce uncertainty (larger distances, more oscillations, data logger)? Suggest specific, realistic improvements.

#### PS2.2 Present data in appropriate ways
Present data appropriately: tables with column headings of quantity and unit (e.g. t / s), consistent decimal places matching the resolution; choose the right graph (line graph for continuous variables).

#### PS2.3 Evaluate results and draw conclusions with reference to measurement uncertainties and errors
Evaluate results and conclusions with reference to uncertainties: compare the percentage difference from an accepted value with the percentage uncertainty; if the difference is smaller, the result agrees within uncertainty. State whether the data support the hypothesis.

#### PS2.4 Identify variables, including those that must be controlled
Identify variables: independent (what you change), dependent (what you measure) and control variables (kept constant, and how); explain why each control matters.

#### PS3.1 Plot and interpret graphs
Plot and interpret graphs: sensible scales (plotted points covering at least half the grid), labelled axes with units, points plotted to within half a small square, a best-fit line or smooth curve (not dot-to-dot), and error bars where appropriate.

#### PS3.2 Process and analyse data using appropriate mathematical skills
Process data: means, rearranging equations, finding gradients and intercepts, and combining results to find a constant (e.g. g from a gradient of 4π^2/g).

#### PS3.3 Consider margins of error, accuracy and precision of data
Margins of error, accuracy and precision: the uncertainty in a gradient is (steepest - shallowest)/2 from worst-fit lines through the error bars; percentage uncertainty = uncertainty/value x 100%. Distinguish accuracy (closeness to the true value) from precision (spread).

#### PS4.1 Know and understand how to use a wide range of experimental and practical instruments, equipment and techniques
Instruments and techniques: vernier and micrometer callipers (check zero error), light gates and data loggers, oscilloscopes, signal generators, GM tubes, thermometers and thermistors, top pan balances; choose one whose resolution suits the measurement; take readings at eye level to avoid parallax.

#### MS 2.1 Understand and use the symbols =, <, <<, >>, >, ∝, ≈, ∆
Symbols: = (equal), < and > (less, greater), << and >> (much less, much greater), ∝ (proportional to), ≈ (approximately equal), ∆ (change in). *Example:* v << c allows the simple Doppler formula.

#### MS 2.2 Change the subject of an equation, including non-linear equations
Change the subject, including non-linear equations: from T = 2π root(l/g), l = gT^2/(4π^2); from E = (1/2)mv^2, v = root(2E/m); from V = V0 e^(-t/RC), t = RC ln(V0/V).

#### MS 2.3 Substitute numerical values into algebraic equations using appropriate units
Substitute values with consistent SI units: convert cm, g, mA, μF and so on before substituting, and check the final unit.

#### MS 2.4 Solve algebraic equations, including quadratic equations
Solve algebraic equations, including quadratics: s = ut + (1/2)at^2 for t. *Example:* 20 = 15t - 4.9t^2 has no real solution (discriminant 225 - 392 < 0), so a ball thrown up at 15 m/s never reaches 20 m; 10 = 15t - 4.9t^2 gives t = 0.98 s or 2.08 s (on the way up and down).

#### MS 3.1 Translate information between graphical, numerical and algebraic forms
Translate between graphical, numerical and algebraic forms: from an equation, predict the graph's shape; from a graph, read values and state the relationship.

#### MS 3.2 Plot two variables from experimental or other data
Plot two variables from data, choosing which is independent (x-axis) and which dependent (y-axis).

#### MS 3.3 Understand that y = mx + c represents a linear relationship
y = mx + c represents a linear relationship; linearise others to fit it: T^2 = (4π^2/g) l compares with y = mx (c = 0); V = -rI + ε with y = mx + c.

#### MS 3.4 Determine the slope and intercept of a linear graph
Determine the slope from a large triangle (covering at least half the line) and the intercept by reading or calculating c = y - mx; give units to both (the gradient has units of y/x).

#### MS 3.8 Understand the possible physical significance of the area between a curve and the x-axis and calculate or estimate it
The area between a curve and the x-axis can have physical meaning: under v-t (displacement), F-x (work done), I-t (charge), Q-V (energy), P-t (energy), g-r (potential difference per kg). For curves, estimate by counting squares or using trapezia.

#### MS 3.9 Apply the concepts underlying calculus (without requiring explicit calculus) to exponential decay
The concepts behind calculus applied to exponential decay: the rate of change is proportional to the quantity present (ΔN/Δt = -λN, ΔQ/Δt = -Q/RC), which gives exponential decay; a spreadsheet or iterative model with small Δt steps reproduces the curve.

#### MS 3.12 Sketch relationships modelled by y = k/x, y = kx^2, y = k/x^2, y = kx, y = sin x, y = cos x, y = e^(±x) and sin^2 x, cos^2 x
Sketch relationships: y = kx (line through the origin), y = kx^2 (parabola), y = k/x (hyperbola), y = k/x^2 (steeper inverse square), y = sin x and cos x, y = e^(-x) (decay towards zero) and y = e^x (growth), y = sin^2 x and cos^2 x (between 0 and 1, with double the frequency).
