# S18_Nuclear_Physics - Lesson: Nuclear physics

## Goal
The learner explains Rutherford scattering, compares alpha, beta and gamma radiation, applies the exponential decay law and half-life, relates stability to the N-Z plot and nuclear radius to nucleon number, calculates mass defect and binding energy, explains fission reactors and their safety, and carries out required practical 12.

## How to teach this
Ask the learner to predict what happens when alpha particles are fired at gold foil only a few hundred atoms thick, if atoms were 'plum puddings'. Teach from the physics outwards: state the principle, derive or justify the equation, then do a worked example with units and sensible significant figures. Use AQA's data and formulae booklet values (e.g. g = 9.81 N kg^-1, e = 1.60 x 10^-19 C, h = 6.63 x 10^-34 J s, c = 3.00 x 10^8 m s^-1). Every numerical answer here was computed when the course was built.

#### 3.8.1.1 Rutherford scattering
Rutherford (Geiger and Marsden): alpha particles fired at thin gold foil in a vacuum. Most passed straight through (the atom is mostly empty space); a few deflected by large angles, and about 1 in 8000 by more than 90° (a small, dense, positively charged nucleus). This replaced the plum pudding model with the nuclear model. Closest approach: when the alpha stops momentarily, its kinetic energy equals the electric potential energy Qq/(4πε0 r), giving an upper limit for the nuclear radius.

#### 3.8.1.2 Alpha, beta and gamma radiation
Alpha (helium nucleus): strongly ionising, range a few cm in air, stopped by paper. Beta-minus (fast electron): moderately ionising, range about 1 m in air, stopped by a few mm of aluminium. Gamma (EM wave): weakly ionising, range long, reduced by several cm of lead. Identify them by absorption experiments with a GM tube, or by deflection in magnetic fields. Gamma from a point source obeys the inverse-square law: I = k/x^2 (corrected for background). Background radiation (radon gas, rocks, cosmic rays, medical sources) must be subtracted. Handling: tongs, distance, shielding, short exposure. Applications: thickness monitoring (beta), smoke detectors (alpha), tracers (gamma).

#### 3.8.1.3 Radioactive decay
Decay is random (a nucleus cannot be predicted) but a large number follow ΔN/Δt = -λN, where λ is the decay constant (the probability of decay per unit time). N = N0 e^(-λt); activity A = λN = A0 e^(-λt) (becquerel, Bq). Half-life T_1/2 = ln 2/λ. *Example:* carbon-14 has T_1/2 = 5730 years; wood with 30% of the living activity is 9950 years old. Uses: carbon dating, choosing isotopes by half-life for medical diagnosis (short half-life, such as technetium-99m at 6 h), and the storage of radioactive waste.

#### 3.8.1.4 Nuclear instability
A graph of neutron number N against proton number Z shows the band of stability: light stable nuclei have N ≈ Z, heavier ones need more neutrons. Nuclei above the band (too many neutrons) decay by β-; below it (too many protons) by β+ or electron capture; very heavy ones by α. Changes: α: Z - 2, A - 4; β-: Z + 1; β+: Z - 1. After decay the daughter nucleus may be left in an excited state and emit a gamma photon to reach its ground state; nuclear energy-level diagrams show these transitions. Technetium-99m is a metastable excited state that emits only gamma, suiting medical imaging.

#### 3.8.1.5 Nuclear radius
Nuclear radius from electron diffraction: high-energy electrons diffracted by nuclei show a first minimum at sin θ ≈ 0.61λ/R. Results show R = R0 A^(1/3), with R0 ≈ 1.2 fm (1.2 x 10^-15 m); so nuclear volume ∝ A and density is constant (about 2.3 x 10^17 kg/m^3). *Example:* for gold-197, R = 1.2 x 197^(1/3) = 7.0 fm. Also estimate R from the closest approach of alpha particles (an upper limit).

#### 3.8.1.6 Mass and energy
E = mc^2: 1 u = 1.661 x 10^-27 kg, equivalent to 931.5 MeV. Mass defect: the mass of a nucleus is less than the total mass of its separate nucleons; the binding energy is the energy equivalent of this defect (the energy needed to separate the nucleus completely). Binding energy per nucleon peaks near iron-56 (about 8.8 MeV), so fusion of light nuclei and fission of heavy nuclei both release energy. *Example:* helium-4: the mass defect is 2(1.00728) + 2(1.00867) - 4.00151 = 0.03039 u, binding energy 28.3 MeV (7.08 MeV per nucleon).

#### 3.8.1.7 Induced fission
Induced fission: a thermal (slow) neutron is absorbed by uranium-235, which splits into two daughter nuclei and releases 2-3 neutrons and energy; those neutrons can cause further fissions (a chain reaction). Critical mass: the minimum mass for a self-sustaining chain reaction. In a thermal reactor: the moderator (water or graphite) slows neutrons by elastic collisions (best with light nuclei similar in mass to a neutron); control rods (boron, cadmium) absorb neutrons so that, on average, each fission causes exactly one further fission; the coolant (water, carbon dioxide) removes heat to the turbines.

#### 3.8.1.8 Safety aspects
Safety: fuel rods are handled remotely; the reactor is inside thick concrete; spent fuel is highly radioactive and hot, cooled in ponds, then stored and vitrified; low-level waste (clothing, tools) needs less shielding. Benefits (low-carbon, reliable supply) must be weighed against the risks (accidents, long-term waste), a judgement that involves society as well as science.

#### RP12 Required practical 12: the inverse-square law for gamma radiation
**Method:** measure background count; place a sealed gamma source at measured distances d from a GM tube; record counts over fixed times and correct for background. The effective source position is uncertain, so write x = d + c. **Analysis:** plot 1/root(corrected count rate) against d: a straight line whose x-intercept is -c confirms the inverse-square law. Safety: tongs, keep the source in its box when not in use, keep distance.

#### MS 1.3 Understand simple probability
Probability: each nucleus has the same chance of decaying in a given time, independent of its history, so λΔt is the probability that one nucleus decays in a short time Δt. Dice or coin simulations model decay: throwing 1000 dice and removing the sixes gives roughly 1/6 decay per throw and an exponential pattern.
