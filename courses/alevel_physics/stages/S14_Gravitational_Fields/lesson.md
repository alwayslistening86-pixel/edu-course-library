# S14_Gravitational_Fields - Lesson: Fields and gravitational fields

## Goal
The learner describes fields and field lines, applies Newton's law of gravitation, gravitational field strength and potential in radial and uniform fields, and analyses circular orbits, escape velocity and geostationary satellites.

## How to teach this
Ask why astronauts on the International Space Station float, when gravity there is still about 90% of its surface value. Teach from the physics outwards: state the principle, derive or justify the equation, then do a worked example with units and sensible significant figures. Use AQA's data and formulae booklet values (e.g. g = 9.81 N kg^-1, e = 1.60 x 10^-19 C, h = 6.63 x 10^-34 J s, c = 3.00 x 10^8 m s^-1). #### 3.7.1 Fields
A force field is a region in which a body experiences a non-contact force. Gravitational fields act on mass (always attractive); electric fields act on charge (attract or repel). Both have inverse-square laws for point sources and are represented by field lines (direction of force) and equipotentials (perpendicular to field lines). Similarities and differences are compared in S15.

#### 3.7.2.1 Newton's law of gravitation
Newton's law: F = Gm1m2/r^2 between point masses (or spheres, measuring r between centres), with G = 6.67 x 10^-11 N m^2 kg^-2. *Example:* Earth and Moon (7.35 x 10^22 kg, 3.84 x 10^8 m apart): F = 1.98 x 10^20 N.

#### 3.7.2.2 Gravitational field strength
Gravitational field strength g = F/m (N/kg): the force per unit mass. Radial field of a point or spherical mass: g = GM/r^2 (magnitude). Near the Earth's surface the field is approximately uniform. *Example:* at the surface, GM_E/R_E^2 = 9.81 N/kg; at the height of the ISS (400 km) it is 8.69 N/kg.

#### 3.7.2.3 Gravitational potential
Gravitational potential V at a point is the work done per unit mass in bringing a small mass from infinity to that point: V = -GM/r (zero at infinity, negative everywhere else). Work done moving mass m: ΔW = mΔV. Equipotentials: surfaces of equal V; no work is done moving along one. g = -ΔV/Δr: field strength is minus the potential gradient; the area under a g-r graph gives ΔV. Graphs of g and V against r (1/r^2 and -1/r). *Example:* the energy to lift a 1000 kg satellite from the surface to 400 km: m(GM/R_E - GM/(R_E + h)) = 3.69 x 10^9 J.

#### 3.7.2.4 Orbits of planets and satellites
For a circular orbit, gravity provides the centripetal force: GMm/r^2 = mv^2/r, so v = root(GM/r) and T^2 = (4π^2/GM) r^3 (Kepler's third law, T^2 ∝ r^3). Energy: kinetic energy GMm/2r, potential energy -GMm/r, total -GMm/2r, so moving to a higher orbit needs energy but gives a lower speed. Escape velocity: (1/2)mv^2 = GMm/R gives v = root(2GM/R) = 11.2 km/s from Earth. Synchronous orbits have the same period as the planet's rotation; a geostationary satellite orbits above the equator, west to east, every 24 h at r = 4.22 x 10^7 m (height about 36 000 km), staying above one point (used for communications). Low orbits (a few hundred km) suit imaging and weather monitoring and have periods of about 90 minutes.
