#### 3.9.2.1 Classification by luminosity
Apparent magnitude m measures brightness as seen from Earth on the Hipparcos (Hipparchus) scale: the brightest stars about 1 and the faintest visible 6; lower numbers are brighter, and very bright objects are negative. A difference of 5 magnitudes is an intensity ratio of 100, so 1 magnitude is a factor of 100^(1/5) ≈ 2.51. Brightness is a subjective scale; intensity is the objective quantity.

#### 3.9.2.2 Absolute magnitude, M
Distance units: the astronomical unit (1 AU, the mean Earth-Sun distance, 1.50 x 10^11 m), the light year (9.46 x 10^15 m) and the parsec (the distance at which 1 AU subtends 1 arcsecond: 3.08 x 10^16 m ≈ 3.26 ly). Absolute magnitude M is the apparent magnitude a star would have at 10 pc: m - M = 5 log(d/10), d in parsecs. *Example:* a star with m = 8.0 and M = 3.0 is at d = 10 x 10^(5/5) = 100 pc.

#### 3.9.2.3 Classification by temperature, black-body radiation
Stars approximate black bodies. Wien's displacement law: λ_max T = 2.9 x 10^-3 m K, so hotter stars peak at shorter wavelengths. Stefan's law: P = σAT^4, with σ = 5.67 x 10^-8 W m^-2 K^-4 and A = 4πr^2. Intensity at distance d: I = P/(4πd^2). *Example:* the Sun (T = 5800 K) peaks at 500 nm; a star twice the Sun's radius at half its temperature has 0.25 of its power.

#### 3.9.2.4 Principles of the use of stellar spectral classes
Spectral classes (hottest to coolest) O, B, A, F, G, K, M: O 25 000-50 000 K blue (He+, He, H lines); B 11 000-25 000 K blue (He, H); A 7500-11 000 K blue-white (H strongest, ionised metals); F 6000-7500 K white (ionised metals); G 5000-6000 K yellow-white (ionised and neutral metals); K 3500-5000 K orange (neutral metals); M below 3500 K red (neutral atoms, TiO molecules). Hydrogen Balmer absorption lines need atoms in the n = 2 state: in cooler stars few are excited, and in the hottest most are ionised, so the lines are strongest in class A.

#### 3.9.2.5 The Hertzsprung-Russell (HR) diagram
The Hertzsprung-Russell diagram plots absolute magnitude (from +15 at the bottom to -10 at the top) against temperature or spectral class (hot on the left, cool on the right). Regions: the main sequence (diagonal band), red giants and supergiants (upper right), white dwarfs (lower left). Evolution of a Sun-like star: protostar → main sequence (hydrogen fusion in the core) → red giant (core hydrogen exhausted, shell burning, helium fusion) → white dwarf (the outer layers form a planetary nebula; the hot core cools). Trace this path on the HR diagram.

#### 3.9.2.6 Supernovae, neutron stars and black holes
Supernovae: a rapid increase in absolute magnitude. Type Ia (a white dwarf accreting mass from a companion) reach a consistent peak absolute magnitude (about -19.3), so they are standard candles for measuring distances; they showed that the expansion of the universe is accelerating (evidence for dark energy), which is controversial as dark energy's nature is unknown. Light curves show the rise and decline. Neutron stars: the collapsed core of a massive star, extremely dense (about the density of nuclear matter). Black holes: the escape velocity at the event horizon exceeds c; the Schwarzschild radius R_s = 2GM/c^2. Supermassive black holes lie at the centres of most galaxies. Gamma-ray bursts come from the collapse of supergiants to form neutron stars or black holes. *Example:* for a star of 10 solar masses (M_sun = 1.99 x 10^30 kg), R_s = 29.5 km.
