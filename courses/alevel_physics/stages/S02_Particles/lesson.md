# S02_Particles - Lesson: Particles, antiparticles and interactions

## Goal
The learner describes atomic and nuclear structure, specific charge, stability and decay (including neutrinos), particles and antiparticles, pair production and annihilation, exchange particles and Feynman diagrams, and classifies hadrons and leptons using quark composition and conservation laws.

## How to teach this
Ask what force holds protons together in a nucleus when they repel each other electrically, and over what range it acts. Teach from the physics outwards: state the principle, derive or justify the equation, then do a worked example with units and sensible significant figures. Use AQA's data and formulae booklet values (e.g. g = 9.81 N kg^-1, e = 1.60 x 10^-19 C, h = 6.63 x 10^-34 J s, c = 3.00 x 10^8 m s^-1). Every numerical answer here was computed when the course was built.

#### 3.2.1.1 Constituents of the atom
Proton (charge +e, mass 1.67 x 10^-27 kg), neutron (0, 1.67 x 10^-27 kg), electron (-e, 9.11 x 10^-31 kg). Nucleon number A and proton number Z: notation A over Z X; isotopes have the same Z but different A. **Specific charge** = charge/mass: proton 9.56 x 10^7 C kg^-1; electron 1.76 x 10^11 C kg^-1; for a nucleus, Ze/(mass). *Example:* the specific charge of a 12-C nucleus: 6e/(12 x 1.66 x 10^-27) = 4.82 x 10^7 C kg^-1.

#### 3.2.1.2 Stable and unstable nuclei
The strong nuclear force holds nucleons together: attractive from about 0.5 fm to 3 fm, repulsive below about 0.5 fm, negligible beyond 3 fm. Unstable nuclei decay. **Alpha decay** (heavy nuclei): A decreases by 4, Z by 2. **Beta-minus decay** (neutron-rich nuclei): n → p + e^- + ν̄_e, so Z increases by 1. The electron antineutrino was hypothesised to explain the continuous range of beta-particle energies (conserving energy and momentum).

#### 3.2.1.3 Particles, antiparticles and photons
Every particle has an antiparticle with the same mass and rest energy but opposite charge (and other quantum numbers): positron, antiproton, antineutron, antineutrino. Photon energy E = hf = hc/λ. **Pair production**: a photon of energy at least 2 x the rest energy of a particle creates a particle-antiparticle pair (e.g. e^-/e^+ needs at least 1.022 MeV). **Annihilation**: a particle and antiparticle meet and produce two photons (for momentum conservation), each with at least the rest energy of one particle. *Example:* minimum photon frequency for electron-positron pair production: 2 x 0.511 MeV = 1.022 x 1.60 x 10^-13 J, f = E/h = 2.47 x 10^20 Hz.

#### 3.2.1.4 Particle interactions
Four fundamental interactions: gravity, electromagnetic, weak and strong. Forces act via exchange particles: virtual photons (electromagnetic), W+ and W- bosons (weak), gluons between quarks, and pions between nucleons (the course's model of the strong nuclear force). Feynman diagrams show the particles and the exchange: in beta-minus decay n → p + e^- + ν̄_e, a W^- is emitted by the neutron; in beta-plus decay p → n + e^+ + ν_e, a W^+ is emitted by the proton; in electron capture p + e^- → n + ν_e, a W^+ passes from the proton to the electron (equivalently a W^- from the electron to the proton); an electron-proton collision can produce the same outcome. Draw time running upwards, with the incoming particles at the bottom.

#### 3.2.1.5 Classification of particles
**Hadrons** feel the strong force: **baryons** (three quarks: proton, neutron; the proton is the only stable baryon; baryon number B = +1) and **mesons** (quark-antiquark: pions π, kaons K; B = 0). Kaons decay into pions. **Leptons** don't feel the strong force: electron, muon, neutrinos (lepton numbers L_e and L_μ conserved separately). The muon decays into an electron. Cosmic rays produce pions and kaons.

#### 3.2.1.6 Quarks and antiquarks
Quarks: up (charge +2/3, B = 1/3), down (-1/3, B = 1/3), strange (-1/3, B = 1/3, strangeness S = -1). Proton uud, neutron udd; π+ = ud̄, π- = dū, π0 = uū or dd̄; K+ = us̄, K- = ūs, K0 = ds̄. In beta-minus decay a down quark becomes an up quark (d → u + e^- + ν̄_e) via the weak interaction.

#### 3.2.1.7 Applications of conservation laws
In every interaction, charge, baryon number and lepton number (L_e, L_μ separately) are conserved, as are energy and momentum. Strangeness is conserved in strong interactions but can change by ±1 (or 0) in weak interactions. *Example:* is p + p → p + n + π+ possible? Q: 2 = 1 + 0 + 1 ✓; B: 2 = 1 + 1 + 0 ✓; L: 0 = 0 ✓: allowed. *Example:* μ- → e- + ν̄_e + ν_μ: L_μ: 1 = 0 + 0 + 1 ✓, L_e: 0 = 1 - 1 + 0 ✓.
