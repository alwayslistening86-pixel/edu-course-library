# S19_Astrophysics_Telescopes - Lesson: Astrophysics: telescopes

## Goal
The learner explains the refracting telescope in normal adjustment and its angular magnification, compares reflecting and refracting telescopes including aberrations, compares radio, infrared, ultraviolet and X-ray telescopes, and applies the Rayleigh criterion, collecting power and CCD performance.

## How to teach this
Ask why the world's largest optical telescopes are mirrors, not lenses. Teach from the physics outwards: state the principle, derive or justify the equation, then do a worked example with units and sensible significant figures. Use AQA's data and formulae booklet values (e.g. g = 9.81 N kg^-1, e = 1.60 x 10^-19 C, h = 6.63 x 10^-34 J s, c = 3.00 x 10^8 m s^-1). #### 3.9.1.1 Astronomical telescope consisting of two converging lenses
The astronomical refractor uses an objective lens (long focal length f_o) and an eyepiece (short f_e). In normal adjustment the focal points coincide, the lenses are f_o + f_e apart, and the final image is at infinity. Ray diagram: parallel rays from a star meet at the principal focus of the objective, then emerge parallel from the eyepiece at a larger angle. Angular magnification M = angle subtended by image at the eye / angle subtended by object at the unaided eye = f_o/f_e. *Example:* f_o = 1.20 m, f_e = 0.040 m: M = 30, length 1.24 m.

#### 3.9.1.2 Reflecting telescopes
The Cassegrain reflector: a concave paraboloidal primary mirror, a convex secondary mirror, and light passing through a hole in the primary to the eyepiece. Spherical aberration: a spherical mirror or lens brings edge and centre rays to different foci (fixed by a parabolic mirror). Chromatic aberration: a lens refracts different wavelengths by different amounts, so colours focus at different points (mirrors do not have this). Refractors' disadvantages: large lenses are heavy, sag under their own weight, absorb some light, may contain bubbles or flaws, and suffer chromatic aberration; mirrors can be supported from behind, and made in segments.

#### 3.9.1.3 Single dish radio telescopes, I-R, U-V and X-ray telescopes
Radio telescopes (a large parabolic dish, often on the ground): similar principle to optical reflectors but λ is much longer, so they need very large dishes (or arrays) for resolving power, and the dish surface need only be accurate to a fraction of λ. Infrared, ultraviolet and X-ray telescopes are placed high or in space because the atmosphere absorbs these wavelengths. Infrared detectors must be cooled to reduce their own thermal emission; X-rays pass through ordinary mirrors, so X-ray telescopes use grazing-incidence mirrors. Compare structure, positioning and use.

#### 3.9.1.4 Advantages of large diameter telescopes
Resolving power: the Rayleigh criterion θ ≈ λ/D (radians): two objects are just resolved when the central maximum of one lies on the first minimum of the other; a larger D resolves finer detail. Collecting power ∝ D^2 (area of the aperture), so fainter objects can be seen. CCDs versus the eye: quantum efficiency (about 80% or more vs about 1%), a wider spectral range, a finer resolution and image storage/processing, and long exposures that add up light. *Example:* a 2.4 m mirror at 550 nm resolves 2.29 x 10^-7 rad; a 10 m mirror collects 17 times as much light.
