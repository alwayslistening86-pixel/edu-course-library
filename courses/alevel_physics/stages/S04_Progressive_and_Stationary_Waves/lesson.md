# S04_Progressive_and_Stationary_Waves - Lesson: Progressive and stationary waves

## Goal
The learner describes progressive waves (amplitude, frequency, wavelength, speed, phase), compares longitudinal and transverse waves including polarisation, explains superposition and stationary waves with nodes, antinodes and harmonics, and carries out and analyses required practical 1.

## Syllabus items taught here
- 3.3.1.1 - Progressive waves
- 3.3.1.2 - Longitudinal and transverse waves
- 3.3.1.3 - Principle of superposition of waves and formation of stationary waves
- RP1 - Required practical 1: stationary waves on a string (frequency against length, tension and mass per unit length)

## How to teach this
Ask how a guitar string that is fixed at both ends can still carry a wave. Teach from the physics outwards: state the principle, derive or justify the equation, then do a worked example with units and sensible significant figures. Use AQA's data and formulae booklet values (e.g. g = 9.81 N kg^-1, e = 1.60 x 10^-19 C, h = 6.63 x 10^-34 J s, c = 3.00 x 10^8 m s^-1). #### 3.3.1.1 Progressive waves
A progressive wave transfers energy without transferring matter. Amplitude, wavelength λ, frequency f, period T = 1/f, speed c = fλ. **Phase difference** between two points Δx apart: 2πΔx/λ (radians) or 360°Δx/λ. *Example:* sound of 256 Hz in air at 340 m/s: λ = 1.328 m; points 0.33 m apart differ in phase by 1.56 rad.

#### 3.3.1.2 Longitudinal and transverse waves
Transverse: oscillations perpendicular to the direction of travel (all EM waves, waves on strings). Longitudinal: oscillations parallel to the direction of travel, with compressions and rarefactions (sound). Only transverse waves can be **polarised** (restricted to one plane). Evidence: two polarising filters crossed at 90° block light. Applications: Polaroid sunglasses reduce glare from reflected (partly polarised) light; aligning TV and radio aerials with the transmitter's polarisation.

#### 3.3.1.3 Principle of superposition of waves and formation of stationary waves
Superposition: where waves meet, the resultant displacement is the sum of the individual displacements. A **stationary wave** forms when two waves of the same frequency and amplitude travel in opposite directions (e.g. a wave reflected back along a string). Nodes (zero amplitude) are λ/2 apart, with antinodes between. Unlike a progressive wave, all points between adjacent nodes are in phase and energy is not transferred. On a string of length L fixed at both ends, the first harmonic has λ = 2L, f1 = (1/2L) root(T/μ), where T is the tension and μ the mass per unit length; the nth harmonic has frequency nf1. Stationary waves also form with microwaves (reflected from a metal plate) and sound in pipes.

#### RP1 Required practical 1: stationary waves on a string (frequency against length, tension and mass per unit length)
**Method:** a string over a pulley with hanging masses (tension T = mg) is driven by a vibration generator connected to a signal generator; adjust the frequency until the first harmonic appears. Vary L (moving the bridge) at fixed T, then vary T at fixed L, then use strings of different μ (found by weighing a measured length). **Analysis:** f = (1/2L) root(T/μ): plot f against 1/L (straight line through the origin, gradient (1/2) root(T/μ)), or f^2 against T. **Improving accuracy:** measure L between the nodes with a metre rule, use a stroboscope to judge the maximum amplitude, and repeat. *Example:* L = 0.80 m, T = 4.9 N, μ = 5.0 x 10^-4 kg m^-1: f1 = 61.9 Hz.
