# S04_Progressive_and_Stationary_Waves - Test: Progressive and stationary waves

## How to run this
A real checkpoint in the style of AQA's papers: structured questions with marks shown (and some multiple choice). Give the whole test at once, with no hints; the learner shows working and may use a calculator and the data booklet. Then mark against the mark schemes below and this stage's entry in `rubric.json`.

## Test items
1. A string of length 0.65 m and mass per unit length 1.2 x 10^-3 kg m^-1 is under a tension of 60 N. Calculate its first harmonic frequency, and the frequency of the third harmonic. [3 marks]
2. State three differences between a stationary wave and a progressive wave. [3 marks]
3. Explain why sound waves cannot be polarised, and give one application of polarisation. [2 marks]
4. In required practical 1, a student doubles the tension in the string. By what factor does the first harmonic frequency change? Explain how to show the relationship graphically. [3 marks]
5. Microwaves reflected from a metal plate form a stationary wave. A detector finds the distance between successive minima is 1.4 cm. Calculate the frequency of the microwaves. [2 marks]

## Answer key (for the tutor only)
1. [3] M1 f = (1/2L) root(T/μ); A1 172 Hz; A1 third harmonic 516 Hz.
2. [3] B1 a stationary wave doesn't transfer energy; B1 its amplitude varies with position (nodes and antinodes), whereas a progressive wave's is the same everywhere; B1 points between adjacent nodes are in phase, whereas phase varies continuously along a progressive wave.
3. [2] B1 sound is longitudinal; oscillations are along the direction of travel, so there is no plane to restrict; B1 e.g. Polaroid sunglasses reducing glare, or aerial alignment.
4. [3] B1 f ∝ root T, so it increases by a factor of root 2 ≈ 1.41; B1 plot f^2 against T; B1 a straight line through the origin confirms f ∝ root T (gradient 1/(4L^2 μ)).
5. [2] M1 λ = 2.8 cm; A1 f = c/λ = 1.07 x 10^10 Hz.
