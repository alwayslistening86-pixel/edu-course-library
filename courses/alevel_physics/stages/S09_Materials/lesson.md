# S09_Materials - Lesson: Materials

## Goal
The learner uses density, Hooke's law, stress, strain and the Young modulus, interprets stress-strain and force-extension graphs (elastic and plastic behaviour, breaking stress, brittle materials), calculates elastic strain energy, and carries out required practical 4.

## Syllabus items taught here
- 3.4.2.1 - Bulk properties of solids
- 3.4.2.2 - The Young modulus
- RP4 - Required practical 4: the Young modulus by a simple method

## How to teach this
Ask why a steel wire can hold a car but stretches permanently if you overload it. Teach from the physics outwards: state the principle, derive or justify the equation, then do a worked example with units and sensible significant figures. Use AQA's data and formulae booklet values (e.g. g = 9.81 N kg^-1, e = 1.60 x 10^-19 C, h = 6.63 x 10^-34 J s, c = 3.00 x 10^8 m s^-1). Every numerical answer here was computed when the course was built.

#### 3.4.2.1 Bulk properties of solids
Density ρ = m/V. Hooke's law: F = kΔL, up to the limit of proportionality; springs in series have 1/k = 1/k1 + 1/k2, in parallel k = k1 + k2. **Tensile stress** = F/A; **tensile strain** = ΔL/L; the **breaking stress** is the stress at fracture. Elastic deformation is reversible; plastic deformation is permanent (beyond the elastic limit). Brittle materials (glass, cast iron) fracture with little plastic deformation. Loading and unloading curves: a metal's unloading line is parallel to the loading line, and the area between them is the energy for permanent deformation. **Elastic strain energy** = (1/2)FΔL = the area under a force-extension graph. *Example:* a spring of k = 40 N m^-1 stretched 0.15 m stores 0.45 J. Energy conversions: a spring launching a mass converts elastic energy to kinetic energy; car crumple zones absorb energy through plastic deformation.

#### 3.4.2.2 The Young modulus
Young modulus E = tensile stress / tensile strain = FL/(AΔL) (Pa). From a stress-strain graph, E is the gradient of the linear section. *Example:* a steel wire 2.0 m long, diameter 0.50 mm, stretches 2.0 mm under 40 N: E = (40 x 2.0)/(π(0.25 x 10^-3)^2 x 2.0 x 10^-3) = 2.04 x 10^11 Pa.

#### RP4 Required practical 4: the Young modulus by a simple method
**Method:** a long thin wire (≈2 m) is clamped at one end and passes over a pulley with a marker (or uses a vernier on a reference wire, Searle's apparatus) to reduce errors from the support sagging. Measure the diameter in several places and orientations with a micrometer, and the original length with a metre rule. Add masses in steps, recording extensions; also unload to check for elastic behaviour. **Analysis:** plot stress against strain (or F against ΔL): E = gradient (or gradient x L/A). **Safety:** wear goggles (the wire may snap), keep feet away from hanging masses. The biggest uncertainties are usually the extension and the diameter.
