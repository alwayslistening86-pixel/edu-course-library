# S11_Advanced_Financial_Management - Lesson: Advanced financial management

## Goal
The learner extends investment appraisal with sensitivity analysis and risk-adjusted discount rates, states the Modigliani-Miller capital structure propositions (with and without tax), explains dividend policy theory, and appraises a merger/acquisition using synergy and valuation -- matching Advanced Financial Accounting and Management Accounting (B352), Part I: advanced financial management.

## Syllabus items taught here
- B352-1 - Sensitivity analysis and risk-adjusted discount rates
- B352-2 - Modigliani-Miller capital structure propositions
- B352-3 - Dividend policy theory
- B352-4 - Mergers and acquisitions: synergy and valuation

## How to teach this
Ask: if capital structure (the mix of debt and equity) genuinely didn't affect a company's overall value, as Modigliani and Miller controversially argued in a perfect market, why do real companies still care so much about their gearing level? Teach each topic by building on what A-level Accounting and A-level Mathematics already gave the learner: name the A-level idea it extends (e.g. A-level Accounting's basic financial statements, before IAS 2/IAS 16/IFRS 15/IFRS 16 formalise specific standards), then show the IFRS-specific technical treatment or quantitative technique degree-level accounting/finance adds. Work every numerical example with the learner predicting a step before it is shown; every figure in this course was computed with Python when the course was built. UK tax figures (S13) are the 2026/27 tax year; always check HMRC's current published rates before relying on a real-world tax calculation, since UK tax rates/thresholds/allowances change at least annually.

#### B352-1 Sensitivity analysis and risk-adjusted discount rates
**Sensitivity analysis** tests how much a single input (e.g. sales volume, discount rate, initial cost) would have to change before a project's NPV falls to zero, identifying which assumptions the decision is most exposed to. *Worked example*: a project has NPV = £45,000 at a cost of capital of 10%, and the same cash flows discounted at 10% give a present value of inflows of £245,000: the NPV would fall to zero if inflows fell by 18.4% (45,000/245,000), so a sales/cost variable driving that much of a swing in cash inflows is the project's most sensitive assumption. A **risk-adjusted discount rate** applies a higher discount rate to a riskier project (or cash flow stream) than the company's normal WACC, reflecting that riskier cash flows should be valued less highly per pound of expected return -- consistent with CAPM's logic that higher (systematic) risk demands a higher required return.

#### B352-2 Modigliani-Miller capital structure propositions
**Modigliani-Miller (MM) capital structure theory**: in a perfect market with **no tax**, MM Proposition I states a company's overall value (and WACC) is independent of its capital structure -- the way a company's cash flows are sliced between debt and equity holders does not change the total value of those cash flows, since investors can replicate any capital structure themselves (homemade leverage). **With corporation tax**, MM Proposition I (with tax) shows that because debt interest is tax-deductible, gearing up creates a genuine **tax shield** that raises company value: value of a geared company = value of an equivalent ungeared company + (tax rate x market value of debt). *Worked example*: an ungeared company is valued at £4,000,000; it takes on £1,000,000 of debt at a 25% tax rate: value of the geared company (MM with tax) = 4,000,000 + (0.25 x 1,000,000) = £4,250,000. In practice, MM's no-tax result is a theoretical benchmark; real capital structure decisions also weigh bankruptcy/financial distress costs and agency costs against the tax shield, which is why companies do not simply gear up to 100% debt.

#### B352-3 Dividend policy theory
**Dividend policy theories**: MM's dividend irrelevance theory (in a perfect market with no taxes or transaction costs) argues a company's dividend policy does not affect shareholder wealth, since a shareholder wanting cash can simply sell some shares ('homemade dividends'), and one preferring reinvestment can use a dividend to buy more shares -- value comes from the company's investment decisions, not how earnings are split between dividends and retention. Real-world qualifications include the **clientele effect** (different investors prefer different payout policies, e.g. for tax or income reasons, so a stable, well-signalled policy attracts and retains a matching investor base -- and abrupt, unexplained changes can prompt costly investor turnover), **signalling** (a dividend change is often read by the market as management's genuine view of future prospects, since cutting a dividend is a costly, hard-to-fake signal of trouble), and taxation/transaction cost differences between dividend income and capital gains that can make policy matter to particular investors even if it is theoretically irrelevant to firm value overall.

#### B352-4 Mergers and acquisitions: synergy and valuation
**Mergers and acquisitions**: the case for a merger typically rests on **synergy** (the combined entity being worth more than the sum of the two separate entities, e.g. through cost savings, revenue enhancement, or complementary capabilities) -- value is only created for the acquirer's shareholders if the synergy gain exceeds any premium paid over the target's standalone (pre-bid) value. *Worked example*: Target's standalone value is £6,000,000; the Acquirer pays £7,500,000 (a premium of £1,500,000); the combined entity, post-merger, is valued at £15,000,000 versus the Acquirer's own standalone value of £7,000,000: synergy gained = 15,000,000 - (7,000,000+6,000,000) = £2,000,000; net value created for the Acquirer's shareholders = synergy - premium paid = 2,000,000 - 1,500,000 = £500,000, i.e. the deal still creates value for the acquirer even after the premium, but only because the synergy achieved exceeds the premium paid -- a deal with the same premium but no real synergy destroys value for the acquirer's own shareholders.
