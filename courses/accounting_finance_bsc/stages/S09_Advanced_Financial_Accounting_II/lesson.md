# S09_Advanced_Financial_Accounting_II - Lesson: Advanced financial accounting II

## Goal
The learner accounts for an associate using the equity method (IAS 28), translates a foreign subsidiary's results using the closing/average rate method (IAS 21), classifies and measures a simple financial instrument (IFRS 9), and calculates basic and diluted earnings per share (IAS 33) -- matching Advanced Financial Accounting (B350), Part II.

## Syllabus items taught here
- B350-5 - Associates: the equity method (IAS 28)
- B350-6 - Foreign currency translation (IAS 21)
- B350-7 - Financial instruments (IFRS 9): classification and amortised cost
- B350-8 - Earnings per share, basic and diluted (IAS 33)

## How to teach this
Ask: why does an investor with only significant influence (not control) over another company still adjust the carrying value of that investment for a share of its profits every year, rather than just leaving it at original cost? Teach each topic by building on what A-level Accounting and A-level Mathematics already gave the learner: name the A-level idea it extends (e.g. A-level Accounting's basic financial statements, before IAS 2/IAS 16/IFRS 15/IFRS 16 formalise specific standards), then show the IFRS-specific technical treatment or quantitative technique degree-level accounting/finance adds. Work every numerical example with the learner predicting a step before it is shown; every figure in this course was computed with Python when the course was built. UK tax figures (S13) are the 2026/27 tax year; always check HMRC's current published rates before relying on a real-world tax calculation, since UK tax rates/thresholds/allowances change at least annually.

#### B350-5 Associates: the equity method (IAS 28)
**IAS 28 Investments in Associates**: an **associate** (significant influence, typically 20-50% of voting rights, but not control) is accounted for using the **equity method**: the investment starts at cost, and its carrying amount is then increased each year by the investor's share of the associate's profit (recognised as investment income in the investor's own profit or loss, not merely disclosed) and decreased by the investor's share of any dividends received (since a dividend is a return of value already recognised, not additional income) and by its share of any losses. Unlike a subsidiary, an associate's revenue/expenses are **not** added line by line to the investor's own statements -- only a single share-of-profit line and a single investment carrying-amount line appear. *Worked example*: Parent owns 30% of Associate A, investment initially £150,000; Associate's profit for the year is £40,000 (parent's share = 30% x 40,000 = £12,000); Associate pays total dividends of £10,000 (parent's share received = 30% x 10,000 = £3,000): closing investment carrying amount = 150,000 + 12,000 - 3,000 = £159,000.

#### B350-6 Foreign currency translation (IAS 21)
**IAS 21 The Effects of Changes in Foreign Exchange Rates**: a foreign subsidiary's financial statements are translated into the group's presentation currency using the **closing rate method**: assets and liabilities at the **closing rate** (the rate on the reporting date), income and expenses typically at the **average rate** for the period (as a practical approximation to the rate on each transaction date), and share capital at the **historical rate** (the rate when it was issued). The resulting **exchange difference** (arising because the closing rate used for net assets differs from the rates used to translate the profit for the year and opening net assets) is recognised in **other comprehensive income**, not profit or loss, since it is a translation effect, not a trading gain or loss. *Worked example*: a foreign subsidiary's revenue for the year is $1,200,000, translated at an average rate of $1.20/£: £1,000,000; its net assets at the year end are $2,000,000, translated at the closing rate of $1.25/£: £1,600,000.

#### B350-7 Financial instruments (IFRS 9): classification and amortised cost
**IFRS 9 Financial Instruments** classifies a financial asset based on the entity's business model and the contractual cash flow characteristics (the 'solely payments of principal and interest' or SPPI test): held to collect contractual cash flows and cash flows are SPPI -> measured at **amortised cost**; held both to collect contractual cash flows and to sell, and SPPI -> **fair value through other comprehensive income (FVOCI)**; anything else (e.g. held for trading, or cash flows that are not SPPI, such as an equity investment) -> **fair value through profit or loss (FVTPL)**. A debt instrument at amortised cost uses the **effective interest method**: interest income/expense each period = opening carrying amount x the effective interest rate, with any difference from the coupon actually paid/received added to (or deducted from) the carrying amount. *Worked example*: a bond is issued for proceeds of £95,000 (a discount to its £100,000 nominal value), coupon 5% (£5,000 a year), effective interest rate 6.5%: year 1 finance cost = 95,000 x 6.5% = £6,175; amount added to the carrying amount (finance cost less coupon paid) = 6,175 - 5,000 = £1,175; closing carrying amount = 95,000 + 1,175 = £96,175.

#### B350-8 Earnings per share, basic and diluted (IAS 33)
**IAS 33 Earnings per Share**: **basic EPS** = profit attributable to ordinary shareholders / weighted average number of ordinary shares in issue during the period. **Diluted EPS** adjusts both the numerator and denominator for the effect of **potential ordinary shares** (e.g. convertible debt, share options) that would dilute EPS if converted/exercised: add back the post-tax finance cost saved on conversion to profit, and add the additional shares that would be issued to the share count -- diluted EPS is disclosed alongside basic EPS precisely because it shows the worst-case dilution a shareholder should be aware of. *Worked example*: profit attributable to ordinary shareholders £600,000, weighted average shares in issue 2,000,000: basic EPS = 600,000/2,000,000 = 30.0p. Convertible loan stock, if converted, would add 200,000 shares and save £24,000 of post-tax interest: diluted EPS = (600,000+24,000)/(2,000,000+200,000) = 28.36p -- lower than basic EPS, as dilution always is (unless the potential shares are anti-dilutive, in which case they are excluded from the calculation).
