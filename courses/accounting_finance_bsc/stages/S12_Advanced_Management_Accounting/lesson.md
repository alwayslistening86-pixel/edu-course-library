# S12_Advanced_Management_Accounting - Lesson: Advanced management accounting

## Goal
The learner sets and evaluates transfer prices between divisions, calculates and compares divisional performance using ROI and residual income, and explains strategic management accounting's broader, externally-focused approach -- matching Advanced Financial Accounting and Management Accounting (B352), Part II: advanced management accounting.

## Syllabus items taught here
- B352-5 - Transfer pricing between divisions
- B352-6 - Divisional performance: ROI vs residual income
- B352-7 - Strategic management accounting and the value chain

## How to teach this
Ask: if one division of a company sells a component to another division of the same company, does the price they charge each other actually matter to the company as a whole? Teach each topic by building on what A-level Accounting and A-level Mathematics already gave the learner: name the A-level idea it extends (e.g. A-level Accounting's basic financial statements, before IAS 2/IAS 16/IFRS 15/IFRS 16 formalise specific standards), then show the IFRS-specific technical treatment or quantitative technique degree-level accounting/finance adds. Work every numerical example with the learner predicting a step before it is shown; every figure in this course was computed with Python when the course was built. UK tax figures (S13) are the 2026/27 tax year; always check HMRC's current published rates before relying on a real-world tax calculation, since UK tax rates/thresholds/allowances change at least annually.

#### B352-5 Transfer pricing between divisions
**Transfer pricing** sets the price at which one division sells goods/services to another division of the same group. It matters for **divisional performance measurement** (a badly set transfer price can make a well-run division look poor, or a poorly-run one look good) even though, in a purely consolidated sense, the group's overall profit is unaffected by what one division charges another (it is only a transfer of profit between divisions, not a change in group profit, unless it distorts a division's genuine decisions, e.g. buying externally when the group's own capacity was cheaper overall). Common approaches: **market-based** (the external market price, where one exists -- generally the theoretically preferred approach, since it reflects genuine opportunity cost); **cost-based** (variable cost, full/absorption cost, or cost-plus a margin -- simpler where no external market exists, but can distort decisions if set at full cost including fixed overhead the group is going to incur regardless); and **negotiated** (divisions bargain a price, preserving some autonomy but potentially wasting management time or reflecting relative bargaining power rather than economic reality). *Worked example, minimum transfer price*: the selling division's variable cost is £28.00/unit and it has spare capacity (no lost external sales): the theoretical minimum transfer price is £28.00/unit (its variable cost, since no external sale is being given up); if the selling division instead has no spare capacity, the minimum rises to variable cost plus the opportunity cost of the lost external contribution, e.g. £28.00 + £12.00 (lost contribution per unit) = £40.00/unit.

#### B352-6 Divisional performance: ROI vs residual income
**Divisional performance measurement**: **return on investment (ROI)** = divisional profit/divisional investment (capital employed), expressed as a percentage -- easily compared across divisions of different sizes, but can encourage a manager to reject a genuinely good project that would lower the division's current (already high) average ROI, even though it exceeds the company's cost of capital. **Residual income (RI)** = divisional profit - (an imputed interest charge x divisional investment), an absolute £ figure -- better aligns divisional decisions with company-wide goal congruence, since any project earning more than the imputed cost of capital increases RI (and so is accepted), regardless of the division's existing average ROI. *Worked example*: Division X has profit of £220,000, investment of £1,000,000, and the company's cost of capital is 12%: ROI = 22.0%; RI = 220,000 - (12% x 1,000,000) = £100,000. A new project earning a 15% return would raise RI (15% exceeds the 12% cost of capital, so it adds value), but a manager judged on ROI alone might reject it anyway, since 15% is below Division X's current 22.0% average ROI and would pull that average down -- illustrating ROI's goal-congruence problem that RI avoids.

#### B352-7 Strategic management accounting and the value chain
**Strategic management accounting** extends traditional, internally-focused management accounting outward: analysing competitors' costs and likely strategies, tracking market share and relative competitive position, and using techniques like the **value chain** (breaking the business into its primary and support activities -- inbound logistics, operations, outbound logistics, marketing/sales, service, plus procurement, technology development, HR management and firm infrastructure -- to identify where genuine competitive advantage/cost leadership or differentiation is created) and **life-cycle costing** and **target costing** (revisited from S06, but now framed explicitly as strategic tools for managing cost competitiveness against known or anticipated rivals, not merely as internal costing techniques). The QAA benchmark's emphasis on graduate-level accounting/finance skill includes exactly this kind of outward-looking, judgement-based application, not just correct technical computation -- strategic management accounting is where the discipline moves from 'what did this cost' to 'how does our cost structure compare, and what should we do about it'.
