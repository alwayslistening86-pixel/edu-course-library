# S06_Management_Accounting_I_Costing_and_Budgeting - Lesson: Management accounting I: costing and budgeting

## Goal
The learner compares absorption and marginal costing, applies activity-based costing, distinguishes job/batch/process costing and target/lifecycle costing, and prepares and evaluates budgets including behavioural aspects of budgetary control -- matching Intermediate Management Accounting (B253), Part I.

## Syllabus items taught here
- B253-1 - Absorption vs marginal costing
- B253-2 - Activity-based costing (ABC)
- B253-3 - Job/batch/process costing; target costing and lifecycle costing
- B253-4 - Budgeting and behavioural aspects of budgetary control

## How to teach this
Ask: two companies make the same product at the same total cost, but one values closing inventory higher than the other under a different costing method -- how can that be, if the underlying cash spent is identical? Teach each topic by building on what A-level Accounting and A-level Mathematics already gave the learner: name the A-level idea it extends (e.g. A-level Accounting's basic financial statements, before IAS 2/IAS 16/IFRS 15/IFRS 16 formalise specific standards), then show the IFRS-specific technical treatment or quantitative technique degree-level accounting/finance adds. Work every numerical example with the learner predicting a step before it is shown; every figure in this course was computed with Python when the course was built. UK tax figures (S13) are the 2026/27 tax year; always check HMRC's current published rates before relying on a real-world tax calculation, since UK tax rates/thresholds/allowances change at least annually.

#### B253-1 Absorption vs marginal costing
**Absorption costing** charges each unit with a share of both variable AND fixed production overheads (fixed overhead absorbed at a predetermined rate per unit, e.g. per labour hour), so closing inventory carries some fixed cost forward to the next period; **marginal costing** charges only variable production costs to units, treating all fixed overhead as a period cost written off in full against the period it is incurred. When production exceeds sales (inventory rises), absorption costing reports higher profit than marginal costing (since some fixed cost is deferred in closing inventory rather than expensed); when sales exceed production, marginal costing reports the higher profit. *Worked example*: fixed production overhead £60,000 for a budgeted 6,000 units, absorption rate = £10/unit; if 500 more units are produced than sold, absorption costing profit exceeds marginal costing profit by 500 x £10 = £5,000.

#### B253-2 Activity-based costing (ABC)
**Activity-based costing (ABC)** refines absorption costing's often-arbitrary single overhead-absorption base (e.g. labour hours) by tracing overhead to **cost pools** driven by **cost drivers** (e.g. number of machine set-ups, number of purchase orders, number of quality inspections) that better reflect what actually causes the overhead cost. *Worked example*: set-up costs of £40,000 are driven by 200 set-ups across the factory (cost driver rate = £200/set-up); a product requiring 15 set-ups for a batch of 500 units absorbs 15 x £200 = £3,000 of set-up cost, or £6.00 per unit -- a low-volume, high-complexity product typically picks up more overhead under ABC than under a simple volume-based absorption rate, which better reflects the resources it actually consumes.

#### B253-3 Job/batch/process costing; target costing and lifecycle costing
**Job, batch and process costing**: **job costing** accumulates cost against a single, identifiable job/contract (e.g. a bespoke building project); **batch costing** accumulates cost against a batch of identical units, then divides by the batch size to get a cost per unit (e.g. a production run of a standard component); **process costing** applies where output is a continuous, homogeneous flow (e.g. chemicals, food processing), splitting total process cost (materials, labour, overheads) between completed units and closing work-in-progress, the latter usually measured in **equivalent units** (partially completed units expressed as a fraction of a complete unit for costing purposes). **Target costing** starts from a market-determined selling price, deducts the required profit margin to derive an allowable target cost, then drives cost reduction (design, sourcing, process) to hit it -- the reverse of cost-plus pricing. **Lifecycle costing** accumulates a product's total cost over its whole life (design, development, production, distribution, customer service, disposal), not just its production cost, recognising that a large share of a product's total lifetime cost is often locked in by decisions made at the design stage.

#### B253-4 Budgeting and behavioural aspects of budgetary control
**Budgeting**: a budget translates strategic objectives into a quantified, time-bound plan, and provides a basis for **budgetary control** (comparing actual results to budget and investigating variances). Approaches include **incremental budgeting** (last year's budget plus/minus an adjustment -- simple, but can perpetuate past inefficiencies), **zero-based budgeting (ZBB)** (every budget line must be justified from zero each period -- more rigorous, but time-consuming), and **rolling budgets** (a further period added as each period ends, keeping a constant planning horizon). **Behavioural aspects**: a budget set too tightly (unachievable) can demotivate staff and encourage dysfunctional behaviour (e.g. cutting corners, deferring necessary spend); one set too loosely (easily achieved, sometimes via **budgetary slack** deliberately built in by managers to make their own future target easier) understates the department's true potential and wastes resources; **participative budgeting** (involving the managers who will be held to the budget in setting it) tends to improve commitment and the quality of the information used, but can invite slack if not checked.
