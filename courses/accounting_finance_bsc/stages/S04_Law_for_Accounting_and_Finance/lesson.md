# S04_Law_for_Accounting_and_Finance - Lesson: Law for accounting and finance

## Goal
The learner explains the essentials of contract law as it affects business dealings, the key features of company law (incorporation, share capital, directors' duties), and the basics of UK insolvency law, matching the Law for Accounting and Finance module (B251).

## Syllabus items taught here
- B251-1 - Contract law essentials for business
- B251-2 - Company law: incorporation and share capital
- B251-3 - Directors' duties (Companies Act 2006, ss.171-177)
- B251-4 - UK insolvency law: administration, liquidation, CVAs, wrongful/fraudulent trading

## How to teach this
Ask: when a company signs a contract, who is actually bound by it -- the company itself, or the individual director who signed it? Teach each topic by building on what A-level Accounting and A-level Mathematics already gave the learner: name the A-level idea it extends (e.g. A-level Accounting's basic financial statements, before IAS 2/IAS 16/IFRS 15/IFRS 16 formalise specific standards), then show the IFRS-specific technical treatment or quantitative technique degree-level accounting/finance adds. Work every numerical example with the learner predicting a step before it is shown; every figure in this course was computed with Python when the course was built. UK tax figures (S13) are the 2026/27 tax year; always check HMRC's current published rates before relying on a real-world tax calculation, since UK tax rates/thresholds/allowances change at least annually.

#### B251-1 Contract law essentials for business
**Contract law essentials**: a legally binding contract requires **offer**, **acceptance**, **consideration** (something of value given by each party -- distinguishing a contract from a mere gift/promise), an **intention to create legal relations** (presumed in business dealings), and **capacity** (the parties must be legally able to contract). A **term** may be a **condition** (a fundamental term; breach entitles the innocent party to terminate and claim damages) or a **warranty** (a less important term; breach gives only a right to damages, not termination). **Remedies for breach** include damages (compensating the innocent party's loss), and in limited cases specific performance or an injunction.

#### B251-2 Company law: incorporation and share capital
**Company law -- incorporation and share capital**: a company incorporated under the Companies Act 2006 is a **separate legal person** from its owners/shareholders (distinct from a sole trader or an ordinary partnership), and shareholders normally have **limited liability** -- their loss is capped at the amount unpaid on their shares. A **private limited company (Ltd)** cannot offer shares to the public; a **public limited company (plc)** can, and must meet a minimum allotted share capital requirement. **Share capital** consists of ordinary shares (usually carrying voting rights and a residual claim on profits/assets) and sometimes preference shares (a fixed dividend, priority over ordinary shareholders on a winding-up, but usually no voting rights). *Worked example*: a company issues 100,000 £1 ordinary shares at £1.50 each: share capital (nominal value) = £100,000, share premium = £50,000.

#### B251-3 Directors' duties (Companies Act 2006, ss.171-177)
**Directors' duties** (Companies Act 2006, ss.171-177): to act within powers (per the constitution); to promote the success of the company for the benefit of members as a whole (having regard to long-term consequences, employees, suppliers/customers, the community/environment, and the company's reputation); to exercise independent judgement; to exercise reasonable care, skill and diligence; to avoid conflicts of interest; not to accept benefits from third parties; and to declare any interest in a proposed transaction. A director who breaches these duties can be personally liable to the company for resulting loss, and may be disqualified from acting as a director under separate disqualification legislation.

#### B251-4 UK insolvency law: administration, liquidation, CVAs, wrongful/fraudulent trading
**UK insolvency law** offers several routes for a company in financial difficulty: **administration** (an administrator takes control, aiming to rescue the company as a going concern, achieve a better result for creditors than immediate liquidation, or realise assets to make a distribution to secured/preferential creditors); **liquidation** (winding up the company, realising its assets and distributing proceeds to creditors in a statutory order of priority, then dissolving the company) -- either **voluntary** (members' or creditors') or **compulsory** (court-ordered); and a **company voluntary arrangement (CVA)** (a binding agreement with creditors to pay debts over time, allowing the company to keep trading). **Wrongful trading** (continuing to trade when a director knew or ought to have known insolvent liquidation was unavoidable, without taking every reasonable step to minimise creditor loss) and **fraudulent trading** (trading with intent to defraud creditors) can both make a director personally liable to contribute to the company's assets on liquidation -- fraudulent trading is also a criminal offence.
