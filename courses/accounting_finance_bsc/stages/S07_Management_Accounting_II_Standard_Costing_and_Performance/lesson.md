# S07_Management_Accounting_II_Standard_Costing_and_Performance - Lesson: Management accounting II: standard costing and performance

## Goal
The learner calculates and interprets material, labour and overhead variances under standard costing, and evaluates performance measurement systems including the balanced scorecard, benchmarking and ESG/sustainability reporting in a management accounting context -- matching Intermediate Management Accounting (B253), Part II.

## How to teach this
Ask: if a factory used more material than the standard allows but paid less per kilo than expected, how do we separate 'we used material inefficiently' from 'we got a good/bad price' in the numbers? Teach each topic by building on what A-level Accounting and A-level Mathematics already gave the learner: name the A-level idea it extends (e.g. A-level Accounting's basic financial statements, before IAS 2/IAS 16/IFRS 15/IFRS 16 formalise specific standards), then show the IFRS-specific technical treatment or quantitative technique degree-level accounting/finance adds. Work every numerical example with the learner predicting a step before it is shown; every figure in this course was computed with Python when the course was built. UK tax figures (S13) are the 2026/27 tax year; always check HMRC's current published rates before relying on a real-world tax calculation, since UK tax rates/thresholds/allowances change at least annually.

#### B253-5 Standard costing: material and labour variances
**Standard costing** sets a predetermined (standard) cost per unit for material, labour and overhead, against which actual results are compared to generate **variances**. **Material variances**: price variance = (standard price - actual price) x actual quantity purchased; usage variance = (standard quantity for actual output - actual quantity used) x standard price. **Labour variances**: rate variance = (standard rate - actual rate) x actual hours; efficiency variance = (standard hours for actual output - actual hours worked) x standard rate. *Worked example, material*: standard price £5.00/kg, actual price £5.30/kg, actual purchase 2,000kg: price variance = (5.00-5.30) x 2,000 = £-600 (adverse -- paid more per kg than standard). Standard usage for the output achieved is 1,800kg, actual usage was 1,950kg, standard price £5.00: usage variance = (1,800-1,950) x 5 = £-750 (adverse -- used more material than the standard allowed).

#### B253-6 Overhead and sales variances
**Overhead variances** (fixed overhead, absorption costing) split into an **expenditure variance** (budgeted fixed overhead less actual fixed overhead) and a **volume variance** (arising because actual output produced differs from budgeted output, valued at the standard absorption rate per unit); the volume variance can be further split into **efficiency** and **capacity** sub-variances where hours worked also differ from budget. **Sales variances**: the **sales price variance** = (actual price - standard price) x actual volume sold; the **sales volume (contribution) variance** = (actual volume - budgeted volume) x standard contribution per unit -- measuring the effect on profit of selling more or fewer units than planned, valued at standard margin so it isn't distorted by the separate price variance. All variances should be investigated for cause (not just reported), since a favourable variance can conceal a problem too (e.g. a favourable material price variance from buying cheaper, lower-quality material that then causes an adverse usage variance from higher wastage).

#### B253-7 Balanced scorecard, benchmarking and ESG/sustainability reporting
**Performance measurement beyond financial variances**: the **balanced scorecard** (Kaplan and Norton) assesses performance across four linked perspectives -- financial (traditional measures like ROI/profit), customer (satisfaction, retention, market share), internal business process (quality, cycle time, efficiency), and learning and growth (employee skills, innovation, systems) -- on the view that a purely financial, backward-looking set of measures misses the drivers of future financial performance. **Benchmarking** compares an organisation's performance against internal (other divisions), competitor, or best-in-class (any industry) standards to identify and close performance gaps. **ESG/sustainability reporting** (environmental, social and governance) extends management accounting's traditional financial focus to non-financial impacts and dependencies (e.g. carbon emissions, supply-chain labour standards, board diversity/governance quality) that increasingly affect long-term value, cost of capital, and regulatory/reputational risk -- management accountants increasingly design internal reporting systems and KPIs to track these alongside financial performance, not as a separate add-on.
