# S10_Governance_Audit_and_Assurance - Lesson: Governance, audit and assurance

## Goal
The learner explains the UK Corporate Governance Code's key principles, plans an audit using risk and materiality, distinguishes tests of control from substantive procedures and explains audit sampling, evaluates an internal control system, and interprets audit report opinions and the fundamental ethical principles of auditor independence -- matching Governance, Audit and Assurance (B351).

## Syllabus items taught here
- B351-1 - UK Corporate Governance Code
- B351-2 - Audit planning: risk and materiality
- B351-3 - Tests of control, substantive procedures and audit sampling
- B351-4 - Internal control systems and their limitations
- B351-5 - Audit report opinions and auditor independence/ethics

## How to teach this
Ask: an auditor's report says the financial statements give a 'true and fair view' -- does that mean the auditor is guaranteeing there is no fraud or error anywhere in the accounts? Teach each topic by building on what A-level Accounting and A-level Mathematics already gave the learner: name the A-level idea it extends (e.g. A-level Accounting's basic financial statements, before IAS 2/IAS 16/IFRS 15/IFRS 16 formalise specific standards), then show the IFRS-specific technical treatment or quantitative technique degree-level accounting/finance adds. Work every numerical example with the learner predicting a step before it is shown; every figure in this course was computed with Python when the course was built. UK tax figures (S13) are the 2026/27 tax year; always check HMRC's current published rates before relying on a real-world tax calculation, since UK tax rates/thresholds/allowances change at least annually.

#### B351-1 UK Corporate Governance Code
The **UK Corporate Governance Code** (FRC) sets principles of good governance for listed companies on a **'comply or explain'** basis (a company either complies with a provision or explains publicly why it has not): a clear division of responsibilities between the chair (leading the board) and the chief executive (running the business), so no one individual has unfettered decision-making power; a board with an appropriate balance of independent non-executive directors; an **audit committee** (of independent non-executive directors) overseeing financial reporting integrity, the external audit relationship (recommending auditor appointment/reappointment and fee, monitoring independence) and internal controls/risk management; and effective, independent remuneration and nomination processes. Good governance reduces the risk of management override of controls and supports the reliability of the financial statements the audit ultimately reports on.

#### B351-2 Audit planning: risk and materiality
**Audit planning**: the auditor assesses **audit risk** (the risk of giving an inappropriate opinion) as **inherent risk** (susceptibility of an area to material misstatement, e.g. a complex estimate like an impairment provision) x **control risk** (the risk the client's own controls fail to prevent/detect a misstatement) x **detection risk** (the risk the auditor's own procedures fail to detect a misstatement that exists -- the only element the auditor directly controls, by the nature, timing and extent of testing performed). **Materiality** is set (often as a percentage of a benchmark such as profit before tax, revenue or total assets) to judge which misstatements, individually or in aggregate, could reasonably influence the economic decisions of users of the financial statements. *Worked example*: profit before tax is £2,000,000; materiality is set at 5% of PBT: materiality = £100,000. A high-risk area (e.g. complex judgemental estimates, related-party transactions, or an area with a history of misstatement) receives more audit attention/lower performance materiality than a routine, low-risk area.

#### B351-3 Tests of control, substantive procedures and audit sampling
**Tests of control** check whether a client's internal control is operating effectively (e.g. re-performing a purchase invoice authorisation check); **substantive procedures** directly test whether an account balance or transaction is free of material misstatement, either **substantive analytical procedures** (e.g. comparing a ratio to expectation and investigating unexplained fluctuations) or **tests of detail** (e.g. tracing a sample of receivables balances to after-date cash receipts, or physically observing inventory count). Since testing every single transaction is normally impractical, auditors use **audit sampling** (statistical or judgemental) to select a representative sample and draw a conclusion about the whole population -- the same statistical logic covered in S01 (a larger, well-selected sample narrows the uncertainty about the true population characteristic, at a cost of more audit time). *Worked example*: the standard error of an estimated population error rate falls with sqrt(sample size) (as in S01); quadrupling the sample from 30 to 120 items narrows that standard error by a factor of sqrt(120/30) = 2.00, i.e. it halves -- so a much bigger sample buys a comparatively modest reduction in uncertainty, which is why auditors calibrate sample size to risk and materiality rather than simply testing as much as possible.

#### B351-4 Internal control systems and their limitations
**Internal control systems** (commonly analysed using a five-component framework: control environment -- the tone at the top and overall control culture; risk assessment -- identifying and analysing risks to achieving objectives; control activities -- policies/procedures that help ensure management directives are carried out, e.g. authorisation limits, segregation of duties, reconciliations; information and communication -- ensuring relevant information flows to those who need it; and monitoring -- ongoing or periodic evaluation of whether controls are working) reduce (but never eliminate) the risk of material misstatement, whether from error or fraud. **Inherent limitations** of any internal control system include human error/judgement, the possibility of controls being deliberately overridden by management (management override), collusion between employees defeating segregation of duties, and a basic cost-benefit constraint (a control costing more than the risk it addresses is not worth implementing) -- which is exactly why external audit exists as an additional, independent check rather than relying on internal controls alone.

#### B351-5 Audit report opinions and auditor independence/ethics
**Audit report opinions**: an **unmodified (unqualified)** opinion states the financial statements give a true and fair view in accordance with the applicable financial reporting framework, in all material respects. A **modified opinion** is required when the auditor concludes the statements are materially misstated, or is unable to obtain sufficient appropriate audit evidence (a scope limitation): a **qualified ('except for')** opinion where the issue is material but not pervasive; an **adverse** opinion where a material misstatement is both material and pervasive; and a **disclaimer of opinion** where an inability to obtain evidence is both material and pervasive, so the auditor cannot express an opinion at all. **Auditor independence** and the fundamental ethical principles (integrity, objectivity, professional competence and due care, confidentiality, professional behaviour, per the IESBA/ICAEW Code of Ethics) require the auditor to be, and be seen to be, free from threats (self-interest, self-review, advocacy, familiarity, intimidation) that could compromise objectivity -- e.g. an audit firm should not also provide certain non-audit services, or hold a financial interest in, the audit client, and audit partners are subject to mandatory rotation after a defined period on a given client.
