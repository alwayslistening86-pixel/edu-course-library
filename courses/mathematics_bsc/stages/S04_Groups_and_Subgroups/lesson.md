# S04_Groups_and_Subgroups - Lesson: Groups and subgroups

## Goal
The learner verifies the group axioms for standard examples, applies the subgroup test, works with cyclic groups and generators, uses cycle notation and parity of permutations, and applies Lagrange's theorem.

## Syllabus items taught here
- 4a - Groups: definition, axioms, examples (Z_n, symmetric groups, dihedral groups, matrix groups)
- 4b - Subgroups; the subgroup test; cyclic groups and generators
- 4c - Permutations: cycle notation, transpositions, even/odd permutations and the sign of a permutation
- 4d - Lagrange's theorem and its consequences (order of an element divides |G|)
- 4e - Cosets of a subgroup

## How to teach this
Ask whether (Z, -) (integers under subtraction) is a group, and which axiom fails, before any definition is given. Work every proof and worked example with the learner line by line before revealing the next step; insist on full, rigorous justification (this is an honours-degree pure/applied mathematics course, not a procedural one). #### 4a Groups: definition, axioms, examples (Z_n, symmetric groups, dihedral groups, matrix groups)
A **group** (G, *) is a set G with a binary operation * satisfying: (i) **closure** -- a*b in G for all a,b in G; (ii) **associativity** -- (a*b)*c = a*(b*c); (iii) **identity** -- there exists e in G with e*a = a*e = a for all a; (iv) **inverses** -- for each a there exists a^{-1} with a*a^{-1} = a^{-1}*a = e. *Examples:* (Z, +) is a group (identity 0, inverse of n is -n); (Z, -) is not, since - is not associative (e.g. (5-3)-1=1 but 5-(3-1)=3). (Z_n, +) (integers mod n) is a group of order n. The **symmetric group** S_n (all bijections of {1,...,n} to itself, under composition) has order n!. The **dihedral group** D_n (symmetries of a regular n-gon: rotations and reflections) has order 2n. Groups where a*b = b*a for all a,b are called **abelian**; S_n for n>=3 and D_n for n>=3 are non-abelian.

#### 4b Subgroups; the subgroup test; cyclic groups and generators
A subset H of a group G is a **subgroup** (written H <= G) if H is itself a group under G's operation. The **subgroup test**: H is a subgroup iff H is non-empty and, for all a,b in H, a*b^{-1} is in H (equivalently, for finite H, just closure suffices since inverses and identity follow automatically). A group G is **cyclic** if G = <g> = {g^n : n in Z} for some g (a **generator**); every cyclic group is abelian. *Example:* in (Z_12, +), the subgroup generated by 3 is <3> = {0,3,6,9}, order 4; the subgroup generated by 5 is all of Z_12 (since gcd(5,12)=1), so 5 is a generator and Z_12 is cyclic of order 12.

#### 4c Permutations: cycle notation, transpositions, even/odd permutations and the sign of a permutation
A **permutation** of {1,...,n} can be written in **cycle notation**: (1 3 5) means 1->3, 3->5, 5->1, fixing everything else. Disjoint cycles commute and every permutation is a product of disjoint cycles. A **transposition** is a 2-cycle (a b); every permutation is a product of transpositions (not uniquely, but the parity of the number used is invariant). A permutation is **even** if it is a product of an even number of transpositions, **odd** otherwise; the **sign** is +1 (even) or -1 (odd), and sign is a homomorphism from S_n to ({1,-1}, x). *Example:* (1 2 3 4) = (1 4)(1 3)(1 2), three transpositions, so it is odd, sign -1. The even permutations of S_n form a subgroup, the **alternating group** A_n, of order n!/2.

#### 4d Lagrange's theorem and its consequences (order of an element divides |G|)
**Lagrange's Theorem**: if G is a finite group and H <= G, then |H| divides |G|. Consequently: the **order of an element** g (the smallest positive n with g^n = e) divides |G|; and every group of prime order p is cyclic (its only subgroups are {e} and itself, so any non-identity element generates the whole group). *Example:* in a group of order 15, by Lagrange the order of every element divides 15, so is 1, 3, 5 or 15; if an element of order 15 exists the group is cyclic. Lagrange's theorem is proved by showing the (left) cosets of H partition G into equal-sized blocks of size |H|, so |H| divides |G|.

#### 4e Cosets of a subgroup
For H <= G and g in G, the **left coset** gH = {gh : h in H}. Distinct left cosets of H partition G (they are either identical or disjoint), and every coset has the same size |H|; this is exactly what proves Lagrange's theorem. The number of distinct cosets is the **index** [G:H] = |G|/|H|. *Example:* in (Z_8, +), for H = {0,4}, the cosets are 0+H={0,4}, 1+H={1,5}, 2+H={2,6}, 3+H={3,7} -- four cosets, each of size 2, partitioning all 8 elements; index [Z_8:H] = 4.
