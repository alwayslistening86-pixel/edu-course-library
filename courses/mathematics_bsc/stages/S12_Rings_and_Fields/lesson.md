# S12_Rings_and_Fields - Lesson: Rings and fields

## Goal
The learner verifies the ring axioms, distinguishes integral domains and fields, works with ideals and quotient rings, applies the First Isomorphism Theorem for rings, and uses unique factorisation in Z and polynomial rings.

## Syllabus items taught here

#### 12a Rings: definition, axioms, examples (Z, Z_n, polynomial rings, matrix rings)
A **ring** (R,+,x) has (R,+) an abelian group, x associative, and x distributing over + (both sides); many rings (including all considered here) also have a multiplicative identity 1. *Examples:* (Z,+,x); (Z_n,+,x) (integers mod n); polynomial rings R[x] (coefficients from a ring R); M_n(R) (n x n matrices, non-commutative for n>=2). A **commutative** ring has xy=yx for all x,y; Z, Z_n and R[x] are commutative, M_n(R) is not (for n>=2).

#### 12b Integral domains and fields; zero divisors; units
A nonzero element a of a ring is a **zero divisor** if ab=0 for some nonzero b. An **integral domain** is a commutative ring with 1≠0 and no zero divisors (ab=0 implies a=0 or b=0). A **field** is a commutative ring with 1≠0 in which every nonzero element has a multiplicative inverse (a **unit**); every field is an integral domain (if ab=0 and a≠0, multiply by a^{-1} to get b=0), but not conversely (Z is a domain, not a field: 2 has no inverse in Z). *Example:* in Z_6, 2x3=0 mod 6, so 2 and 3 are zero divisors -- Z_6 is not a domain. In Z_5, direct checking (or Fermat's little theorem) confirms every nonzero element has an inverse: 1x1=1, 2x3=6≡1 (mod 5, since 5 is divisible by 5), 4x4=16≡1 (mod 5, since 15 is divisible by 5) -- so the inverse pairs are 1<->1, 2<->3, 4<->4, and Z_5 is a field. Z_n is a field exactly when n is prime.

#### 12c Ideals and quotient rings
An **ideal** I of a commutative ring R is an additive subgroup closed under multiplication by ANY ring element (rI in I for all r in R, not just elements of I) -- the ring analogue of a normal subgroup. Given ideal I, the **quotient ring** R/I has elements the cosets r+I, with (a+I)+(b+I)=(a+b)+I and (a+I)(b+I)=ab+I (well-defined because I absorbs multiplication by ring elements). *Example:* in Z, nZ is an ideal (closed under multiplication by any integer); Z/nZ ≅ Z_n exactly as for the additive quotient group, but now also carries the ring structure (multiplication mod n).

#### 12d Ring homomorphisms and the First Isomorphism Theorem for rings
A **ring homomorphism** phi: R -> S satisfies phi(a+b)=phi(a)+phi(b) and phi(ab)=phi(a)phi(b) (and phi(1_R)=1_S, if both rings have 1). ker(phi) = {a: phi(a)=0} is always an ideal of R; im(phi) is a subring of S. **First Isomorphism Theorem for rings**: R/ker(phi) ≅ im(phi). *Example:* the evaluation map phi: R[x] -> R, phi(p(x)) = p(0), is a ring homomorphism (sum and product of evaluations equal evaluation of sum/product); ker(phi) = {polynomials with zero constant term} = (x) (the ideal generated by x); im(phi) = R; so R[x]/(x) ≅ R.

#### 12e Unique factorisation in Z and in polynomial rings; irreducibility
Z has **unique factorisation**: every integer > 1 factors into primes uniquely up to order (the Fundamental Theorem of Arithmetic). Polynomial rings F[x] over a field F have the analogous property: every non-constant polynomial factors uniquely (up to order and unit/scalar factors) into **irreducible** polynomials (those that cannot be factored into two lower-degree polynomials over F). *Example:* over Q, x^4-1 factors as (x - 1)(x + 1)(x^2 + 1) -- irreducible factors (x-1), (x+1), (x^2+1) (the last is irreducible over R/Q since it has no real roots, but factors further over C as (x-i)(x+i)). Irreducibility is field-dependent: x^2+1 is irreducible over Q and R but reducible over C.
