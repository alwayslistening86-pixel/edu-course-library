# A-level Law (AQA 7162): The Nature of Law and the English Legal System, Criminal Law, Tort, and Contract - Cumulative Exam

## Unlock condition
Only available once every stage in `stage_ladder` has a passed test.

## Format
Each paper: five 1-mark MCQs, one 5-mark non-substantive short-answer question, a 5-mark application scenario, a 10-mark application/advice scenario, a 15-mark discursive essay, and one or two extended questions worth 30 marks each (a scenario, an essay, or a combined scenario-plus-non-substantive-law discussion, as in AQA's real papers) -- 100 marks per paper. Questions are original, in AQA's style using AQA's own command words; write fresh ones rather than reusing stage tests. The ready-made questions below are a starter bank; the tutor writes the rest.

## 9 ready-made items (write the rest fresh, never reusing stage-test items)
1. [Paper 1] State two differences between binding and persuasive precedent. [4 marks]
2. [Paper 1 - application] Priya, in a heated argument, picks up a heavy book and throws it at Sam, intending only to frighten him, but it strikes Sam and breaks his arm. Using IRAC, advise on Priya's liability under s20 Offences Against the Person Act 1861. [10 marks]
3. [Paper 1 - discursive essay] Discuss the extent to which the rules on murder and voluntary manslaughter achieve justice. [15 marks]
4. [Paper 2] Explain two factors a court weighs when deciding whether a defendant has breached the objective standard of care in negligence. [5 marks]
5. [Paper 2 - application] A shopper is struck by a falling shelf in a supermarket that had been visibly overloaded and unsecured for several days despite staff walking past it repeatedly. Using IRAC, advise on the shopper's claim in negligence. [10 marks]
6. [Paper 2 - extended scenario] Set a Rylands v Fletcher and private nuisance problem question (write fresh facts: e.g. chemicals escaping from a factory's storage tank onto a neighbouring farm) and a linked discussion of the advantages and disadvantages of strict liability in tort. Mark using the scenario points plus a discussion of policy. [30 marks]
7. [Paper 3A] Explain the postal rule in contract formation and one situation in which it does not apply. [5 marks]
8. [Paper 3A - application] A trader tells a buyer during negotiations that a second-hand van 'has never had any engine problems', not knowing this is false, and the buyer agrees to buy it in reliance on that statement. The engine fails a week later. Using IRAC, advise the buyer on a claim in misrepresentation. [10 marks]
9. [Paper 3A - discursive essay] Assess the extent to which the law of contract balances freedom of contract with the protection of consumers. [15 marks]

## Answer key for the ready-made items (tutor only)
1. [4] M2 binding precedent must be followed by a court lower in the hierarchy than the court that made it, forming part of stare decisis; M2 persuasive precedent (e.g. obiter dicta, a decision of a court lower in the hierarchy, a dissenting judgment, or a decision of a court outside England and Wales) need not be followed but may influence a later court.
2. [10] Structured application/advice items are marked issue-rule-application-conclusion (IRAC): identify the legal issue(s) the facts raise, state the applicable rule precisely with its authority (statute section or case, named exactly), apply it to the specific facts given (not a restatement of the rule in the abstract), and reach a supported conclusion or piece of advice. A correct rule with no application to the facts earns AO1 credit only, not the AO2 application marks. Credit: issue = whether Priya is liable for malicious wounding/GBH under s20; rule = s20 requires an unlawful and malicious wounding or infliction of GBH, with mens rea of intention or subjective (Cunningham) recklessness as to some harm, not the full harm caused (R v Mowatt / R v Savage and Parmenter); application = throwing a heavy book at Sam is an unlawful act; a broken arm is GBH; Priya need only have foreseen some physical harm, which is satisfied by deliberately throwing an object at him even though she intended only to frighten; conclusion = Priya is likely liable under s20.

[5] M2 the degree of risk of harm (the greater the foreseeable risk, the higher the standard, e.g. Bolton v Stone); M2 the cost and practicability of taking precautions against the risk (Latimer v AEC); M1 (or) any one other recognised factor, e.g. the defendant's own special skill/characteristics (Nettleship v Weston) or the social utility/benefit of the defendant's conduct -- credit any two, each explained.
5. [10] Structured application/advice items are marked issue-rule-application-conclusion (IRAC): identify the legal issue(s) the facts raise, state the applicable rule precisely with its authority (statute section or case, named exactly), apply it to the specific facts given (not a restatement of the rule in the abstract), and reach a supported conclusion or piece of advice. A correct rule with no application to the facts earns AO1 credit only, not the AO2 application marks. Credit: issue = whether the supermarket owed and breached a duty of care in negligence; rule = a duty of care arises applying the Caparo test (foreseeability, proximity, fair/just/reasonable), easily satisfied between an occupier/retailer and a lawful customer; breach is judged by the objective reasonable-person standard, weighing the foreseeable risk of an overloaded, unsecured shelf against the low cost of securing it; application = staff repeatedly walking past an obviously unsafe shelf for days shows the risk was foreseeable and easily avoidable at minimal cost, so a reasonable retailer would have acted; conclusion = the supermarket is likely in breach, and (subject to causation/damage being straightforward on these facts) liable in negligence.

[30] Mark out of 30 using AQA's generic levels for extended scenario/essay writing (AO1 10 + AO2 10 + AO3 10, or AO1 10 + AO3 20 for a pure essay question): Level 1 (1-6) minimal understanding, fragmented legal argument; Level 2 (7-12) limited knowledge and application, a limited conclusion; Level 3 (13-18) satisfactory knowledge, application and developing analysis and reasoning; Level 4 (19-24) good knowledge, sound application and sustained analysis leading to a partially justified conclusion; Level 5 (25-30) excellent, precise knowledge, thorough application to the facts, and logical, sustained, well-developed reasoning to a valid, relevant and substantiated conclusion.
7. [5] M2 the postal rule (Adams v Lindsell): where post is a reasonable method of acceptance, acceptance is complete and the contract formed the moment the letter is posted, even if it is delayed or lost in the post; M2 (or) it does not apply where the offeror has expressly excluded it (e.g. requiring acceptance to be received), or where the method of communication is instantaneous (e.g. email, fax); M1 credit any one correctly stated exception.
8. [10] Structured application/advice items are marked issue-rule-application-conclusion (IRAC): identify the legal issue(s) the facts raise, state the applicable rule precisely with its authority (statute section or case, named exactly), apply it to the specific facts given (not a restatement of the rule in the abstract), and reach a supported conclusion or piece of advice. A correct rule with no application to the facts earns AO1 credit only, not the AO2 application marks. Credit: issue = whether the buyer has a claim in misrepresentation; rule = misrepresentation is a false statement of fact made before/at formation, inducing the contract; a statement made honestly believing it true but without reasonable grounds is negligent misrepresentation under s2(1) Misrepresentation Act 1967, reversing the burden onto the maker to prove reasonable grounds for belief; remedies are rescission and/or damages; application = the trader's statement is a false statement of fact about the van's history, made without knowledge of its falsity, and the buyer relied on it in deciding to buy; conclusion = the buyer has a strong claim in negligent misrepresentation under s2(1), entitling them to rescind and/or claim damages, unless the trader can show reasonable grounds for the belief (unlikely here).
