# S11_GBH_and_Wounding - Lesson: Malicious wounding/GBH s20; wounding/GBH with intent s18

## Goal
The learner explains malicious wounding/GBH under s20 OAPA 1861 and wounding/GBH with intent under s18 OAPA 1861, including what counts as a 'wound' or 'GBH' and the different mens rea each requires.

## Syllabus items taught here
- NFO.3 - Non-fatal offences: malicious wounding or inflicting grievous bodily harm, s20 Offences Against the Person Act 1861
- NFO.4 - Non-fatal offences: wounding or causing grievous bodily harm with intent, s18 Offences Against the Person Act 1861

## How to teach this
Ask: two defendants cause identical, severe injuries -- why might one face a maximum of five years and the other life imprisonment? Teach each rule as: the precise legal test (statute section or case authority, stated exactly, since AQA rewards named, accurate authority), why it exists (the policy AQA's own specification names, e.g. public policy limits on the duty of care, freedom of contract vs consumer protection), and how it applies to facts -- AQA's application and scenario questions (worth over half the marks on every paper) need the rule applied to the specific facts given, not restated in the abstract. Use AQA's own command words precisely: 'explain'/'suggest' target AO1 (accurate, precise legal knowledge); 'advise'/'consider' target AO1+AO2+AO3 together (apply the law to a scenario and reach a reasoned conclusion); 'examine'/'discuss'/'assess' target AO3 (evaluate non-substantive law, or substantive law within a non-substantive framework, e.g. discussing judicial precedent's advantages through a case scenario). Push the learner to build a bank of exact case names, years and the precise principle each establishes -- a vague 'there was a case about...' earns nothing under AQA's marking. Law changes: this course states the law of England and Wales as understood to be current in September 2026; statutory thresholds, ongoing reform and any pending legislation should be checked against a live source (legislation.gov.uk, aqa.org.uk) before a real exam sitting.

#### NFO.3 Non-fatal offences: malicious wounding or inflicting grievous bodily harm, s20 Offences Against the Person Act 1861
**Malicious wounding or grievous bodily harm, s20 Offences Against the Person Act 1861.** Actus reus: **unlawfully and maliciously** **wounding** (breaking both layers of the skin -- a graze or internal bleeding without a break in the skin is not a wound) **or** inflicting **grievous bodily harm** (really serious harm -- a question of fact and degree for the jury, guided by case law and, since *R v Ireland; R v Burstow* [1998] AC 147, extending to a recognised psychiatric injury that is genuinely serious). Mens rea: 'maliciously' has been interpreted as requiring only that the defendant **intended or was (Cunningham) subjectively reckless as to causing some harm**, not the full wound/GBH actually caused -- the leading case is ***R v Mowatt* [1968] 1 QB 421** (the defendant need not have foreseen a wound or GBH specifically, only that some physical harm might result), confirmed alongside s47's mens rea in *R v Savage; DPP v Parmenter* [1992] 1 AC 699. This makes s20, like s47, a constructive liability offence, though it sits higher on the sentencing scale (maximum 5 years) reflecting the more serious harm actually caused.

#### NFO.4 Non-fatal offences: wounding or causing grievous bodily harm with intent, s18 Offences Against the Person Act 1861
**Wounding or causing grievous bodily harm with intent, s18 Offences Against the Person Act 1861.** Actus reus: **unlawfully and maliciously** **wounding** or **causing** grievous bodily harm -- note 'causing' rather than 'inflicting', historically read as slightly wider than s20's 'inflicting', though in practice the distinction rarely matters given modern causation principles. Mens rea: this is a **specific intent** offence -- the prosecution must prove the defendant **intended** to cause grievous bodily harm (or intended to resist/prevent a lawful arrest, an alternative limb rarely examined in detail), and (unlike s20) **recklessness is not sufficient** (*R v Belfon* [1976] 1 WLR 741 -- recklessness as to causing some harm, even serious harm, is not enough for s18; only actual intention to cause GBH satisfies its mens rea). This makes s18 the most serious non-fatal offence (maximum sentence life imprisonment), and the higher mens rea threshold is the key distinguishing feature examiners test against s20: two defendants who cause identical injuries may be charged differently, and convicted of different offences, purely because of what each intended or foresaw, illustrating the significance the law places on mental state even where the physical harm is the same -- a point often drawn on in AQA's 'does the law achieve justice' essay questions on the non-fatal offences.
