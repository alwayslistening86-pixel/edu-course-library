# S13_Attempt_and_Capacity_Defences - Lesson: Attempt; insanity and automatism

## Goal
The learner explains the offence of attempt under s1 Criminal Attempts Act 1981 (the 'more than merely preparatory' test), and the capacity defences of insanity (the M'Naghten Rules) and automatism.

## Syllabus items taught here
- PREL.1 - Preliminary offence: attempt, s1 Criminal Attempts Act 1981 (an act more than merely preparatory, done with intent to commit the full offence)
- DEF.1 - Defences: capacity -- insanity (the M'Naghten Rules) and automatism (including self-induced automatism)

## How to teach this
Ask: someone breaks into a shed to get equipment to burgle a house next door, but is caught before leaving the shed -- have they attempted burglary yet? Teach each rule as: the precise legal test (statute section or case authority, stated exactly, since AQA rewards named, accurate authority), why it exists (the policy AQA's own specification names, e.g. public policy limits on the duty of care, freedom of contract vs consumer protection), and how it applies to facts -- AQA's application and scenario questions (worth over half the marks on every paper) need the rule applied to the specific facts given, not restated in the abstract. Use AQA's own command words precisely: 'explain'/'suggest' target AO1 (accurate, precise legal knowledge); 'advise'/'consider' target AO1+AO2+AO3 together (apply the law to a scenario and reach a reasoned conclusion); 'examine'/'discuss'/'assess' target AO3 (evaluate non-substantive law, or substantive law within a non-substantive framework, e.g. discussing judicial precedent's advantages through a case scenario). Push the learner to build a bank of exact case names, years and the precise principle each establishes -- a vague 'there was a case about...' earns nothing under AQA's marking. Law changes: this course states the law of England and Wales as understood to be current in September 2026; statutory thresholds, ongoing reform and any pending legislation should be checked against a live source (legislation.gov.uk, aqa.org.uk) before a real exam sitting.

#### PREL.1 Preliminary offence: attempt, s1 Criminal Attempts Act 1981 (an act more than merely preparatory, done with intent to commit the full offence)
**Attempt, s1 Criminal Attempts Act 1981.** A **preliminary offence**, applicable in principle to any indictable (or some either-way) full offence: liability attaches where the defendant, **with intent to commit the full offence**, does **an act which is more than merely preparatory** to its commission. The mens rea required is generally the same intention the full offence requires (for a result crime, intention as to that result is required even where the full offence itself could be committed recklessly, e.g. attempted murder requires an intention to kill, not merely GBH-intent, unlike murder itself). The key contested issue is almost always the actus reus: how far along the defendant's plan must they have got? The Act deliberately left "more than merely preparatory" undefined, leaving it a question of fact and degree for the jury, guided by case law: *R v Gullefer* [1990] 1 WLR 1063 (jumping onto a racetrack to try to have a race declared void, to reclaim a losing bet, was mere preparation, not yet an attempt to steal the stake, since the defendant had not yet embarked on the actual crime of obtaining property); *R v Jones (Kenneth)* [1990] 1 WLR 1057 (the Court of Appeal confirmed the correct approach is simply to apply the statutory words, without over-elaborate paraphrase, and held that a defendant who had obtained a shotgun, sawn it down, loaded it, got into the victim's car and pointed the gun at him had gone beyond mere preparation and could be convicted of attempted murder, even though further acts, e.g. releasing the safety catch, remained); *R v Geddes* [1996] Crim LR 894 (a man found hiding in a school toilet block with a knife, rope and masking tape, apparently intending to abduct a pupil, had not gone beyond preparation, since he had not yet approached or engaged with any pupil -- he had merely equipped and positioned himself to commit the offence). The dividing line the cases draw is roughly between planning/equipping oneself (preparatory) and actually embarking on the commission of the offence itself (more than merely preparatory).

#### DEF.1 Defences: capacity -- insanity (the M'Naghten Rules) and automatism (including self-induced automatism)
**Defences: insanity and automatism.** **Insanity** is a general defence to any crime (not just murder), governed by the common law **M'Naghten Rules (1843) 10 Cl & Fin 200**: the defendant must, at the time of the act, have been labouring under such a **defect of reason**, from a **disease of the mind**, that they either did not know the **nature and quality** of their act, or, if they did, did not know that it was **legally wrong**. 'Disease of the mind' is a legal, not purely medical, concept, and has been interpreted broadly to include any **internal** cause affecting the mind's functioning, even a physical illness -- *R v Kemp* [1957] 1 QB 399 (hardening of the arteries reducing blood flow to the brain, causing a temporary loss of consciousness during which the defendant attacked his wife, was a disease of the mind, since the cause was internal, not the external mechanism of the impairment that mattered); *R v Sullivan* [1984] AC 156 (epilepsy, causing a seizure during which the defendant injured someone, was a disease of the mind for the same reason -- an internal cause, however temporary its effect); *R v Hennessy* [1989] 1 WLR 287 (hyperglycaemia from a diabetic's failure to take insulin, an internal malfunction, was insanity). A successful insanity plea leads not to acquittal but to a **special verdict** ('not guilty by reason of insanity'), with disposal options including a hospital order. **Automatism** is a complete defence (a true acquittal) where the defendant's act was wholly involuntary, due to an **external** cause, with total loss of voluntary control -- *Bratty v Attorney-General for Northern Ireland* [1963] AC 386 defines automatism as an act done by the muscles without any control of the mind, such as a reflex or a spasm; contrast with insanity, an external cause keeps the defence as ordinary automatism rather than insanity -- *R v Quick* [1973] QB 910 (hypoglycaemia caused by taking prescribed insulin without eating enough, an external factor -- the insulin -- acting on the body, was automatism, not insanity, distinguishing *Hennessy*'s internal diabetic malfunction). **Self-induced automatism** (e.g. through voluntary intoxication, or knowingly failing to eat after taking insulin) generally does not excuse basic intent crimes, overlapping with the intoxication rules (DEF.2).
