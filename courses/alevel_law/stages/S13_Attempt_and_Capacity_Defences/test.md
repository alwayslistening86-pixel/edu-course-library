## Test items
1. Explain, with authority, why an internal cause and an external cause of a loss of mental control lead to different defences. [6 marks]
2. Priti breaks into a neighbour's garage at night and is caught by police standing over a set of house keys and a torch she has taken from a hook, having not yet touched the internal door to the house itself; she says she was planning to burgle the house once inside the garage gave her access. Using IRAC, advise on whether Priti has committed attempted burglary. [10 marks]

## Answer key (for the tutor only)
1. [6] B2 an internal cause (a condition originating within the defendant's own body/mind, e.g. epilepsy, diabetes-related hyperglycaemia, arteriosclerosis) that impairs the mind is treated as a 'disease of the mind' for insanity purposes, however temporary its effect (R v Sullivan [1984] AC 156; R v Hennessy [1989] 1 WLR 287; R v Kemp [1957] 1 QB 399); B2 an external cause (something acting on the body from outside, e.g. insulin taken but not followed by enough food, a blow to the head, or an anaesthetic) causing total loss of voluntary control instead supports ordinary automatism, a full acquittal (R v Quick [1973] QB 910); B2 the distinction matters because insanity leads only to a special verdict/hospital disposal, not a straightforward acquittal, unlike automatism.
2. [10] Mark using issue-rule-application-conclusion (IRAC): identify the issue(s), state the rule precisely with its authority (case or statute, named exactly), apply it to the facts given, and reach a supported conclusion. Credit: issue = whether Priti has gone beyond mere preparation for burglary; rule = s1 Criminal Attempts Act 1981 requires an act more than merely preparatory to the full offence, with intent to commit it; case law draws the line between equipping/positioning oneself (preparatory, e.g. R v Geddes [1996] Crim LR 894) and actually embarking on the offence (more than merely preparatory, e.g. R v Jones (Kenneth) [1990] 1 WLR 1057); application = taking the keys and torch to gain access is arguably still equipping/positioning herself within the garage, similar to Geddes, rather than having embarked on entering the house itself, which is the actual offence of burglary; conclusion = on these facts Priti has a reasonable argument she had not yet gone beyond mere preparation for burgling the house, though this is a fact-sensitive jury question and closer to the line than Geddes given she had already taken the keys needed for entry.
