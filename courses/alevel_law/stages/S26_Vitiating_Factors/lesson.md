# S26_Vitiating_Factors - Lesson: Misrepresentation; economic duress

## Goal
The learner explains the types, requirements and remedies for misrepresentation, and the requirements for economic duress.

## Syllabus items taught here
- VIT.1 - Vitiating factors: misrepresentation -- a false statement of fact inducing the contract; the categories (fraudulent, negligent under Misrepresentation Act 1967 s2(1), and innocent) and the remedies (rescission, damages) each attracts
- VIT.2 - Vitiating factors: economic duress -- illegitimate pressure that is a significant cause of entering the contract, leaving the victim no reasonable alternative, and the remedy of rescission

## How to teach this
Ask: a seller genuinely, honestly believes what they're telling a buyer, but it turns out to be false -- does 'I didn't lie on purpose' get them off the hook? Teach each rule as: the precise legal test (statute section or case authority, stated exactly, since AQA rewards named, accurate authority), why it exists (the policy AQA's own specification names, e.g. public policy limits on the duty of care, freedom of contract vs consumer protection), and how it applies to facts -- AQA's application and scenario questions (worth over half the marks on every paper) need the rule applied to the specific facts given, not restated in the abstract. Use AQA's own command words precisely: 'explain'/'suggest' target AO1 (accurate, precise legal knowledge); 'advise'/'consider' target AO1+AO2+AO3 together (apply the law to a scenario and reach a reasoned conclusion); 'examine'/'discuss'/'assess' target AO3 (evaluate non-substantive law, or substantive law within a non-substantive framework, e.g. discussing judicial precedent's advantages through a case scenario). Push the learner to build a bank of exact case names, years and the precise principle each establishes -- a vague 'there was a case about...' earns nothing under AQA's marking. Law changes: this course states the law of England and Wales as understood to be current in September 2026; statutory thresholds, ongoing reform and any pending legislation should be checked against a live source (legislation.gov.uk, aqa.org.uk) before a real exam sitting.

#### VIT.1 Vitiating factors: misrepresentation -- a false statement of fact inducing the contract; the categories (fraudulent, negligent under Misrepresentation Act 1967 s2(1), and innocent) and the remedies (rescission, damages) each attracts
**Misrepresentation.** A **false statement of fact** (not opinion, or mere sales talk/'puff'), made by one party to the other **before or at the time** the contract is formed, which **induces** the other party to enter the contract, rendering the contract **voidable** (not automatically void) at the innocent party's election. A statement of **opinion**, genuinely held, is not a statement of fact and generally cannot found misrepresentation (*Bisset v Wilkinson* [1927] AC 177 -- a seller's statement that land could support a certain number of sheep, when neither party had ever actually farmed sheep on it, was a genuine opinion, not a statement of fact); but an opinion given by a party with **special knowledge** the other lacks may be treated as an implied statement they had reasonable grounds for that opinion (*Smith v Land and House Property Corp* (1884) 28 Ch D 7 -- describing a tenant as "most desirable" when the seller knew the tenant was in fact seriously in arrears was an actionable implied misstatement of fact, since the seller was in a position of special knowledge). A statement of **future intention**, if the intention did not truly exist when stated, is itself a misrepresentation of present fact (about the maker's actual state of mind), not merely an unfulfilled promise (*Edgington v Fitzmaurice* (1885) 29 Ch D 459 -- stating loan proceeds would be used to expand a business, while actually intending to use them to pay off existing debts, was a false statement of present fact about the true intention held at the time). A statement true when made, but which becomes false before the contract is concluded, must be **corrected** -- a failure to update a statement known to have become false is itself a misrepresentation (*With v O'Flanagan* [1936] Ch 575). **Categories and remedies**: **fraudulent misrepresentation** -- a false statement made knowingly, or without belief in its truth, or recklessly as to whether it is true (*Derry v Peek* (1889) 14 App Cas 337, the definition of fraud) -- gives a right to **rescission** and damages in the tort of deceit; **negligent misrepresentation under s2(1) Misrepresentation Act 1967** -- a false statement made carelessly, without reasonable grounds for believing it true, with the burden placed on the **maker** to prove they had reasonable grounds (reversing the ordinary burden of proof) -- gives a right to rescission and/or damages, assessed (per *Royscot Trust Ltd v Rogerson* [1991] 2 QB 297) as if the misrepresentation were fraudulent, a notably claimant-friendly measure of damages; **innocent misrepresentation** (neither fraudulent nor negligent -- the maker had genuine, reasonable grounds for the statement) gives a right to rescission, with damages only at the court's discretion **in lieu** of rescission under s2(2) of the 1967 Act.

#### VIT.2 Vitiating factors: economic duress -- illegitimate pressure that is a significant cause of entering the contract, leaving the victim no reasonable alternative, and the remedy of rescission
**Economic duress.** A vitiating factor rendering a contract, or a variation to it, voidable where it was entered into or agreed to only because of **illegitimate economic pressure**, leaving the victim with **no reasonable practical alternative** but to submit. The leading formulation is from ***Universe Tankships Inc of Monrovia v International Transport Workers Federation* [1983] 1 AC 366**: the pressure must be **illegitimate** (not simply hard commercial bargaining, which is a normal, lawful feature of negotiations) and be a **significant cause** of the victim entering the contract/agreement. Illegitimate pressure is most clearly shown where the threat itself would be unlawful (e.g. a threatened breach of an existing contract, or unlawful industrial action), though pressure can in principle be illegitimate even where the specific threat is not itself unlawful, if it is otherwise an abuse of a dominant bargaining position (*Atlas Express Ltd v Kafco (Importers and Distributors) Ltd* [1989] QB 833 -- a haulage firm's threat to stop deliveries under an existing contract unless the customer, a small business with no realistic alternative supplier in time for the Christmas trading season, agreed to a large unilateral price increase was illegitimate economic pressure, the customer having genuinely no reasonable practical alternative but to agree). Relevant factors (drawn together in *Pao On v Lau Yiu Long* [1980] AC 614 and later refined in cases such as *DSND Subsea Ltd v Petroleum Geo Services ASA* [2000] BLR 530) include: whether the victim protested at the time; whether they had an alternative course of action realistically available (e.g. an alternative supplier, or the ability to sue for breach instead of submitting); whether they took independent legal advice; and whether, once free of the pressure, they took steps to avoid/rescind the agreement promptly. Where economic duress is established, the contract or variation is **voidable**, and the remedy is **rescission** -- setting the coerced agreement (or variation) aside, so the parties' original position/obligations are restored.
