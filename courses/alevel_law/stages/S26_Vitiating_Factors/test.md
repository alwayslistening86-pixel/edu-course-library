## Test items
1. Explain, with authority, why a statement of opinion is not ordinarily a misrepresentation, and one situation where it can still be treated as one. [6 marks]
2. A small independent bakery relies on a single specialist flour supplier under an existing supply contract. With two days' notice before its busiest trading week, the supplier says it will stop all deliveries unless the bakery agrees, immediately and without negotiation, to a 40% price increase for the rest of the contract term; the bakery, unable to source replacement flour of the right quality in time and fearing ruinous losses that week, agrees under protest. Using IRAC, advise the bakery on challenging the price increase. [10 marks]

## Answer key (for the tutor only)
1. [6] B2 an opinion, genuinely held, is not a statement of fact and so cannot ordinarily found misrepresentation (Bisset v Wilkinson [1927] AC 177); B2 but an opinion given by a party with special knowledge the other side lacks can be treated as an implied statement that the maker had reasonable grounds for the opinion, and so as a statement of fact if untrue (Smith v Land and House Property Corp (1884) 28 Ch D 7); B2 credit the further example of a statement of future intention that was never actually true when made, itself being a misrepresentation of present fact as to the maker's true state of mind (Edgington v Fitzmaurice (1885) 29 Ch D 459).
2. [10] Mark using issue-rule-application-conclusion (IRAC): identify the issue(s), state the rule precisely with its authority (case or statute, named exactly), apply it to the facts given, and reach a supported conclusion. Credit: issue = whether the price variation can be set aside for economic duress; rule = economic duress requires illegitimate pressure that is a significant cause of entering the agreement, leaving no reasonable practical alternative (Universe Tankships Inc of Monrovia v International Transport Workers Federation [1983] 1 AC 366); relevant factors include protest at the time, availability of an alternative, and independent advice (Pao On v Lau Yiu Long [1980] AC 614); a threat to breach an existing contract unless a unilateral price rise is accepted, where the victim has no realistic alternative supplier in time, is a clear example of illegitimate pressure (Atlas Express Ltd v Kafco (Importers and Distributors) Ltd [1989] QB 833); application = the supplier's threat to breach the existing supply contract, combined with the bakery's inability to source an alternative in time before its busiest week and the bakery's protest at the time, closely mirrors Atlas Express; conclusion = the bakery has a strong case that the price variation was procured by economic duress and is voidable, entitling it to rescind the variation and pay only the original contracted price, though it should be advised to formally communicate its objection/reservation of rights promptly (as it did by agreeing 'under protest') to strengthen the case that the agreement was not a genuine, freely negotiated variation.
