## Test items
1. Explain the four situations in which the law recognises a duty to act, so that an omission can form the actus reus of an offence. [8 marks]
2. Devi throws a dart at a target on a wall in a crowded pub, intending only to hit the target, but the dart misses and strikes Femi, standing nearby, in the eye. Using IRAC, advise on whether Devi's mens rea for a battery on the target-throwing (had it hit someone deliberately) can be used against her in relation to Femi. [10 marks]

## Answer key (for the tutor only)
1. [8] M2 contractual duty, e.g. R v Pittwood (1902) 19 TLR 37 (gatekeeper's contractual duty); M2 a duty voluntarily undertaken, e.g. R v Stone and Dobinson [1977] QB 354 (assumed responsibility for a vulnerable relative); M2 a duty arising because the defendant created a dangerous situation and failed to avert it once aware, e.g. R v Miller [1983] 2 AC 161 (fire started accidentally, no steps taken once aware); M2 a duty arising from a special/family relationship -- credit correct statement even without a named case.
2. [10] Mark using issue-rule-application-conclusion (IRAC): identify the issue(s), state the rule precisely with its authority (case or statute, named exactly), apply it to the facts given, and reach a supported conclusion. Credit: issue = whether Devi's intention can be attributed to the injury actually caused to Femi; rule = the doctrine of transferred malice allows mens rea aimed at one target to transfer where the actus reus of the same type of offence is committed against a different victim (R v Latimer (1886) 17 QBD 359); application = Devi had no intention to hit anyone with the dart at all (she intended to hit the target, an inanimate object), which is a different scenario to Latimer (where the defendant did intend to strike a person, just not that person) -- so there is a real argument the necessary mens rea for battery (intention or subjective recklessness as to unlawful force against a person) is missing altogether, though if Devi was reckless as to hitting a bystander given the crowded room, Cunningham recklessness could independently found liability; conclusion = transferred malice as such does not straightforwardly apply since no mens rea toward a person existed to transfer, but Devi may still be liable if she was subjectively reckless as to the risk of hitting someone in a crowded pub.
