# S07_General_Elements_of_Criminal_Liability - Lesson: Actus reus; mens rea, transferred malice, coincidence and strict liability

## Goal
The learner explains the actus reus and mens rea elements common to criminal offences -- conduct, omissions, causation, the different states of mens rea, transferred malice, coincidence, and strict liability -- with authority for each.

## Syllabus items taught here
- GE.1 - General elements of criminal liability: actus reus -- conduct, acts and omissions (and when a duty to act arises), state-of-affairs offences, voluntariness, causation (factual and legal, including intervening acts) and consequences
- GE.2 - General elements of criminal liability: mens rea -- direct and oblique intention, subjective (Cunningham) recklessness, negligence, transferred malice, the coincidence of actus reus and mens rea (continuing act/single transaction), and strict liability offences

## How to teach this
Ask: a lifeguard sees a swimmer drowning and does nothing -- can failing to act ever itself be a crime? Teach each rule as: the precise legal test (statute section or case authority, stated exactly, since AQA rewards named, accurate authority), why it exists (the policy AQA's own specification names, e.g. public policy limits on the duty of care, freedom of contract vs consumer protection), and how it applies to facts -- AQA's application and scenario questions (worth over half the marks on every paper) need the rule applied to the specific facts given, not restated in the abstract. Use AQA's own command words precisely: 'explain'/'suggest' target AO1 (accurate, precise legal knowledge); 'advise'/'consider' target AO1+AO2+AO3 together (apply the law to a scenario and reach a reasoned conclusion); 'examine'/'discuss'/'assess' target AO3 (evaluate non-substantive law, or substantive law within a non-substantive framework, e.g. discussing judicial precedent's advantages through a case scenario). Push the learner to build a bank of exact case names, years and the precise principle each establishes -- a vague 'there was a case about...' earns nothing under AQA's marking. Law changes: this course states the law of England and Wales as understood to be current in September 2026; statutory thresholds, ongoing reform and any pending legislation should be checked against a live source (legislation.gov.uk, aqa.org.uk) before a real exam sitting.

#### GE.1 General elements of criminal liability: actus reus -- conduct, acts and omissions (and when a duty to act arises), state-of-affairs offences, voluntariness, causation (factual and legal, including intervening acts) and consequences
**Actus reus.** The 'guilty act' -- the external, conduct element of an offence, which may be a positive **act**, an **omission** (a failure to act), a **state of affairs** (simply being in a defined situation, regardless of will, e.g. being found drunk in a public place), and for result crimes must be shown to have **caused** the prohibited consequence. Actus reus must generally be **voluntary**: an involuntary act (e.g. a reflex, or an act during automatism) is not a true actus reus at all. **Omissions**: English law has no general duty to act to prevent harm, but liability can arise from an omission where a specific **duty to act** exists: a **contractual duty** (*R v Pittwood* (1902) 19 TLR 37 -- a gatekeeper's failure to close a level-crossing gate, contrary to his contract, caused a death); a duty arising from a **special/family relationship**; a duty **voluntarily undertaken** (*R v Stone and Dobinson* [1977] QB 354 -- once the defendants took on caring for a vulnerable relative, their failure to get help as her condition worsened was a breach of that assumed duty); and a duty arising because the **defendant created a dangerous situation** and failed to act to avert it once aware of the danger (*R v Miller* [1983] 2 AC 161 -- a squatter who accidentally started a fire and, on waking, simply moved to another room instead of raising the alarm or trying to put it out, was liable for arson by omission). **Causation** (for result crimes): **factual causation** uses the **"but for" test** -- but for the defendant's act, would the result have occurred when it did? (*R v White* [1910] 2 KB 124 -- a son who poisoned his mother's drink was not the factual cause of her death, since she died of an unrelated heart attack before the poison could act, so he was liable only for attempted murder). **Legal causation** requires the defendant's act to be an **operating and substantial cause**, not broken by a **novus actus interveniens** (a new intervening act breaking the chain) -- a free, deliberate and informed act by a third party will usually break the chain (*R v Pagett* (1983) 76 Cr App R 279 -- using a hostage as a shield, provoking police to fire, did not break the chain since the police response was a natural, foreseeable consequence, not an independent free act), while medical treatment will rarely break the chain unless it is so independent of the defendant's act and so potent in causing death that it makes the defendant's contribution insignificant (*R v Cheshire* [1991] 1 WLR 844 -- negligent medical treatment for a gunshot wound did not break the chain, since the original wound remained an operating and significant cause of death). The **"thin skull" rule** requires the defendant to take the victim as they find them, including any pre-existing vulnerability (*R v Blaue* [1975] 1 WLR 1411 -- a victim who, as a Jehovah's Witness, refused a blood transfusion that would have saved her life after being stabbed did not break the chain of causation; the defendant had to take his victim, including her genuinely held beliefs, as he found her).

#### GE.2 General elements of criminal liability: mens rea -- direct and oblique intention, subjective (Cunningham) recklessness, negligence, transferred malice, the coincidence of actus reus and mens rea (continuing act/single transaction), and strict liability offences
**Mens rea, transferred malice, coincidence, and strict liability.** **Mens rea** is the 'guilty mind' -- the mental element an offence requires, which varies by offence: **direct intention** (the defendant's aim or purpose was the prohibited result) and **oblique intention** (the defendant did not desire the result but it was a virtually certain consequence of their act, which they appreciated -- the jury may (not must) find intention where the result was a virtual certainty and the defendant appreciated that, per ***R v Woollin* [1999] 1 AC 82**, refining the earlier guidance in *R v Nedrick* [1986] 1 WLR 1025). **Subjective (Cunningham) recklessness** requires the defendant to have foreseen a risk of the prohibited harm and unjustifiably gone on to take it (*R v Cunningham* [1957] 2 QB 396 -- tearing a gas meter from a wall to steal money from it, without turning off the gas supply first, showed recklessness as to whether the escaping gas might endanger someone, since the risk was one Cunningham himself must be shown to have foreseen). **Negligence** (the objective failure to meet the standard of a reasonable person) is the mens rea for a smaller number of offences (e.g. gross negligence manslaughter, FO.4). **Transferred malice**: if a defendant intends to commit an offence against one victim/target but in fact commits the *same type* of offence against a different victim, the mens rea transfers (*R v Latimer* (1886) 17 QBD 359 -- a blow aimed with a belt at one man that rebounded and struck a woman standing nearby transferred the defendant's malice, making him liable for the injury to her); malice cannot transfer between fundamentally *different* types of crime (*Attorney-General's Reference (No 3 of 1994)* [1998] AC 245 -- intent to harm a pregnant woman could not transfer to found murder of her child born alive and later dying of the injuries, since murder and the assault on the mother were not the same type of offence, though it could found unlawful act manslaughter instead). **Coincidence of actus reus and mens rea**: they must generally exist at the same moment, but the courts treat a course of conduct as continuing, so mens rea formed partway through an ongoing act can coincide with it (*Fagan v Metropolitan Police Commissioner* [1969] 1 QB 439 -- driving onto a police officer's foot by accident, then deliberately refusing to move the car once aware, was treated as one continuing act, so the actus reus (the car remaining on the foot) and the mens rea (formed once aware) coincided). **Strict liability offences** require no proof of mens rea for at least one element of the actus reus (commonly regulatory offences, e.g. food safety, licensing) -- courts start from a presumption that mens rea is required even where a statute is silent (*Sweet v Parsley* [1970] AC 132), displaced only where factors set out in *Gammon (Hong Kong) Ltd v Attorney-General of Hong Kong* [1985] AC 1 point the other way (the offence is truly regulatory rather than 'truly criminal', clear statutory wording, and strict liability would promote the statute's public-protection purpose, e.g. by encouraging greater vigilance).
