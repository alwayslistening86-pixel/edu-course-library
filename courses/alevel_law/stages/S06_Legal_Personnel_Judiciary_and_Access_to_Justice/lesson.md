# S06_Legal_Personnel_Judiciary_and_Access_to_Justice - Lesson: Legal personnel and the judiciary; access to justice and funding

## Goal
The learner identifies the legal professions and their regulation, the types and role of judges and why judicial independence matters, and the routes to legal advice and funding available to someone who cannot easily afford a lawyer.

## Syllabus items taught here
- LP.1 - Legal personnel and the judiciary: the roles of barristers, solicitors and legal executives and how they are regulated; types of judge; the role of judges in civil and criminal courts; judicial independence -- security of tenure, immunity from suit, independence from the executive -- why it matters and how it is secured
- AJ.1 - Access to justice and funding: alternative sources of legal advice (helplines, Citizens Advice, law centres, trade unions); private funding (own resources, insurance, conditional fee agreements); public funding for criminal and civil matters

## How to teach this
Ask: a litigant can't afford a solicitor and the case is too complex for the small claims track -- what routes to help or funding might still exist? Teach each rule as: the precise legal test (statute section or case authority, stated exactly, since AQA rewards named, accurate authority), why it exists (the policy AQA's own specification names, e.g. public policy limits on the duty of care, freedom of contract vs consumer protection), and how it applies to facts -- AQA's application and scenario questions (worth over half the marks on every paper) need the rule applied to the specific facts given, not restated in the abstract. Use AQA's own command words precisely: 'explain'/'suggest' target AO1 (accurate, precise legal knowledge); 'advise'/'consider' target AO1+AO2+AO3 together (apply the law to a scenario and reach a reasoned conclusion); 'examine'/'discuss'/'assess' target AO3 (evaluate non-substantive law, or substantive law within a non-substantive framework, e.g. discussing judicial precedent's advantages through a case scenario). Push the learner to build a bank of exact case names, years and the precise principle each establishes -- a vague 'there was a case about...' earns nothing under AQA's marking. Law changes: this course states the law of England and Wales as understood to be current in September 2026; statutory thresholds, ongoing reform and any pending legislation should be checked against a live source (legislation.gov.uk, aqa.org.uk) before a real exam sitting.

#### LP.1 Legal personnel and the judiciary: the roles of barristers, solicitors and legal executives and how they are regulated; types of judge; the role of judges in civil and criminal courts; judicial independence -- security of tenure, immunity from suit, independence from the executive -- why it matters and how it is secured
**Legal personnel.** **Solicitors** (regulated by the Solicitors Regulation Authority) typically have broad initial training across areas of law before specialising, advise clients directly, conduct litigation and increasingly exercise rights of audience in court; **barristers** (regulated by the Bar Standards Board) traditionally specialise in advocacy and specialist advisory work, historically instructed by solicitors though direct access by the public is increasingly available; **Chartered Legal Executives** (regulated by CILEX Regulation) are qualified lawyers who specialise from an early stage in a chosen area of law, able (once fully qualified) to undertake work equivalent to a solicitor in that specialism. **Types of judge**, broadly senior to junior: **Justices of the Supreme Court**; **Lords/Lady Justices of Appeal** (Court of Appeal); **High Court Judges**; **Circuit Judges** (Crown Court and higher County Court work); **Recorders** (part-time, mainly Crown Court); **District Judges** (County Court and Magistrates' Court case management and trials). **The role of judges**: in civil courts, judges manage cases, determine facts and apply the law to reach a judgment, and (in the higher courts) develop the law through precedent; in criminal courts, judges (in the Crown Court) rule on law and procedure, sum up the evidence and direct the jury on the law, and pass sentence following conviction. **Judicial independence** -- freedom from improper influence by the executive, the legislature or the parties -- is protected by: **security of tenure** (senior judges can be removed only by a very demanding process, e.g. for Supreme Court/senior judges, on an address by both Houses of Parliament, insulating them from executive displeasure at unpopular decisions); **immunity from suit** (a judge cannot be sued for things said or decided in the course of judicial duties, so they can decide difficult, controversial cases without fear of personal liability, illustrated by the general common law principle recognised in *Sirros v Moore* [1975] QB 118); and **separation from the executive** (since the Constitutional Reform Act 2005, the Lord Chancellor's historic judicial functions and the House of Lords' former appellate role were removed, creating the institutionally separate Supreme Court, and an independent Judicial Appointments Commission now leads judicial selection). Judicial independence matters because it underpins the rule of law: a judiciary that could be pressured or dismissed for unpopular decisions could not be relied on to hold the government to the law, or to decide disputes (including disputes involving the state itself) impartially.

#### AJ.1 Access to justice and funding: alternative sources of legal advice (helplines, Citizens Advice, law centres, trade unions); private funding (own resources, insurance, conditional fee agreements); public funding for criminal and civil matters
**Access to justice and funding.** Beyond instructing a solicitor or barrister directly, a person can seek **alternative sources of legal advice**: **helplines** (e.g. specialist charity or union advice lines), the **Citizens Advice Bureau** (free, general advice, including some casework help and referral), **law centres** (not-for-profit centres offering free legal advice/representation, often focused on social welfare law in a local area), and **trade unions** (offering legal advice and representation to members, particularly in employment matters). **Private funding**: a client's **own resources** (paying privately, by the hour or a fixed fee); **legal expenses insurance** (before-the-event insurance, e.g. as part of a home or motor policy, covering the cost of a future claim); and **conditional fee agreements ("no win, no fee")**, where the lawyer's fee (often with an agreed uplift/"success fee" if the case succeeds, subject to statutory caps) is payable only if the case succeeds, giving access to representation without upfront cost but with the litigant often needing "after the event" insurance to cover the risk of an adverse costs order. **Public funding**: for criminal matters, funded through the Legal Aid Agency, available (subject to means and, for some proceedings, interests-of-justice tests) so that a defendant facing serious criminal charges is not denied a fair trial for want of funds, reflecting the rule of law's requirement of genuine access to justice; for civil matters, public funding (legal aid) is now available only for a narrower range of case types than in the past (e.g. some family cases involving domestic abuse, and certain other categories defined by statute and regulation), with most civil litigants relying instead on private funding, CFAs or pro bono/charitable help. The narrowing of civil legal aid since the Legal Aid, Sentencing and Punishment of Offenders Act 2012 is frequently cited as a genuine access-to-justice/rule-of-law concern, since it can leave people with a good legal claim unable, in practice, to bring or defend it.
