## Test items
1. Explain why specific performance was refused in Cohen v Roche [1927] 1 KB 169 but might be available for a rare, one-of-a-kind item, with authority for the latter. [6 marks]
2. A specialist software house breaches a contract to deliver a bespoke stock-management system to a retailer by the agreed date, knowing (because the retailer had explained this in detail during negotiations) that the retailer had a specific, unusually large seasonal contract with one major customer that depended entirely on the new system being ready in time, and that this contract would be lost if the system was late. Using IRAC, advise on what loss the retailer can recover in damages. [10 marks]

## Answer key (for the tutor only)
1. [6] B2 specific performance is only available where damages would be an inadequate remedy, typically where the subject matter is unique/not readily obtainable elsewhere; B2 in Cohen v Roche, ordinary Hepplewhite chairs, though not identical, were of a kind reasonably available on the open market, so damages (funding an equivalent purchase) were an adequate remedy, and specific performance was refused; B2 for a genuinely unique item with no real substitute (e.g. rare china, Falcke v Gray (1859) 4 Drew 651), damages could not put the claimant in as good a position as actual performance, so specific performance is more likely to be granted.
2. [10] Mark using issue-rule-application-conclusion (IRAC): identify the issue(s), state the rule precisely with its authority (case or statute, named exactly), apply it to the facts given, and reach a supported conclusion. Credit: issue = whether the retailer can recover the lost profit on the specific seasonal contract, not just ordinary business disruption; rule = damages are recoverable only if not too remote, under the two limbs of Hadley v Baxendale (1854) 9 Exch 341 -- loss arising naturally, or loss actually within the parties' contemplation at the time of contracting given special knowledge the defendant had; the second-limb principle was applied in Victoria Laundry (Windsor) Ltd v Newman Industries Ltd [1949] 2 KB 528 (an unusually lucrative specific contract, not disclosed at the time, was not recoverable, unlike ordinary foreseeable lost business use); the test is whether the loss was 'not unlikely'/a serious possibility, not simply reasonably foreseeable (Koufos v C Czarnikow Ltd (The Heron II) [1969] 1 AC 350); application = unlike the undisclosed specific contract in Victoria Laundry, here the software house was expressly told about the specific, unusually large seasonal contract during negotiations, giving it the special knowledge the second limb of Hadley requires; conclusion = the retailer can likely recover the lost profit on the specific seasonal contract as well as ordinary consequential loss, since the software house had the special knowledge needed to bring that loss within its reasonable contemplation at the time of contracting, distinguishing this from Victoria Laundry's undisclosed exceptional contract.
