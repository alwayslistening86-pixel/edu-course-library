# S28_Contract_Remedies - Lesson: Damages; equitable remedies

## Goal
The learner explains compensatory damages (recoverable loss, remoteness and mitigation), and the equitable remedies of specific performance and rescission and why they are discretionary.

## Syllabus items taught here
- CREM.1 - Contract remedies: compensatory damages -- the categories of recoverable loss, causation, remoteness (the rule in Hadley v Baxendale) and the claimant's duty to mitigate loss
- CREM.2 - Contract remedies: equitable remedies -- specific performance and rescission, and why they are discretionary and subject to equitable bars (delay, a claimant not coming 'with clean hands', third-party rights)

## How to teach this
Ask: a builder finishes a swimming pool at the wrong (but still usable) depth -- should damages be the huge cost of digging it up and rebuilding, or something smaller? Teach each rule as: the precise legal test (statute section or case authority, stated exactly, since AQA rewards named, accurate authority), why it exists (the policy AQA's own specification names, e.g. public policy limits on the duty of care, freedom of contract vs consumer protection), and how it applies to facts -- AQA's application and scenario questions (worth over half the marks on every paper) need the rule applied to the specific facts given, not restated in the abstract. Use AQA's own command words precisely: 'explain'/'suggest' target AO1 (accurate, precise legal knowledge); 'advise'/'consider' target AO1+AO2+AO3 together (apply the law to a scenario and reach a reasoned conclusion); 'examine'/'discuss'/'assess' target AO3 (evaluate non-substantive law, or substantive law within a non-substantive framework, e.g. discussing judicial precedent's advantages through a case scenario). Push the learner to build a bank of exact case names, years and the precise principle each establishes -- a vague 'there was a case about...' earns nothing under AQA's marking. Law changes: this course states the law of England and Wales as understood to be current in September 2026; statutory thresholds, ongoing reform and any pending legislation should be checked against a live source (legislation.gov.uk, aqa.org.uk) before a real exam sitting.

#### CREM.1 Contract remedies: compensatory damages -- the categories of recoverable loss, causation, remoteness (the rule in Hadley v Baxendale) and the claimant's duty to mitigate loss
**Contract remedies: compensatory damages.** The default remedy for breach of contract, aiming to put the claimant, so far as money can, in the position they would have been in **had the contract been properly performed** (the "expectation" measure -- notably different in aim from tort damages, which look back to the pre-tort position rather than forward to the promised position). Recoverable loss must not be too **remote**: the rule from ***Hadley v Baxendale* (1854) 9 Exch 341** allows recovery only for loss arising **naturally**, in the ordinary course of things, from the breach (the "first limb"), or loss the parties **actually contemplated**, at the time of contracting, as a probable result of the breach, given any special knowledge the defendant had (the "second limb") -- in Hadley itself, a mill's lost profit from a broken crankshaft, delayed in transit by the defendant carrier, was not recoverable, since the carrier had no reason to know the mill would be completely stopped (rather than having a spare) while the shaft was away for repair. The second limb was applied in *Victoria Laundry (Windsor) Ltd v Newman Industries Ltd* [1949] 2 KB 528 (lost profit from a late-delivered boiler was recoverable for ordinary lost business use, since that was reasonably foreseeable, but not for the loss of a specific, exceptionally lucrative dyeing contract the defendant did not know about), and the remoteness test itself was refined by the House of Lords in *Koufos v C Czarnikow Ltd (The Heron II)* [1969] 1 AC 350 (the correct question is not simply whether loss was "reasonably foreseeable" as in tort, but the narrower contractual test of whether it was **"not unlikely"**/a **serious possibility**, reflecting that contract damages depend on what the parties are taken to have had in their contemplation when they made their bargain, not simply what a reasonable person might foresee after the event). Damages can, in an appropriate case, compensate for **loss of amenity/enjoyment** even where the cost of literally curing a defect would be wholly disproportionate to the benefit gained (*Ruxley Electronics and Construction Ltd v Forsyth* [1996] AC 344 -- a swimming pool built shallower than specified, but still perfectly safe and usable, did not justify the enormous cost of demolishing and rebuilding it to the exact contracted depth, since that cost was wholly disproportionate to any real benefit; the claimant instead recovered a more modest sum for loss of amenity/general disappointment). The claimant is under a **duty to mitigate** their loss (broadly the same principle as in tort, see REM.1) -- they cannot recover for losses they could reasonably have avoided.

#### CREM.2 Contract remedies: equitable remedies -- specific performance and rescission, and why they are discretionary and subject to equitable bars (delay, a claimant not coming 'with clean hands', third-party rights)
**Contract remedies: equitable remedies.** Unlike damages (available as of right for an established breach), equitable remedies are **discretionary**, granted only where damages would be an **inadequate** remedy, and subject to established **equitable bars/maxims** (e.g. a claimant who has themselves acted improperly, delayed unreasonably, or who did not act "with clean hands" may be refused the remedy). **Specific performance** orders the defendant actually to perform their contractual obligations -- typically available only where the subject matter is **unique** or otherwise not readily obtainable elsewhere, so damages (which could simply fund buying a substitute) would not adequately compensate the claimant (*Falcke v Gray* (1859) 4 Drew 651, illustrating specific performance's availability for genuinely unique items, e.g. rare china, where no substitute of equivalent value/character could be bought on the open market); it is **not** available for ordinary, freely available goods, where damages (sufficient to buy an equivalent elsewhere) are an adequate remedy (*Cohen v Roche* [1927] 1 KB 169 -- ordinary Hepplewhite chairs, freely available on the market even if not identical pieces, did not justify specific performance, since money damages could simply fund buying comparable replacements), and it is never granted to compel **personal service** (a court will not force one person to work for, or supervise ongoing personal performance by, another, since this would be both practically difficult to enforce and akin to compelled labour, *De Francesco v Barnum* (1890) 45 Ch D 430, refusing to compel a young performer's contract of personal service to be specifically performed) -- though a court may instead grant a negative **injunction** restraining the defendant from working for a competitor in breach of an exclusivity term, without directly compelling the personal service itself (*Warner Bros Pictures Inc v Nelson* [1937] 1 KB 209 -- an actress in breach of an exclusive services contract could be restrained by injunction from working for a rival film studio, even though she could not be compelled to perform for Warner Bros itself). **Rescission** sets the contract aside, restoring the parties (so far as possible) to their pre-contract positions -- the primary remedy for misrepresentation (VIT.1) as well as for other vitiating factors such as economic duress (VIT.2). Rescission is barred (i.e., no longer available) where: **restitution is impossible** (the parties cannot practically be restored to their original positions, e.g. the subject matter has been consumed or fundamentally changed); a **third party has acquired rights** in the property in good faith for value in the meantime; the innocent party has **affirmed** the contract after discovering the truth; or there has been **excessive delay/lapse of time** (*Leaf v International Galleries* [1950] 2 KB 86 -- five years' delay in seeking to rescind a contract for an innocently misdescribed painting, after the innocent misrepresentation was discovered, was fatal to a claim for rescission, the court treating such prolonged delay as itself a bar even though no third-party rights had arisen).
