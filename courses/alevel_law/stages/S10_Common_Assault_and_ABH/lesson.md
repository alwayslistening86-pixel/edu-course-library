# S10_Common_Assault_and_ABH - Lesson: Common assault (assault and battery); assault occasioning ABH

## Goal
The learner explains common assault (assault and battery) and assault occasioning actual bodily harm under s47 OAPA 1861, including the meaning of 'occasioning' and what counts as bodily harm.

## Syllabus items taught here
- NFO.1 - Non-fatal offences: common assault -- assault and battery at common law
- NFO.2 - Non-fatal offences: assault occasioning actual bodily harm, s47 Offences Against the Person Act 1861

## How to teach this
Ask: is making a threatening phone call, with no physical contact at all, capable of being a criminal assault? Teach each rule as: the precise legal test (statute section or case authority, stated exactly, since AQA rewards named, accurate authority), why it exists (the policy AQA's own specification names, e.g. public policy limits on the duty of care, freedom of contract vs consumer protection), and how it applies to facts -- AQA's application and scenario questions (worth over half the marks on every paper) need the rule applied to the specific facts given, not restated in the abstract. Use AQA's own command words precisely: 'explain'/'suggest' target AO1 (accurate, precise legal knowledge); 'advise'/'consider' target AO1+AO2+AO3 together (apply the law to a scenario and reach a reasoned conclusion); 'examine'/'discuss'/'assess' target AO3 (evaluate non-substantive law, or substantive law within a non-substantive framework, e.g. discussing judicial precedent's advantages through a case scenario). Push the learner to build a bank of exact case names, years and the precise principle each establishes -- a vague 'there was a case about...' earns nothing under AQA's marking. Law changes: this course states the law of England and Wales as understood to be current in September 2026; statutory thresholds, ongoing reform and any pending legislation should be checked against a live source (legislation.gov.uk, aqa.org.uk) before a real exam sitting.

#### NFO.1 Non-fatal offences: common assault -- assault and battery at common law
**Common assault: assault and battery.** Two separate common law offences, often charged together as 'common assault' but legally and conceptually distinct. **Assault** is causing the victim to apprehend immediate unlawful force (no touching is required) -- *Collins v Wilcock* [1984] 1 WLR 1172 sets out the basic principle that any unwanted touching, or the apprehension of it, is prima facie unlawful. Words alone can constitute assault (*R v Ireland; R v Burstow* [1998] AC 147 -- silent telephone calls could amount to assault, since they could cause the victim to apprehend immediate unlawful violence, even without a word spoken), and can also be conveyed in writing where they create an apprehension of immediate force (*R v Constanza* [1997] 2 Cr App R 492 -- a campaign of threatening letters could found assault given the immediacy of the fear they created). Conversely, clearly conditional or future threats, or words that make clear no violence will follow, can negate assault (*Tuberville v Savage* (1669) -- 'if it were not assize time I would not take such language' negated the apprehension of *immediate* force). **Battery** is the actual infliction of unlawful force on another, however slight (no injury need result) -- deliberately or recklessly applied. The actus reus and mens rea must coincide, but the law treats a continuing act as satisfying this even where the mens rea forms after the act begins (*Fagan v Metropolitan Police Commissioner* [1969] 1 QB 439, GE.2), and an omission can found battery where the defendant created the dangerous situation, e.g. by failing to warn of a known risk once aware of it (*DPP v Santana-Bermudez* [2003] EWHC 2908 (Admin) -- failing to tell a police officer conducting a search about a concealed hypodermic needle, causing injury, was battery).

#### NFO.2 Non-fatal offences: assault occasioning actual bodily harm, s47 Offences Against the Person Act 1861
**Assault occasioning actual bodily harm, s47 Offences Against the Person Act 1861.** Actus reus: an assault or battery (as defined above) that **occasions** (causes) **actual bodily harm**. 'Actual bodily harm' is any injury calculated to interfere with the health or comfort of the victim -- it need not be permanent, but must be more than merely transient and trifling (*R v Chan-Fook* [1994] 1 WLR 689, which also confirmed that a recognised **psychiatric injury** (not mere emotion such as fear, distress or panic) can amount to ABH, later approved by the House of Lords in *R v Ireland; R v Burstow* [1998] AC 147, where the sustained psychiatric harm caused by silent calls satisfied ABH/GBH). Mens rea: only the mens rea for the underlying assault or battery is required -- intention or (Cunningham) subjective recklessness as to causing the victim to apprehend or suffer unlawful force; the defendant need **not** additionally intend or foresee the actual bodily harm itself (*R v Savage; DPP v Parmenter* [1992] 1 AC 699, the leading case confirming that s47's mens rea requirement stops at the assault/battery level, with the resulting harm assessed objectively/by causation rather than by the defendant's foresight of it). This makes s47 a **constructive liability offence**: the defendant is liable for the more serious harm (ABH) on proof of only the lesser mens rea (for a simple assault/battery), which is a recurring criticism raised in AQA's essay/discursive questions on whether the non-fatal offences achieve justice.
