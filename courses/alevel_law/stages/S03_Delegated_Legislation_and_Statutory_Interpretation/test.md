## Test items
1. Explain the difference between the literal rule and the mischief rule of statutory interpretation, with a case example for each. [8 marks]
2. A newly enabled statutory instrument, made under an Act only empowering the minister to set 'administrative filing fees' for a licensing scheme, is found to introduce a new criminal offence for late filing. Using IRAC, advise a trade body that wants to challenge the instrument. [10 marks]

## Answer key (for the tutor only)
1. [8] M2 literal rule: words given their plain, ordinary, grammatical meaning even if the result seems absurd; M2 case example, e.g. Fisher v Bell [1961] 1 QB 394 (shop window display of a flick knife held not an 'offer for sale' on the literal wording); M2 mischief rule: the court asks what gap/mischief the statute was passed to remedy and interprets it to suppress that mischief, from Heydon's Case; M2 case example, e.g. Smith v Hughes [1960] 1 WLR 830 (soliciting visible from a window caught, since the mischief -- visible street solicitation -- was the same).
2. [10] IRAC (issue-rule-application-conclusion). Credit: issue = whether the instrument can be challenged as ultra vires; rule = delegated legislation must remain within the scope of the power granted by the enabling Act, and content going beyond that scope is substantively ultra vires (Attorney-General v Wilts United Dairies Ltd (1922) 37 TLR 884); application = creating a new criminal offence is well outside a power limited to setting 'administrative filing fees', so the instrument's content exceeds the enabling power; conclusion = the trade body has a strong basis to argue the instrument is substantively ultra vires and void to that extent.
