# S03_Delegated_Legislation_and_Statutory_Interpretation - Lesson: Delegated legislation; statutory interpretation

## Goal
The learner explains the types, justification, and parliamentary/judicial control of delegated legislation (including ultra vires), and the rules and aids courts use to interpret statutes, with their advantages and disadvantages.

## Syllabus items taught here
- DL.1 - Delegated legislation: types (Orders in Council, statutory instruments, by-laws); reasons for its use; parliamentary and judicial controls (including substantive and procedural ultra vires); advantages and disadvantages
- SI.1 - Statutory interpretation: the literal, golden, mischief and purposive approaches; intrinsic and extrinsic aids; the impact of the Human Rights Act 1998 and retained EU law principles on interpretation; advantages and disadvantages of each approach

## How to teach this
Ask: a minister makes detailed regulations under a power Parliament granted, but the regulations go further than the Act allows -- what can stop that, and how? Teach each rule as: the precise legal test (statute section or case authority, stated exactly, since AQA rewards named, accurate authority), why it exists (the policy AQA's own specification names, e.g. public policy limits on the duty of care, freedom of contract vs consumer protection), and how it applies to facts -- AQA's application and scenario questions (worth over half the marks on every paper) need the rule applied to the specific facts given, not restated in the abstract. Use AQA's own command words precisely: 'explain'/'suggest' target AO1 (accurate, precise legal knowledge); 'advise'/'consider' target AO1+AO2+AO3 together (apply the law to a scenario and reach a reasoned conclusion); 'examine'/'discuss'/'assess' target AO3 (evaluate non-substantive law, or substantive law within a non-substantive framework, e.g. discussing judicial precedent's advantages through a case scenario). Push the learner to build a bank of exact case names, years and the precise principle each establishes -- a vague 'there was a case about...' earns nothing under AQA's marking. Law changes: this course states the law of England and Wales as understood to be current in September 2026; statutory thresholds, ongoing reform and any pending legislation should be checked against a live source (legislation.gov.uk, aqa.org.uk) before a real exam sitting.

#### DL.1 Delegated legislation: types (Orders in Council, statutory instruments, by-laws); reasons for its use; parliamentary and judicial controls (including substantive and procedural ultra vires); advantages and disadvantages
**Delegated legislation.** Law made under authority delegated by an enabling (parent) Act, without needing a full separate Act each time. **Types**: **Orders in Council** (made by the Privy Council, used for significant/urgent matters, e.g. transferring functions between departments, or in emergencies); **statutory instruments** (the most common type, made by government ministers/departments under a specific enabling Act, covering detailed/technical rules such as bringing an Act's provisions into force or setting regulatory detail); **by-laws** (made by local authorities or certain public bodies for their own area/undertaking, e.g. local parking rules). **Reasons for use**: saves parliamentary time for major policy debate; allows technical/specialist input that Parliament as a generalist body lacks; allows quick updating or response to changing circumstances (e.g. emergency regulations) without a full Bill. **Parliamentary control**: the enabling Act may require an instrument to be laid before Parliament under the **affirmative resolution procedure** (it lapses unless both Houses actively approve it) or the **negative resolution procedure** (it becomes law unless either House objects within a set period); the **Joint Committee on Statutory Instruments** scrutinises instruments for technical defects (e.g. exceeding power, unclear drafting) and reports to Parliament, though it cannot itself block one. **Judicial control**: delegated legislation can be challenged as **ultra vires** -- **substantive ultra vires** (the content exceeds the scope the enabling Act granted, e.g. *Attorney-General v Wilts United Dairies Ltd* (1922) 37 TLR 884, a levy imposed without the necessary statutory authority was struck down) or **procedural ultra vires** (a required procedure, such as consultation, was not followed, e.g. *Agricultural, Horticultural and Forestry Industry Training Board v Aylesbury Mushrooms Ltd* [1972] 1 WLR 190, a failure to consult as the enabling Act required made the order not binding on the claimant). **Advantages**: speed and flexibility; technical expertise; relieves parliamentary time pressure. **Disadvantages**: less parliamentary scrutiny and debate than primary legislation, particularly under the negative procedure; large volume makes effective oversight difficult; risk of sub-delegation reducing democratic accountability further.

#### SI.1 Statutory interpretation: the literal, golden, mischief and purposive approaches; intrinsic and extrinsic aids; the impact of the Human Rights Act 1998 and retained EU law principles on interpretation; advantages and disadvantages of each approach
**Statutory interpretation.** Because legislative drafting cannot anticipate every situation (broad/ambiguous wording, drafting errors, new circumstances, or a wide term used deliberately), courts apply established rules/approaches: the **literal rule** gives words their plain, ordinary, grammatical meaning even if the result seems absurd (*Fisher v Bell* [1961] 1 QB 394 -- displaying a flick knife in a shop window was, on the plain wording, an invitation to treat, not an "offer for sale", so no offence was committed under the Act as literally worded; *Whiteley v Chappell* (1868) LR 4 QB 147 -- a defendant impersonating a dead person to vote was acquitted because a dead person, literally read, is not "entitled to vote"). The **golden rule** (narrow and wide) allows a court to depart from the literal meaning only far enough to avoid an absurd or repugnant result (*Re Sigsworth* [1935] Ch 89 -- a son who murdered his mother was denied intestacy inheritance despite literal wording entitling him, since Parliament could not have intended a murderer to profit). The **mischief rule** (from *Heydon's Case* (1584)) asks what mischief/gap in the law the statute was passed to remedy, and interprets it to suppress that mischief (*Smith v Hughes* [1960] 1 WLR 830 -- prostitutes soliciting from windows were caught by an Act aimed at solicitation "in a street", since the mischief -- public nuisance from soliciting visible to passers-by -- existed regardless of exactly where the soliciting occurred; *Royal College of Nursing v DHSS* [1981] AC 800 -- nurses performing part of an abortion procedure were held to act "by a registered medical practitioner" as the mischief the Act addressed, safe legal abortion, was served). The **purposive approach** goes further, asking what Parliament's underlying purpose was (not just the specific mischief) and interpreting accordingly, encouraged especially where EU-derived or Human-Rights-Act-affected legislation is involved (*Jones v Tower Boot Co Ltd* [1997] 2 All ER 406 -- "in the course of employment" for vicarious liability under discrimination legislation was read purposively, broadly, to give effect to Parliament's protective purpose). **Aids to interpretation**: **intrinsic (internal) aids** within the Act itself (the long title, preamble, interpretation/definition sections, schedules); **extrinsic (external) aids** outside the Act (dictionaries, earlier case law, other statutes on the same subject, and -- since *Pepper v Hart* [1993] AC 593 -- Hansard (the official record of parliamentary debates), which may be consulted where legislation is ambiguous/obscure and a clear ministerial statement addresses the very point). The **Human Rights Act 1998 s3** requires legislation to be interpreted compatibly with Convention rights so far as possible, and retained EU law principles (e.g. purposive interpretation of legislation implementing former EU obligations) continue to influence interpretation post-Brexit. **Advantages/disadvantages**: the literal rule promotes certainty and respects parliamentary sovereignty but can produce absurd or unjust results; the golden and mischief rules and the purposive approach can avoid absurdity and better serve Parliament's real intention, but at the cost of certainty and by handing unelected judges more interpretive discretion.
