# S12_Theft_and_Robbery - Lesson: Theft; robbery

## Goal
The learner explains the five elements of theft under s1 Theft Act 1968 and how robbery builds on theft by adding force or the threat of force under s8 Theft Act 1968.

## Syllabus items taught here
- PO.1 - Property offences: theft, s1 Theft Act 1968 (appropriation, property, belonging to another, dishonesty, intention permanently to deprive)
- PO.2 - Property offences: robbery, s8 Theft Act 1968 (theft plus force or the threat of force, immediately before or at the time of stealing, in order to steal)

## How to teach this
Ask: a customer switches a price label in a shop before paying the lower price at the till -- is that theft, even though they did pay something? Teach each rule as: the precise legal test (statute section or case authority, stated exactly, since AQA rewards named, accurate authority), why it exists (the policy AQA's own specification names, e.g. public policy limits on the duty of care, freedom of contract vs consumer protection), and how it applies to facts -- AQA's application and scenario questions (worth over half the marks on every paper) need the rule applied to the specific facts given, not restated in the abstract. Use AQA's own command words precisely: 'explain'/'suggest' target AO1 (accurate, precise legal knowledge); 'advise'/'consider' target AO1+AO2+AO3 together (apply the law to a scenario and reach a reasoned conclusion); 'examine'/'discuss'/'assess' target AO3 (evaluate non-substantive law, or substantive law within a non-substantive framework, e.g. discussing judicial precedent's advantages through a case scenario). Push the learner to build a bank of exact case names, years and the precise principle each establishes -- a vague 'there was a case about...' earns nothing under AQA's marking. Law changes: this course states the law of England and Wales as understood to be current in September 2026; statutory thresholds, ongoing reform and any pending legislation should be checked against a live source (legislation.gov.uk, aqa.org.uk) before a real exam sitting.

#### PO.1 Property offences: theft, s1 Theft Act 1968 (appropriation, property, belonging to another, dishonesty, intention permanently to deprive)
**Theft, s1 Theft Act 1968.** Defined as the **dishonest appropriation of property belonging to another with the intention of permanently depriving the other of it**, requiring all five elements: **appropriation** (s3) -- any assumption of the rights of an owner, which can occur even where the owner consents to what is done (*DPP v Gomez* [1993] AC 442 -- goods obtained by a dishonestly induced 'sale' were still appropriated, even though the shop manager consented to the transaction, since consent obtained by deception does not prevent appropriation), and even in relation to a **valid gift** (*R v Hinks* [2001] 2 AC 241 -- money validly given as a gift by a vulnerable man to the defendant could still be 'appropriated' for theft purposes if the taking was dishonest); switching a price label (*R v Morris* [1984] AC 320) is a classic example of appropriation. **Property** (s4) -- broadly defined to include money and all personal/real property (with limited exceptions, e.g. wild plants/animals in limited circumstances, and land, generally, cannot be stolen except in specific situations). **Belonging to another** (s5) -- property belongs to anyone with possession or control of it, or a proprietary interest, so an owner can in principle 'steal' their own property if someone else has a superior possessory right in it at the time (*R v Turner (No 2)* [1971] 1 WLR 901 -- the owner of a car, who took it back from a garage that was lawfully in possession of it while repairing it, without paying, was held to have stolen his own car, since the garage had possession/control at that moment). **Dishonesty** (s2, and the test now set out in ***Ivey v Genting Casinos (UK) Ltd t/a Crockfords* [2017] UKSC 67**, adopted for criminal cases in *Barton and Booth v R* [2020] EWCA Crim 575, replacing the old two-stage *Ghosh* test) -- first establish the defendant's actual subjective knowledge/belief as to the facts, then ask objectively whether the conduct was dishonest by the standards of ordinary decent people, applying s2's specific statutory exceptions (e.g. a genuine belief in a legal right to the property, s2(1)(a)). **Intention permanently to deprive** (s6) -- the defendant need not intend the owner never to get the property back in every literal sense; treating the property as one's own to dispose of regardless of the owner's rights suffices (*DPP v Lavender* [1994] Crim LR 297), and even an intention to return an *equivalent* rather than the identical item/money can suffice where the specific thing taken is effectively used up or its value entirely appropriated (*R v Velumyl* [1989] Crim LR 299 -- taking money from a safe intending to replace it later with different notes was still theft, since the defendant did not intend to return the very notes taken).

#### PO.2 Property offences: robbery, s8 Theft Act 1968 (theft plus force or the threat of force, immediately before or at the time of stealing, in order to steal)
**Robbery, s8 Theft Act 1968.** Defined as **theft, accompanied by the use of force or the threat of force on any person, immediately before or at the time of the theft, in order to steal**. All elements of theft (above) must first be proved -- if there is no theft (e.g. the defendant genuinely believed they had a legal right to the property, negating dishonesty), there can be no robbery either. **Force** can be minimal -- a jostle or a tug can suffice (*R v Dawson and James* (1976) 64 Cr App R 170 -- a nudge that unbalanced the victim enough for his wallet to be taken was sufficient force, the jury being entitled to find even relatively slight force sufficient). Force applied to **property closely connected to the victim** (rather than directly to the victim's body) can also suffice (*R v Clouden* [1987] Crim LR 56 -- wrenching a shopping bag from the victim's grasp was force sufficient for robbery, even though no force was applied to the victim's body directly). The theft and the force must be linked in time -- **'immediately before or at the time of' stealing** -- and the courts treat theft (appropriation) as a **continuing act**, so force used shortly after the initial appropriation, while the theft is still in progress, can still satisfy robbery (*R v Hale* (1979) 68 Cr App R 415 -- tying up the victim after taking jewellery from her bedroom, while the defendants remained in the process of leaving with the goods, was force at the time of the (continuing) theft). Finally, the force must be used **in order to steal** -- force used for an entirely unconnected reason, with theft only an afterthought, would not satisfy robbery (though would likely still be theft plus a separate assault).
