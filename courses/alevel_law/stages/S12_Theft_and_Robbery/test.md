## Test items
1. Explain, with authority, why the requirement of 'belonging to another' does not always prevent an owner from being liable for stealing their own property. [5 marks]
2. Maya pushes past Noor on a crowded train platform, causing her to stumble, and in the moment grabs Noor's phone from her hand before running off; she later claims she only decided to take the phone after the push, which was accidental. Using IRAC, advise on whether Maya is liable for robbery. [10 marks]

## Answer key (for the tutor only)
1. [5] B2 s5 Theft Act 1968 defines property as belonging to anyone with possession or control, or a proprietary interest, not only the strict legal owner; B2 so an owner can in principle steal their own property if another has possession/control of it at the relevant time; B1 authority: R v Turner (No 2) [1971] 1 WLR 901 (owner who took his car back from a garage lawfully in possession of it, without paying, was liable for theft of his own car).
2. [10] Mark using issue-rule-application-conclusion (IRAC): identify the issue(s), state the rule precisely with its authority (case or statute, named exactly), apply it to the facts given, and reach a supported conclusion. Credit: issue = whether the force and the theft are sufficiently linked in time and purpose for robbery; rule = robbery requires theft plus force or threat of force, immediately before or at the time of stealing, in order to steal (s8 Theft Act 1968); force can be minimal (R v Dawson and James (1976) 64 Cr App R 170) and can be applied to the victim directly or to property closely connected with them; theft is treated as a continuing act, allowing force during that continuing period to count (R v Hale (1979) 68 Cr App R 415); but the force must be used in order to steal; application = if the push was genuinely accidental and the decision to take the phone was a separate, later opportunistic act with no force used to facilitate the taking itself, the 'in order to steal' link is weak and this looks more like theft alone (plus, possibly, an unrelated earlier incident); if instead the jury finds the push was used, even opportunistically, to enable the grab, the force-and-theft link could be established; conclusion = on Maya's own account (accidental push, later decision), robbery is unlikely to be made out for want of force used in order to steal, though theft is clearly established, and the tutor should push the learner to identify this as the key contested issue rather than assume robbery automatically follows from force plus a taking.
