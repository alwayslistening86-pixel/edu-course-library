# S05_Civil_and_Criminal_Courts - Lesson: The civil legal system and dispute resolution; the criminal legal system and lay people

## Goal
The learner describes the civil court structure, the track system and forms of dispute resolution, and the criminal court structure, classification of offences, sentencing powers, and the role of magistrates and juries.

## Syllabus items taught here
- CC.1 - The civil legal system: the civil courts and the track system (small claims, fast track, multi-track); the civil appeal system; tribunals; the role of mediation and negotiation as dispute resolution methods
- CR.1 - The criminal legal system and lay people: classification of offences (summary/either-way/indictable); the criminal courts and the appeal system; the powers of the criminal courts and adult offender sentencing; the role and powers of magistrates; the role, selection and advantages/disadvantages of juries

## How to teach this
Ask: a shopper is owed £3,000 by a trader who won't refund a faulty sofa -- which court, and how formal will it be? Teach each rule as: the precise legal test (statute section or case authority, stated exactly, since AQA rewards named, accurate authority), why it exists (the policy AQA's own specification names, e.g. public policy limits on the duty of care, freedom of contract vs consumer protection), and how it applies to facts -- AQA's application and scenario questions (worth over half the marks on every paper) need the rule applied to the specific facts given, not restated in the abstract. Use AQA's own command words precisely: 'explain'/'suggest' target AO1 (accurate, precise legal knowledge); 'advise'/'consider' target AO1+AO2+AO3 together (apply the law to a scenario and reach a reasoned conclusion); 'examine'/'discuss'/'assess' target AO3 (evaluate non-substantive law, or substantive law within a non-substantive framework, e.g. discussing judicial precedent's advantages through a case scenario). Push the learner to build a bank of exact case names, years and the precise principle each establishes -- a vague 'there was a case about...' earns nothing under AQA's marking. Law changes: this course states the law of England and Wales as understood to be current in September 2026; statutory thresholds, ongoing reform and any pending legislation should be checked against a live source (legislation.gov.uk, aqa.org.uk) before a real exam sitting.

#### CC.1 The civil legal system: the civil courts and the track system (small claims, fast track, multi-track); the civil appeal system; tribunals; the role of mediation and negotiation as dispute resolution methods
**The civil legal system.** Civil claims are heard at first instance in the **County Court** (most claims) or the **High Court** (higher-value or more complex claims, divided into the King's Bench Division, Chancery Division, and Family Division), governed by the **Civil Procedure Rules 1998**, whose overriding objective is dealing with cases justly and at proportionate cost. The **track system** allocates claims by value/complexity: the **small claims track** (broadly claims up to £10,000, informal, limited recoverable costs, designed for litigants in person); the **fast track** (broadly £10,000-£25,000, a fixed, tighter timetable to trial); the **multi-track** (higher-value or complex claims, actively case-managed by a judge). **Appeals** generally go from the County Court/High Court to the **Court of Appeal (Civil Division)**, and from there (with permission, usually on a point of law of general public importance) to the **Supreme Court**. **Tribunals** provide a specialist, generally cheaper and less formal forum for defined categories of dispute (e.g. employment, immigration, social security), typically with a legally qualified chair and, for some tribunals, lay members with relevant expertise. **Alternative dispute resolution** avoids full litigation: **negotiation** (the parties or their representatives resolve the dispute directly, without a third party); **mediation** (a neutral, trained third party facilitates the parties toward their own agreed settlement -- non-binding unless and until a settlement is reached and recorded); increasingly encouraged by the courts (including cost sanctions for unreasonably refusing to attempt it) as a faster, cheaper and less adversarial route than trial.

#### CR.1 The criminal legal system and lay people: classification of offences (summary/either-way/indictable); the criminal courts and the appeal system; the powers of the criminal courts and adult offender sentencing; the role and powers of magistrates; the role, selection and advantages/disadvantages of juries
**The criminal legal system and lay people.** Offences are classified by seriousness: **summary offences** (minor, tried only in the Magistrates' Court, e.g. most motoring offences); **either-way offences** (may be tried in either court depending on seriousness and the defendant's election, e.g. theft, assault occasioning ABH); **indictable-only offences** (the most serious, tried only in the Crown Court, e.g. murder, rape, robbery). The **Magistrates' Court** (lay magistrates sitting as a bench, or a single District Judge) tries summary and some either-way offences, holds the first hearing for every criminal case, and handles bail and committal/sending decisions; the **Crown Court** (a judge, with a jury for a not-guilty plea on indictment) tries indictable and sent either-way cases. **Appeal routes**: from the Magistrates' Court, either a full rehearing on appeal to the Crown Court, or an appeal on a point of law to the **High Court (Divisional Court)** by way of case stated; from the Crown Court, appeal against conviction and/or sentence to the **Court of Appeal (Criminal Division)**, and (rarely, on a point of law of general public importance) to the Supreme Court. **Sentencing powers**: Magistrates' Courts have more limited sentencing powers than the Crown Court (which can impose the full range up to the maximum for the offence, including longer custodial terms); courts weigh **aggravating factors** (e.g. planning, vulnerability of the victim, use of a weapon) and **mitigating factors** (e.g. genuine remorse, a guilty plea, previous good character) within sentencing guidelines. **Magistrates**: unpaid lay volunteers (usually sitting as a bench of two or three, advised on law and procedure by a legally qualified clerk/legal adviser) who try the great majority of criminal cases; **advantages**: drawn from the local community, cheaper than professional judges, high-volume local justice; **disadvantages**: potential inconsistency between different benches, limited legal training compared with a professional judge, and criticism that the lay magistracy has not always reflected the diversity of the communities it serves. **Juries** (12 members in the Crown Court, for a trial on indictment) decide guilt or innocence on the facts, applying the law as directed by the judge; **advantages**: public participation and legitimacy in serious criminal justice, the "jury equity" of applying common sense/community standards, and independence from the state; **disadvantages**: jurors may struggle with complex evidence (e.g. in serious fraud), can in principle be influenced by media coverage or prejudice, and (since deliberations are secret) give no reasoned verdict that can be scrutinised for legal error the way a judge's reasoning can.
