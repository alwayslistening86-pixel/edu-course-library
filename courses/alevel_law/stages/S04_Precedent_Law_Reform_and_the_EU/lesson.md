# S04_Precedent_Law_Reform_and_the_EU - Lesson: Judicial precedent; law reform; the European Union

## Goal
The learner explains the doctrine of judicial precedent, the court hierarchy and how binding/persuasive precedent, ratio and obiter operate, the process and value of Law Commission-led reform, and the institutions, sources and continuing domestic impact of EU law.

## Syllabus items taught here
- JP.1 - Judicial precedent: the doctrine of stare decisis; the court hierarchy including the Supreme Court; ratio decidendi and obiter dicta; law reporting; original, binding and persuasive precedent; distinguishing, overruling and reversing; advantages and disadvantages
- LR.1 - Law reform: the role of the Law Commission in reform, codification, consolidation and repeal, and the process by which it works; advantages and disadvantages of Law Commission-led reform
- EU.1 - The European Union: the institutions of the EU and their functions; sources of EU law (treaties, regulations, directives); the impact of EU law and its retained status on the law of England and Wales after Brexit

## How to teach this
Ask: two Court of Appeal panels reach different conclusions on the same point of law a few years apart -- how can that happen, and which one binds the High Court now? Teach each rule as: the precise legal test (statute section or case authority, stated exactly, since AQA rewards named, accurate authority), why it exists (the policy AQA's own specification names, e.g. public policy limits on the duty of care, freedom of contract vs consumer protection), and how it applies to facts -- AQA's application and scenario questions (worth over half the marks on every paper) need the rule applied to the specific facts given, not restated in the abstract. Use AQA's own command words precisely: 'explain'/'suggest' target AO1 (accurate, precise legal knowledge); 'advise'/'consider' target AO1+AO2+AO3 together (apply the law to a scenario and reach a reasoned conclusion); 'examine'/'discuss'/'assess' target AO3 (evaluate non-substantive law, or substantive law within a non-substantive framework, e.g. discussing judicial precedent's advantages through a case scenario). Push the learner to build a bank of exact case names, years and the precise principle each establishes -- a vague 'there was a case about...' earns nothing under AQA's marking. Law changes: this course states the law of England and Wales as understood to be current in September 2026; statutory thresholds, ongoing reform and any pending legislation should be checked against a live source (legislation.gov.uk, aqa.org.uk) before a real exam sitting.

#### JP.1 Judicial precedent: the doctrine of stare decisis; the court hierarchy including the Supreme Court; ratio decidendi and obiter dicta; law reporting; original, binding and persuasive precedent; distinguishing, overruling and reversing; advantages and disadvantages
**Judicial precedent.** The doctrine of **stare decisis** ("stand by what has been decided") requires a court to follow a legal principle established in an earlier case where the material facts are sufficiently similar, giving consistency and predictability. The **court hierarchy** (from the top): the **Supreme Court** (final appellate court for the UK, formerly the House of Lords, whose pre-2009 decisions carry the same precedent weight); the **Court of Appeal** (Civil and Criminal Divisions); the **High Court** (whose Divisional Courts bind lower courts, and whose ordinary decisions are persuasive on each other but binding on lower courts); and below that the Crown Court, County Court and Magistrates' Court, which do not generally create binding precedent. A judgment separates the **ratio decidendi** (the binding legal principle necessary to the decision -- what a later court must follow) from **obiter dicta** (remarks not necessary to the decision, e.g. hypothetical reasoning or comment on a point not argued -- persuasive only). **Law reporting** (in official series such as the Law Reports, and now via neutral citation, e.g. [2020] UKSC 1) makes precedents accessible and citable. Precedent is **binding** where a lower court must follow a higher court's ratio, or **persuasive** where a court may but need not follow it (obiter dicta; decisions of courts lower in the hierarchy or outside England and Wales; a dissenting judgment). A court can **distinguish** an earlier case (treat it as not binding because the material facts differ), a higher court can **overrule** it (in a later, different case, declaring the earlier statement of law wrong), and an appeal court can **reverse** a decision on appeal in the same case. The **Supreme Court** normally follows its own past decisions but, under the **Practice Statement (Judicial Precedent) [1966] 1 WLR 1234**, may depart from a previous decision when it appears right to do so (used sparingly, to preserve certainty). The **Court of Appeal** is generally bound by its own past decisions, subject to the exceptions recognised in ***Young v Bristol Aeroplane Co Ltd* [1944] KB 718**: where its own earlier decisions conflict (it may choose which to follow), where an earlier decision cannot stand with a later decision of the Supreme Court, or where the earlier decision was given *per incuriam* (in ignorance of a relevant binding authority or statute). **Advantages**: certainty and predictability (people can plan their affairs); consistency and fairness (like cases treated alike); flexibility (through distinguishing, and the 1966 Practice Statement) to allow the law to develop; efficiency (settled points need not be re-argued). **Disadvantages**: rigidity (a bad precedent can persist until a suitable case reaches a high enough court); complexity (the volume of case law, and the difficulty of extracting a precise ratio) makes the law hard to access; and it can develop unpredictably/haphazardly, depending on which cases happen to be litigated, rather than through coherent planned reform.

#### LR.1 Law reform: the role of the Law Commission in reform, codification, consolidation and repeal, and the process by which it works; advantages and disadvantages of Law Commission-led reform
**Law reform.** The **Law Commission** (established by the Law Commissions Act 1965) is a permanent, independent body reviewing areas of law referred to it (by the Lord Chancellor) or chosen from its own programme of reform, and recommending change. Its work covers: **reform** (updating law to reflect modern conditions or fix identified problems); **codification** (bringing the whole of a topic's law, scattered across statute and case law, into one coherent statute); **consolidation** (combining several related statutes into one, without changing the substance, to simplify access); and **repeal** (removing obsolete/spent legislation). Its process typically involves research, wide consultation (issue papers and consultation papers inviting responses from the public, practitioners and academics), and a final report with a draft Bill, which Parliament may then enact (there is no guarantee it will -- the Commission has no power to legislate itself). **Advantages**: independent, expert-led review free from short-term political pressure; thorough public consultation improves the quality of proposals; a track record of significant reform being enacted (e.g. its earlier work underpinning parts of the Fraud Act 2006 and the Bribery Act 2010). **Disadvantages**: the government is not obliged to implement its recommendations, and many reports are not acted on or wait years for parliamentary time; the process can be slow given full research and consultation; where reform is politically sensitive, government may decline to act even on a well-evidenced report.

#### EU.1 The European Union: the institutions of the EU and their functions; sources of EU law (treaties, regulations, directives); the impact of EU law and its retained status on the law of England and Wales after Brexit
**The European Union.** Though the UK left the EU in 2020, its institutions, sources of law and legacy remain examinable as part of understanding the current legal landscape (retained EU law). Key institutions and their functions (as they operated while the UK was a member, and as they continue to function for remaining member states): the **Council of the European Union** (represents member state governments, shares legislative and budgetary power with the Parliament); the **European Commission** (proposes legislation, enforces EU law, represents the EU externally); the **European Parliament** (directly elected, shares legislative power with the Council, scrutinises the Commission); the **Court of Justice of the European Union (CJEU)** (interprets EU law and ensures its uniform application, formerly able to bind UK courts on questions of EU law). **Sources of EU law**: **treaties** (primary law, e.g. the founding treaties, directly effective in member states); **regulations** (directly applicable in all member states without national implementing legislation); **directives** (set a binding result member states must achieve, but leave the method/form of implementation to national law, e.g. via a UK statutory instrument or Act). **Impact on the law of England and Wales**: while a member, EU law took precedence over conflicting domestic law, and courts interpreted domestic legislation implementing EU obligations purposively, in light of EU law's own purpose. Since Brexit, most such law continues in force domestically as **retained EU law** under the European Union (Withdrawal) Act 2018 (as amended), meaning existing rules derived from EU membership generally still apply unless and until Parliament changes them, though the CJEU no longer has binding authority over UK courts and the status/interpretation of retained EU law continues to evolve -- check a live source for the current position before relying on any specific claim about what remains in force.
