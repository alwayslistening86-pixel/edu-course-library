# S24_Contract_Terms_and_Exclusion_Clauses - Lesson: Express/implied terms, conditions/warranties/innominate terms; exclusion clauses

## Goal
The learner classifies contract terms as express, implied, conditions, warranties or innominate terms, and explains how exclusion clauses are incorporated, construed and controlled by statute.

## Syllabus items taught here
- TERMS.1 - Contract terms: express terms; implied terms (by statute, custom, courts and, for commercial contracts, by trade custom); the distinction between conditions, warranties and innominate terms, and the remedy each attracts on breach
- TERMS.2 - Exclusion and limitation clauses: incorporation (signature, notice, course of dealing) and construction (the contra proferentem rule) at common law; statutory control under the Unfair Contract Terms Act 1977 (s2, s3) for business-to-business contracts and the Consumer Rights Act 2015 (s31, s57, s65) for consumer contracts

## How to teach this
Ask: a contract is breached in a minor, trivial way -- should the innocent party always be free to walk away from the whole deal? Teach each rule as: the precise legal test (statute section or case authority, stated exactly, since AQA rewards named, accurate authority), why it exists (the policy AQA's own specification names, e.g. public policy limits on the duty of care, freedom of contract vs consumer protection), and how it applies to facts -- AQA's application and scenario questions (worth over half the marks on every paper) need the rule applied to the specific facts given, not restated in the abstract. Use AQA's own command words precisely: 'explain'/'suggest' target AO1 (accurate, precise legal knowledge); 'advise'/'consider' target AO1+AO2+AO3 together (apply the law to a scenario and reach a reasoned conclusion); 'examine'/'discuss'/'assess' target AO3 (evaluate non-substantive law, or substantive law within a non-substantive framework, e.g. discussing judicial precedent's advantages through a case scenario). Push the learner to build a bank of exact case names, years and the precise principle each establishes -- a vague 'there was a case about...' earns nothing under AQA's marking. Law changes: this course states the law of England and Wales as understood to be current in September 2026; statutory thresholds, ongoing reform and any pending legislation should be checked against a live source (legislation.gov.uk, aqa.org.uk) before a real exam sitting.

#### TERMS.1 Contract terms: express terms; implied terms (by statute, custom, courts and, for commercial contracts, by trade custom); the distinction between conditions, warranties and innominate terms, and the remedy each attracts on breach
**Contract terms.** **Express terms** are those the parties actually agreed, whether in writing or orally. **Implied terms** are not expressly agreed but read into the contract by: **statute** (e.g. the Consumer Rights Act 2015 terms at STAT.1-STAT.2); **custom** (terms customary in a particular trade or locality); or **the courts**, which may imply a term to give the contract **business efficacy** -- to make it work as the parties must have intended (*The Moorcock* (1889) 14 PD 64 -- a term that a wharf owner's jetty was reasonably safe for a ship to dock at was implied, since without it the contract to use the wharf would not function as both parties evidently intended) -- or via the **"officious bystander" test**, asking whether, if an officious bystander had asked the parties at the time whether some obvious term applied, they would have testily agreed "of course" (*Shirlaw v Southern Foundries (1926) Ltd* [1939] 2 KB 206). Terms are also classified by their **importance**: a **condition** is a term so central to the contract that breaching it entitles the innocent party to **terminate** the contract and claim damages (*Poussard v Spiers and Pond* (1876) 1 QBD 410 -- an opera singer's failure to appear for the first few nights of a run was breach of a condition, entitling the theatre to engage a replacement for the whole run); a **warranty** is a less important term, breach of which gives only a right to **damages**, not termination (*Bettini v Gye* (1876) 1 QBD 183 -- missing a few days of rehearsals, as opposed to performances themselves, was only breach of a warranty, not entitling termination). Some terms cannot sensibly be classified in advance as either, since the *consequences* of breach can range from trivial to very serious depending how they are breached -- these are **innominate (intermediate) terms**, where the remedy (termination, or damages only) depends on the actual **effect** of the specific breach that occurred (*Hong Kong Fir Shipping Co Ltd v Kawasaki Kisen Kaisha Ltd* [1962] 2 QB 26 -- a term requiring a ship to be seaworthy could be breached trivially or very seriously depending on the nature of the defect, so the court asked whether the breach as it actually occurred deprived the innocent party of substantially the whole benefit of the contract, rather than fixing the term's classification in advance).

#### TERMS.2 Exclusion and limitation clauses: incorporation (signature, notice, course of dealing) and construction (the contra proferentem rule) at common law; statutory control under the Unfair Contract Terms Act 1977 (s2, s3) for business-to-business contracts and the Consumer Rights Act 2015 (s31, s57, s65) for consumer contracts
**Exclusion and limitation clauses.** A term seeking to exclude or limit a party's liability, which is only effective if it is validly **incorporated** into the contract and, construed correctly, actually **covers** the loss in question, and (where relevant) passes statutory control. **Incorporation**: by **signature** -- a signed document is binding regardless of whether the signer read or understood it, provided there was no misrepresentation about its effect (*L'Estrange v F Graucob Ltd* [1934] 2 KB 394); by **reasonable notice** given before or at the time the contract is formed, not after (*Olley v Marlborough Court Ltd* [1949] 1 KB 532 -- a notice inside a hotel room disclaiming liability for stolen property came too late, since the contract was already formed at the reception desk before the guest ever saw the notice), with particularly onerous or unusual clauses needing to be given more prominent notice, sometimes called the "red hand rule" (*Thornton v Shoe Lane Parking Ltd* [1971] 2 QB 163 -- a wide exclusion clause printed on a car park ticket, only visible after the ticket had already been taken from an automatic machine (by which point the contract was arguably already made), was not validly incorporated, since such an unusual, onerous term needed to be drawn to the customer's attention more clearly and before the contract was concluded); or by a **consistent course of dealing** between the same parties (*Hollier v Rambler Motors (AMC) Ltd* [1972] 2 QB 71 -- a handful of past transactions, not consistently on the same written terms, was not enough to incorporate a clause by course of dealing, since the dealing must be regular and consistent). A document that is not one a reasonable person would expect to contain contractual terms (e.g. a mere receipt) cannot incorporate a clause even if handed over at the right time (*Chapelton v Barry Urban District Council* [1940] 1 KB 532 -- a ticket handed over for hiring a deckchair, which a reasonable person would treat merely as a receipt, did not incorporate an exclusion clause printed on it). **Construction**: ambiguous exclusion clauses are construed **contra proferentem** -- against the party seeking to rely on them. **Statutory control**: the **Unfair Contract Terms Act 1977** governs business-to-business contracts (and some consumer contracts for property damage) -- **s2(1)** makes any clause excluding liability for death or personal injury caused by negligence **automatically void**; **s2(2)** allows exclusion of other negligence liability (e.g. for property damage) only to the extent the clause is **reasonable**; **s3** subjects exclusion clauses in standard-term contracts to a similar reasonableness requirement for breach of contract. For **consumer contracts**, the **Consumer Rights Act 2015** now provides the primary statutory control (largely superseding UCTA for consumer contracts): **s31** renders void any term excluding liability for the statutory implied terms on goods (STAT.1); **s57** does the same for services (STAT.2); and **s65** makes void any term excluding liability for death or personal injury caused by negligence, mirroring UCTA's s2(1) protection but for consumer contracts specifically.
