# S02_Rule_of_Law_and_Parliamentary_Law_Making - Lesson: The rule of law; parliamentary law making

## Goal
The learner explains the constitutional doctrine of the rule of law and how it constrains law-making, the legal system and substantive law, and describes the parliamentary process by which primary legislation is made, including its influences and the limits of parliamentary supremacy.

## Syllabus items taught here
- RL.1 - The rule of law: the constitutional doctrine and its application to law-making, the legal system and substantive law -- no punishment without breach of a known law, equality before the law, and access to fair, clear legal process
- PLM.1 - Parliamentary law making: Green and White Papers; the formal legislative process and the stages a Bill goes through; influences on Parliament (pressure groups, the media, the Law Commission); parliamentary supremacy and its limits; advantages and disadvantages of the process and its influences

## How to teach this
Ask: if a government could pass a law today making yesterday's lawful conduct a crime, why would that be wrong -- and what constitutional idea says so? Teach each rule as: the precise legal test (statute section or case authority, stated exactly, since AQA rewards named, accurate authority), why it exists (the policy AQA's own specification names, e.g. public policy limits on the duty of care, freedom of contract vs consumer protection), and how it applies to facts -- AQA's application and scenario questions (worth over half the marks on every paper) need the rule applied to the specific facts given, not restated in the abstract. Use AQA's own command words precisely: 'explain'/'suggest' target AO1 (accurate, precise legal knowledge); 'advise'/'consider' target AO1+AO2+AO3 together (apply the law to a scenario and reach a reasoned conclusion); 'examine'/'discuss'/'assess' target AO3 (evaluate non-substantive law, or substantive law within a non-substantive framework, e.g. discussing judicial precedent's advantages through a case scenario). Push the learner to build a bank of exact case names, years and the precise principle each establishes -- a vague 'there was a case about...' earns nothing under AQA's marking. Law changes: this course states the law of England and Wales as understood to be current in September 2026; statutory thresholds, ongoing reform and any pending legislation should be checked against a live source (legislation.gov.uk, aqa.org.uk) before a real exam sitting.

#### RL.1 The rule of law: the constitutional doctrine and its application to law-making, the legal system and substantive law -- no punishment without breach of a known law, equality before the law, and access to fair, clear legal process
**The rule of law.** A foundational, if contested, constitutional doctrine (most associated with A.V. Dicey) holding that everyone, including the government, is bound by and accountable under the law, and that law should be applied predictably, equally and fairly. Core strands examinable at AQA level: **no person may be punished except for a clear breach of an established law** (no retrospective criminal liability, and law must be sufficiently certain that people can know in advance what is prohibited); **equality before the law** (the same law applies to ordinary citizens and government officials alike, and is enforced by the same courts); and **fairness, clarity and access** (law should be reasonably clear/accessible, and everyone should have a genuine opportunity to have their legal rights determined by an independent court, which links to the access-to-justice content at AJ.1). The rule of law applies to **law-making** (Parliament should not legislate retrospectively to criminalise past conduct, and delegated legislation must stay within its granted powers -- see DL.1), to **the legal system** (an independent judiciary, and courts genuinely open and accessible), and to **substantive law** (rules should be reasonably certain and prospective, e.g. the requirement in criminal law that mens rea and actus reus both be proved to a fixed legal standard). AQA's non-substantive essay questions often ask the learner to assess a specific rule (e.g. a criminal defence, or the funding of civil claims) against the rule of law's requirements of certainty, equality or access.

#### PLM.1 Parliamentary law making: Green and White Papers; the formal legislative process and the stages a Bill goes through; influences on Parliament (pressure groups, the media, the Law Commission); parliamentary supremacy and its limits; advantages and disadvantages of the process and its influences
**Parliamentary law making.** Before a Bill is introduced, government policy may be developed through a **Green Paper** (a consultative document setting out options and inviting comment) followed by a **White Paper** (setting out firm, more developed proposals ahead of a Bill). The **formal legislative process**: a Bill (Public -- Government or Private Members'; Private; or Hybrid) goes through, in each House: **First Reading** (formal introduction, no debate), **Second Reading** (debate on general principles, a vote), **Committee Stage** (a committee scrutinises and amends the Bill clause by clause), **Report Stage** (further amendments considered by the whole House), **Third Reading** (final debate, usually on the Bill as amended), then the same stages in the other House, before **Royal Assent** makes it law. The **Parliament Acts 1911 and 1949** allow the Commons to bypass Lords opposition to most public Bills (not, notably, a Bill to extend the life of Parliament) in defined circumstances. **Influences on Parliament**: pressure groups (sectional, representing a defined membership, e.g. a trade union, or cause, campaigning for a belief, e.g. an environmental group) lobby ministers, MPs and the public; the media shapes public and political debate; the **Law Commission** proposes reform (see LR.1). **Parliamentary supremacy** (Parliament can make or unmake any law, and no Act binds a future Parliament) is the traditional core of the UK constitution, but is qualified in practice by the Human Rights Act 1998 (courts must interpret legislation compatibly with Convention rights so far as possible), the effect of retained EU law, and political constraints such as the near-impossibility of repealing entrenched constitutional statutes outright. **Advantages** of the process: democratic legitimacy (elected MPs), thorough scrutiny across multiple stages and both Houses, opportunity for expert/public input via consultation and committee evidence. **Disadvantages**: can be slow, vulnerable to time pressure (a busy legislative timetable can crowd out non-Government Bills), and influences such as pressure groups or the media can distort priorities toward vocal or well-resourced interests rather than the wider public interest.
