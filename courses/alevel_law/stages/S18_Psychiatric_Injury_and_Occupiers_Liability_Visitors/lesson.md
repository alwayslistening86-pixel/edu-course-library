# S18_Psychiatric_Injury_and_Occupiers_Liability_Visitors - Lesson: Psychiatric injury; occupiers' liability to visitors

## Goal
The learner explains the primary/secondary victim distinction and control mechanisms for psychiatric injury claims, and the duty owed to visitors under the Occupiers' Liability Act 1957.

## Syllabus items taught here
- ECO.2 - Liability in negligence for psychiatric injury: the primary/secondary victim distinction and the additional control mechanisms secondary victims must satisfy (proximity of relationship, time/space, and perception through own unaided senses)
- OL.1 - Occupiers' liability to visitors under the Occupiers' Liability Act 1957: the common duty of care, who is an occupier, who is a visitor, and defences (warnings, independent contractors, volenti, contributory negligence)

## How to teach this
Ask: a bystander who merely witnesses a horrific accident, with no risk to themselves, later develops a diagnosed psychiatric illness -- should the law treat them the same as someone physically hurt in the accident? Teach each rule as: the precise legal test (statute section or case authority, stated exactly, since AQA rewards named, accurate authority), why it exists (the policy AQA's own specification names, e.g. public policy limits on the duty of care, freedom of contract vs consumer protection), and how it applies to facts -- AQA's application and scenario questions (worth over half the marks on every paper) need the rule applied to the specific facts given, not restated in the abstract. Use AQA's own command words precisely: 'explain'/'suggest' target AO1 (accurate, precise legal knowledge); 'advise'/'consider' target AO1+AO2+AO3 together (apply the law to a scenario and reach a reasoned conclusion); 'examine'/'discuss'/'assess' target AO3 (evaluate non-substantive law, or substantive law within a non-substantive framework, e.g. discussing judicial precedent's advantages through a case scenario). Push the learner to build a bank of exact case names, years and the precise principle each establishes -- a vague 'there was a case about...' earns nothing under AQA's marking. Law changes: this course states the law of England and Wales as understood to be current in September 2026; statutory thresholds, ongoing reform and any pending legislation should be checked against a live source (legislation.gov.uk, aqa.org.uk) before a real exam sitting.

#### ECO.2 Liability in negligence for psychiatric injury: the primary/secondary victim distinction and the additional control mechanisms secondary victims must satisfy (proximity of relationship, time/space, and perception through own unaided senses)
**Liability in negligence for psychiatric injury.** Claims for a **recognised psychiatric illness** (not mere grief, distress or normal emotional upset, which are not compensable) are divided into **primary victims** (those within the actual area of foreseeable physical danger, or who reasonably believed themselves to be) and **secondary victims** (witnesses to a shocking event involving danger/harm to someone else, without being themselves in danger). A **primary victim** need only show that **some** personal injury -- physical or psychiatric -- was a reasonably foreseeable consequence of the defendant's negligence; they need not additionally show that psychiatric injury specifically was foreseeable (*Page v Smith* [1996] AC 155 -- a claimant involved in a minor car collision, physically unhurt but who suffered a recurrence of chronic fatigue syndrome triggered by the shock, recovered even though psychiatric harm specifically was not foreseeable, since some physical injury was foreseeable from the collision, and that sufficed to establish him as a primary victim). A **secondary victim** must additionally satisfy strict **control mechanisms**, set out in ***Alcock v Chief Constable of South Yorkshire Police* [1992] 1 AC 310** (relatives of victims of the Hillsborough disaster, who watched scenes on live television or heard about it, largely failed in their claims): (1) a **close tie of love and affection** with the primary victim (presumed for spouses/parents/children, but requiring proof for other relationships); (2) **proximity in time and space** to the event or its immediate aftermath (*McLoughlin v O'Brian* [1983] 1 AC 410 -- a mother who arrived at hospital shortly after a serious accident involving her family and saw them in their distressing, injured state before they had been cleaned up was within the "immediate aftermath", even though not present at the accident itself); and (3) the psychiatric injury must be caused by **direct perception** through the claimant's own **unaided senses** (seeing or hearing the event or its immediate aftermath directly -- being told about it by a third party, or watching it on a broadcast that did not identify individual victims, does not suffice, as most of the Alcock claimants discovered). Rescuers and professional responders (e.g. police officers) attending a horrific scene are not automatically treated as primary victims merely by virtue of their professional role -- they must still satisfy the ordinary requirements for whichever category applies to them on the facts (*White v Chief Constable of South Yorkshire Police* [1999] 2 AC 455 -- police officers who assisted at Hillsborough, not themselves in physical danger, could not recover as primary victims simply because of the trauma of what they witnessed while on duty).

#### OL.1 Occupiers' liability to visitors under the Occupiers' Liability Act 1957: the common duty of care, who is an occupier, who is a visitor, and defences (warnings, independent contractors, volenti, contributory negligence)
**Occupiers' liability to visitors, Occupiers' Liability Act 1957.** An **occupier** is anyone with a sufficient degree of **control** over premises, not necessarily the legal owner (*Wheat v E Lacon & Co Ltd* [1966] AC 552 -- a pub manager's employer, retaining a general right of control over the premises even though day-to-day management was delegated, remained an occupier alongside the manager, confirming that more than one person can be an occupier of the same premises at once). A **visitor** is anyone with express or implied permission to be on the premises (a person who exceeds the scope of their permission, e.g. by going somewhere they were not invited to go, may become a trespasser instead, shifting them to the 1984 Act). The Act imposes a **common duty of care**: to take such care as is reasonable in the circumstances to see that the visitor will be **reasonably safe** in using the premises for the purposes for which they are permitted to be there (not a guarantee of absolute safety). The standard adjusts for the type of visitor: **children** are owed a higher standard, since an occupier must be prepared for children to be less careful than adults, including being attracted to an "allurement" they may not appreciate as dangerous (*Phipps v Rochester Corporation* [1955] 1 QB 450 -- a very young child injured falling into a trench on open building land was owed a lower duty than an older child might have been, since for a very young child the occupier could reasonably expect a responsible parent to be supervising, and the risk was one an accompanying parent should have guarded against); **skilled visitors** exercising their calling may be expected to guard against risks ordinarily incidental to that calling, so a clear **warning** can discharge the duty for such a visitor even though it might not for an ordinary visitor (*Roles v Nathan* [1963] 1 WLR 1117 -- chimney sweeps, warned of the specific danger of carbon monoxide from a lit fire while working on a flue, were owed no further duty when they ignored the warning and continued regardless). Where harm is caused by the faulty work of an **independent contractor**, the occupier will not be liable provided it was reasonable to entrust the work to a contractor, the contractor was reasonably competent, and (for technical work) the occupier took reasonable steps to check the work had been done properly (*Haseldine v C A Daw & Son Ltd* [1941] 2 KB 343 -- a lift maintained by a competent specialist contractor, whose negligent repair caused injury, did not make the occupier liable, since checking the technical adequacy of specialist repair work was beyond what a reasonable occupier could be expected to do personally). Defences include an effective **warning** (s2(4)(a)), engaging a competent **independent contractor** as above, **contributory negligence**, and **volenti**.
