## Test items
1. Explain the distinction between a primary and a secondary victim in a psychiatric injury claim, with authority. [6 marks]
2. A restaurant owner engages a properly qualified, reputable electrician to rewire the kitchen. The electrician does the work negligently, and a young child of a customer, permitted to wait near the kitchen door while their parent settled the bill, is injured touching an exposed live wire the electrician left uncovered. Using IRAC, advise on the restaurant's liability under the Occupiers' Liability Act 1957. [10 marks]

## Answer key (for the tutor only)
1. [6] B2 a primary victim is within the actual or reasonably apprehended area of physical danger, and need only show some personal injury (physical or psychiatric) was foreseeable, not psychiatric injury specifically (Page v Smith [1996] AC 155); B2 a secondary victim witnesses a shocking event involving danger/harm to someone else without themselves being in danger; B2 a secondary victim must additionally satisfy the Alcock control mechanisms (close tie of love/affection, proximity in time/space, direct perception through unaided senses) (Alcock v Chief Constable of South Yorkshire Police [1992] 1 AC 310).
2. [10] Mark using issue-rule-application-conclusion (IRAC): identify the issue(s), state the rule precisely with its authority (case or statute, named exactly), apply it to the facts given, and reach a supported conclusion. Credit: issue = whether the restaurant, as occupier, is liable for a visitor's injury caused by an independent contractor's negligent work, and whether the child-visitor standard applies; rule = the 1957 Act imposes a common duty to take reasonable care that visitors are reasonably safe; an occupier is not liable for a competent independent contractor's negligent technical work provided it was reasonable to use a contractor, the contractor was competent, and (for technical work) reasonable checks were made (Haseldine v C A Daw & Son Ltd [1941] 2 KB 343); a higher standard applies for child visitors, who may be less careful than adults (Phipps v Rochester Corporation [1955] 1 QB 450); application = engaging a properly qualified electrician for technical rewiring work is reasonable, and electrical work is precisely the kind of technical work a reasonable occupier could not personally verify, supporting the independent-contractor defence; but the child-visitor point cuts the other way -- if leaving an exposed live wire uncovered, even briefly, in an area accessible to a young permitted visitor was an obvious, checkable risk (e.g. a simple visual check before allowing customers back into that area), the restaurant's own separate duty to reasonably supervise the area for its more vulnerable visitors may not be fully discharged just by pointing to the contractor's competence; conclusion = the restaurant has a good independent-contractor defence for the electrician's negligent workmanship itself, but should still be advised the child-visitor standard means it cannot simply rely on that defence if it also failed to take basic reasonable steps of its own (e.g. checking the area was safe) once the customer's child was permitted to wait there.
