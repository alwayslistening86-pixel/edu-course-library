# S09_Diminished_Responsibility_and_Involuntary_Manslaughter - Lesson: Voluntary manslaughter -- diminished responsibility; involuntary manslaughter

## Goal
The learner explains the diminished responsibility partial defence under s2 Homicide Act 1957 (as amended), and the two forms of involuntary manslaughter -- unlawful act (constructive) manslaughter and gross negligence manslaughter.

## Syllabus items taught here
- FO.3 - Fatal offences: voluntary manslaughter -- diminished responsibility under s2 Homicide Act 1957 as amended by the Coroners and Justice Act 2009 (abnormality of mental functioning from a recognised medical condition, substantially impairing responsibility)
- FO.4 - Fatal offences: involuntary manslaughter -- unlawful act (constructive) manslaughter and gross negligence manslaughter

## How to teach this
Ask: someone kills while suffering a severe, medically recognised mental illness that isn't insanity -- how does the law respond differently from an ordinary murder charge? Teach each rule as: the precise legal test (statute section or case authority, stated exactly, since AQA rewards named, accurate authority), why it exists (the policy AQA's own specification names, e.g. public policy limits on the duty of care, freedom of contract vs consumer protection), and how it applies to facts -- AQA's application and scenario questions (worth over half the marks on every paper) need the rule applied to the specific facts given, not restated in the abstract. Use AQA's own command words precisely: 'explain'/'suggest' target AO1 (accurate, precise legal knowledge); 'advise'/'consider' target AO1+AO2+AO3 together (apply the law to a scenario and reach a reasoned conclusion); 'examine'/'discuss'/'assess' target AO3 (evaluate non-substantive law, or substantive law within a non-substantive framework, e.g. discussing judicial precedent's advantages through a case scenario). Push the learner to build a bank of exact case names, years and the precise principle each establishes -- a vague 'there was a case about...' earns nothing under AQA's marking. Law changes: this course states the law of England and Wales as understood to be current in September 2026; statutory thresholds, ongoing reform and any pending legislation should be checked against a live source (legislation.gov.uk, aqa.org.uk) before a real exam sitting.

#### FO.3 Fatal offences: voluntary manslaughter -- diminished responsibility under s2 Homicide Act 1957 as amended by the Coroners and Justice Act 2009 (abnormality of mental functioning from a recognised medical condition, substantially impairing responsibility)
**Voluntary manslaughter: diminished responsibility.** A **partial defence to murder only**, under **s2 Homicide Act 1957**, as substantially rewritten by **s52 Coroners and Justice Act 2009**. The defendant must show: (a) an **abnormality of mental functioning**, (b) arising from a **recognised medical condition**, (c) which **substantially impaired** the defendant's ability to do one or more of: understand the nature of their conduct, form a rational judgment, or exercise self-control, and (d) provides an **explanation** for the killing (the abnormality must be at least a significant contributory factor). 'Abnormality of mental functioning' replaces the old, broader 'abnormality of mind' language from *R v Byrne* [1960] 2 QB 396 (still illustrative of the underlying concept -- a state of mind so different from an ordinary person's that a reasonable person would call it abnormal), but now must be tied to a specific recognised medical condition (e.g. depression, PTSD, a personality disorder), reflecting the 2009 reform's aim of anchoring the defence in psychiatric evidence rather than a jury's lay impression. The meaning of 'substantially' impaired was clarified by the Supreme Court in ***R v Golds* [2016] UKSC 61**: 'substantial' means something more than merely more than trivial, but need not mean 'total'; in most cases the jury can be trusted to give the word its ordinary meaning without further direction unless the case genuinely requires it. Diminished responsibility differs from insanity (DEF.1) in that it is a partial defence (reducing murder to manslaughter) available only for murder, whereas insanity is a general defence (leading to a special verdict) available to any crime; it also differs from loss of control in focusing on the defendant's underlying mental condition rather than a triggered emotional response to specific events.

#### FO.4 Fatal offences: involuntary manslaughter -- unlawful act (constructive) manslaughter and gross negligence manslaughter
**Involuntary manslaughter.** Killing without the mens rea for murder, in one of two recognised forms. **Unlawful act (constructive) manslaughter** requires: (1) an **unlawful act** (a criminal act, not merely a civil wrong or omission -- *R v Lowe* [1973] QB 702, an omission alone cannot found this form of manslaughter, though the same facts might support gross negligence manslaughter instead); (2) that is **dangerous**, judged objectively -- would a sober and reasonable bystander recognise the act as carrying some risk of some (not necessarily serious) physical harm (*R v Church* [1966] 1 QB 59); and (3) that **causes death**, applying ordinary causation rules. The defendant need not have foreseen any harm at all -- the test is entirely objective from the reasonable bystander's viewpoint (*DPP v Newbury and Jones* [1977] AC 500 -- teenagers who pushed a piece of paving stone off a railway bridge into the path of an oncoming train, killing the guard, were liable even though they said they had not thought about whether anyone might be hurt, since a reasonable bystander would recognise the risk). **Gross negligence manslaughter** requires: (1) a **duty of care** owed by the defendant to the victim, applying ordinary negligence principles (NEG.1); (2) a **breach** of that duty; (3) the breach **causes death**; and (4) the negligence is so bad, in the jury's view, that it should be characterised as criminal -- **gross negligence** (*R v Adomako* [1995] 1 AC 171, the leading case -- an anaesthetist who failed to notice a disconnected oxygen tube during an operation, causing the patient's death, was liable; the House of Lords confirmed ordinary civil negligence principles establish the duty and breach, but the jury must then separately decide whether the negligence was gross enough, having regard to the risk of death, to amount to a crime -- a question ultimately left to the jury's judgment rather than a precise legal formula). This form of manslaughter can, unlike unlawful act manslaughter, be committed by omission, provided a duty to act existed (see GE.1).
