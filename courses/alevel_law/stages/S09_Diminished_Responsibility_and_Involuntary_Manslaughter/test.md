## Test items
1. Explain the three elements of unlawful act (constructive) manslaughter, with authority. [6 marks]
2. Hana, a care worker with a diagnosed, severe psychotic disorder, kills a patient in her care while experiencing a delusion that the patient was about to attack her. Separately, Iwo, a builder, fails to check that a heavy scaffold board is properly secured despite clear signs of risk, and it falls and kills a passer-by. Using IRAC, advise on the most appropriate charge/defence route for each. [12 marks]

## Answer key (for the tutor only)
1. [6] M2 an unlawful (criminal) act, which cannot be an omission alone (R v Lowe [1973] QB 702); M2 the act must be dangerous, judged objectively by whether a sober and reasonable person would recognise a risk of some physical harm (R v Church [1966] 1 QB 59); M2 the act must cause death, applying ordinary causation rules, and the defendant need not personally have foreseen any harm (DPP v Newbury and Jones [1977] AC 500).
2. [12] Mark using issue-rule-application-conclusion (IRAC): identify the issue(s), state the rule precisely with its authority (case or statute, named exactly), apply it to the facts given, and reach a supported conclusion. Credit: issue = whether Hana can rely on diminished responsibility for a murder charge, and whether Iwo is liable for gross negligence manslaughter; rule = diminished responsibility requires an abnormality of mental functioning from a recognised medical condition that substantially impaired understanding/rational judgment/self-control and explains the killing (s2 Homicide Act 1957 as amended, R v Golds [2016] UKSC 61 on 'substantially'); gross negligence manslaughter requires a duty of care, breach, causation of death, and negligence so gross a jury would call it criminal (R v Adomako [1995] 1 AC 171); application = Hana's diagnosed severe psychotic disorder causing a delusional belief plausibly satisfies diminished responsibility's medical-condition and impaired-judgment/understanding requirements, reducing any murder charge to manslaughter; Iwo, as a builder, owed a duty of care to passers-by, breached it by failing an obvious safety check, and the death followed -- the jury would need to decide if the negligence was gross enough to be criminal rather than merely a civil wrong; conclusion = Hana has a strong diminished responsibility case reducing murder to voluntary manslaughter; Iwo is exposed to gross negligence manslaughter, subject to the jury's assessment of how gross the negligence was.
