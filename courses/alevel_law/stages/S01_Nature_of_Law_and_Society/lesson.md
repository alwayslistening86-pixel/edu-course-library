# S01_Nature_of_Law_and_Society - Lesson: Nature of law; law and society; law and morality; law and justice

## Goal
The learner explains what makes a rule legally enforceable rather than merely social or moral, the civil/criminal distinction, law's role in balancing competing interests and the meaning of fault, the contested relationship between law and morality, and the main theories of justice and how far law achieves it.

## Syllabus items taught here
- NL.1 - The nature of law: enforceable legal rules distinguished from other social/moral rules; the distinction between civil and criminal law; the main sources of law (custom, case law, legislation)
- NL.2 - Law and society: the role of law in regulating society and balancing competing interests; enforceable legal rights and obligations; the meaning and importance of fault
- NL.3 - Law and morality: the distinction between law and morality in a pluralist society; the relationship between the two; the extent to which law enforces moral values
- NL.4 - Law and justice: the meaning of justice, and theories of justice (formal, procedural, distributive, corrective/commutative); the extent to which law achieves justice in civil and criminal contexts

## How to teach this
Ask: queuing is a social rule almost everyone follows, but breaking it isn't illegal -- so what actually makes a rule *law*? Teach each rule as: the precise legal test (statute section or case authority, stated exactly, since AQA rewards named, accurate authority), why it exists (the policy AQA's own specification names, e.g. public policy limits on the duty of care, freedom of contract vs consumer protection), and how it applies to facts -- AQA's application and scenario questions (worth over half the marks on every paper) need the rule applied to the specific facts given, not restated in the abstract. Use AQA's own command words precisely: 'explain'/'suggest' target AO1 (accurate, precise legal knowledge); 'advise'/'consider' target AO1+AO2+AO3 together (apply the law to a scenario and reach a reasoned conclusion); 'examine'/'discuss'/'assess' target AO3 (evaluate non-substantive law, or substantive law within a non-substantive framework, e.g. discussing judicial precedent's advantages through a case scenario). Push the learner to build a bank of exact case names, years and the precise principle each establishes -- a vague 'there was a case about...' earns nothing under AQA's marking. Law changes: this course states the law of England and Wales as understood to be current in September 2026; statutory thresholds, ongoing reform and any pending legislation should be checked against a live source (legislation.gov.uk, aqa.org.uk) before a real exam sitting.

#### NL.1 The nature of law: enforceable legal rules distinguished from other social/moral rules; the distinction between civil and criminal law; the main sources of law (custom, case law, legislation)
**The nature of law.** Law is a body of enforceable rules, backed by the state's coercive power (courts, sanctions), that governs conduct and relationships in society -- distinguished from other social rules (etiquette, religious rules, moral codes) which may carry social disapproval but no legal sanction if broken. Law is created and recognised through defined sources: **custom** (local practices long recognised as binding, now a minor source), **case law/common law** (judge-made law through precedent), and **legislation** (Acts of Parliament and delegated legislation). Law is further classified as **public law** (regulates the relationship between the state and individuals/organisations, e.g. criminal law, constitutional law) or **private law** (regulates relationships between individuals, e.g. contract, tort); and, cutting across that, as **criminal law** (conduct the state prosecutes as an offence against society as a whole, proved beyond reasonable doubt, styled *R v Defendant*, resulting in punishment) or **civil law** (disputes between private parties over rights and obligations, proved on the balance of probabilities, styled *Claimant v Defendant*, resulting in a remedy such as damages).

#### NL.2 Law and society: the role of law in regulating society and balancing competing interests; enforceable legal rights and obligations; the meaning and importance of fault
**Law and society.** Law regulates society by setting the boundaries of acceptable conduct and providing mechanisms to resolve disputes and enforce rights, so individuals can rely on legally protected expectations (e.g. that a contract will be honoured, or that they will not be assaulted) rather than self-help. A central function is **balancing competing interests**: an individual's right to free expression against another's right to privacy or protection from harassment; a landowner's freedom to use their land against a neighbour's right to enjoy theirs (nuisance); the state's interest in public safety against an individual's liberty. **Enforceable rights and obligations** are what distinguish a legal claim from a moral or social expectation -- a legal right can be vindicated through the courts, with a remedy the state will enforce. **Fault** is central to much of English law: criminal liability generally requires *mens rea* (a guilty state of mind) alongside the prohibited conduct, and civil liability in tort and much of contract turns on whether the defendant is at fault (e.g. negligence requires a breach of the standard of care); but law also recognises **strict** and **no-fault liability** in limited areas (e.g. some regulatory criminal offences, or contractual liability for breach regardless of blame), reflecting a policy choice that fault is not always the right or practicable basis for liability.

#### NL.3 Law and morality: the distinction between law and morality in a pluralist society; the relationship between the two; the extent to which law enforces moral values
**Law and morality.** Morality is a society's shared or individual sense of right and wrong, which may vary between individuals, communities and over time; the UK today is a **pluralist society** with a range of moral and religious views rather than one shared moral code, which makes it harder for law simply to enact "the" moral view. Law and morality overlap substantially (murder and theft are both illegal and widely considered immoral) but are not the same: some immoral conduct is not illegal (e.g. adultery, or lying to a friend), and some illegal conduct is not obviously immoral (e.g. many strict liability regulatory offences, or historic laws later repealed, such as those criminalising homosexuality before the Sexual Offences Act 1967). The **Hart-Devlin debate** captures the tension: Lord Devlin argued law should enforce a shared public morality to preserve the fabric of society, while Hart (following Mill's harm principle) argued the state should only intervene where conduct harms others, not merely because a majority disapproves of private conduct. In practice Parliament and the courts continually decide how far to let law enforce moral values -- e.g. in areas such as assisted dying, indecency or drug use -- reflecting shifting social consensus rather than a fixed answer.

#### NL.4 Law and justice: the meaning of justice, and theories of justice (formal, procedural, distributive, corrective/commutative); the extent to which law achieves justice in civil and criminal contexts
**Law and justice.** "Justice" has no single agreed meaning, but recognised theories include: **formal justice** (like cases treated alike, applying rules consistently and impartially); **procedural justice** (a fair process -- the right to be heard, an unbiased decision-maker, natural justice); **distributive justice** (a fair allocation of society's benefits and burdens); and **corrective/commutative justice** (putting right a wrong done by one party to another, e.g. through compensation or punishment proportionate to the wrong). The **extent to which law achieves justice** is contestable in both civil and criminal contexts: in civil law, unequal access to funding can undermine procedural justice even where the substantive rules are fair (see Access to justice, AJ.1); in criminal law, strict liability offences can convict a defendant who is not morally at fault (arguably offending corrective justice), while overly rigid rules (e.g. the historic position that a battered woman without an immediate threat could not always rely on self-defence) have been criticised as producing unjust outcomes, prompting reform such as the loss of control defence (FO.2). AQA essay questions on this topic (e.g. "discuss the extent to which the law on X achieves justice") expect the learner to apply a named theory of justice to specific, accurately stated rules -- not just assert that an outcome "feels unfair".
