# S17_Causation_Remoteness_and_Economic_Loss - Lesson: Damage, causation and remoteness; pure economic loss

## Goal
The learner applies factual and legal causation and the remoteness test in negligence, and explains the more restrictive rules for recovering pure economic loss.

## Syllabus items taught here
- NEG.3 - Liability in negligence: damage -- factual causation (the 'but for' test), legal causation (intervening acts) and remoteness of damage (the type of harm must be a reasonably foreseeable kind)
- ECO.1 - Liability in negligence for pure economic loss: loss from negligent acts (generally not recoverable) and loss from negligent misstatement (recoverable where a special relationship of proximity/reliance exists)

## How to teach this
Ask: a doctor negligently fails to treat a patient who was going to die anyway -- did the negligence actually cause the death in law? Teach each rule as: the precise legal test (statute section or case authority, stated exactly, since AQA rewards named, accurate authority), why it exists (the policy AQA's own specification names, e.g. public policy limits on the duty of care, freedom of contract vs consumer protection), and how it applies to facts -- AQA's application and scenario questions (worth over half the marks on every paper) need the rule applied to the specific facts given, not restated in the abstract. Use AQA's own command words precisely: 'explain'/'suggest' target AO1 (accurate, precise legal knowledge); 'advise'/'consider' target AO1+AO2+AO3 together (apply the law to a scenario and reach a reasoned conclusion); 'examine'/'discuss'/'assess' target AO3 (evaluate non-substantive law, or substantive law within a non-substantive framework, e.g. discussing judicial precedent's advantages through a case scenario). Push the learner to build a bank of exact case names, years and the precise principle each establishes -- a vague 'there was a case about...' earns nothing under AQA's marking. Law changes: this course states the law of England and Wales as understood to be current in September 2026; statutory thresholds, ongoing reform and any pending legislation should be checked against a live source (legislation.gov.uk, aqa.org.uk) before a real exam sitting.

#### NEG.3 Liability in negligence: damage -- factual causation (the 'but for' test), legal causation (intervening acts) and remoteness of damage (the type of harm must be a reasonably foreseeable kind)
**Liability in negligence: damage, causation and remoteness.** The claimant must show the defendant's breach **caused** their damage, and that the damage is not too **remote**. **Factual causation** applies the **"but for" test**: but for the defendant's breach, would the claimant's damage have occurred? (*Barnett v Chelsea & Kensington Hospital Management Committee* [1969] 1 QB 428 -- a doctor negligently failed to examine a patient who later died of arsenic poisoning, but the claim failed on causation, since the patient would have died regardless even with proper treatment -- the negligence was not, in fact, the cause of death). **Legal causation** requires the breach to remain an operating and substantial cause, not broken by a **novus actus interveniens** -- applying the same intervening-act principles as in criminal law (see GE.1), e.g. an unforeseeable, independent act by a third party or the claimant themselves can break the chain, but a foreseeable consequence (including, per the "thin skull" rule, the claimant's own particular vulnerability) will not. **Remoteness of damage** asks whether the **type** of harm suffered was a **reasonably foreseeable** consequence of the breach -- the test from ***The Wagon Mound (No 1)* [1961] AC 388** (oil negligently spilled into a harbour, which was thought unlikely to ignite on water but did, causing fire damage to a wharf -- the Privy Council held the damage was too remote, since fire damage of that kind was not a reasonably foreseeable consequence of an oil spill on water, even though some form of damage, e.g. fouling, plainly was). Provided the **type** of harm was foreseeable, it does not matter that its precise **extent** or the exact **manner** it came about was not (*Smith v Leech Brain & Co Ltd* [1962] 2 QB 405 -- a minor burn to the lip, foreseeable, triggered cancer in a claimant with a pre-existing susceptibility, and unusually severe/fatal consequences; the defendant remained liable for the full extent of the harm once the type -- a burn injury -- was foreseeable, applying the thin skull rule; *Robinson v Post Office* [1974] 1 WLR 1176 -- a foreseeable minor wound became infected due to an unforeseeable allergic reaction to an anti-tetanus injection; since the type of harm, infection following the wound, remained foreseeable, the defendant was liable for the full, unusually severe consequence).

#### ECO.1 Liability in negligence for pure economic loss: loss from negligent acts (generally not recoverable) and loss from negligent misstatement (recoverable where a special relationship of proximity/reliance exists)
**Liability in negligence for pure economic loss.** **Pure economic loss** (financial loss not consequential on any physical injury to the claimant or damage to their property, e.g. lost profit from being unable to use a machine that was never actually damaged) is, as a matter of policy, generally **not recoverable** in negligence, to avoid indeterminate liability to an indeterminate class for an indeterminate amount, and because such loss is often better protected (if at all) through contract. **Loss consequential on physical damage** (e.g. lost profit *because* the claimant's own property was physically damaged) is recoverable as an ordinary head of damage; **pure** economic loss with no accompanying physical damage to the claimant's own property is not, even where a third party's property was damaged (*Spartan Steel & Alloys Ltd v Martin & Co (Contractors) Ltd* [1973] QB 27 -- a power cut caused by the defendant's negligence damaged the claimant's furnace and the metal being melted in it at the time (recoverable, being physical damage and the loss of profit on that specific melt, consequential on it), but the claimant's further lost profit on melts that could not be carried out at all because of the power cut, with no physical damage involved, was pure economic loss and irrecoverable). A distinct, narrower exception allows recovery for pure economic loss caused by a **negligent misstatement**, where a **special relationship** of proximity and reasonable reliance exists between the parties -- the foundational case is ***Hedley Byrne & Co Ltd v Heller & Partners Ltd* [1964] AC 465** (a bank gave a negligent credit reference, knowing or having reason to know it would be relied on for a specific purpose; though the claim itself failed because the bank had included an effective disclaimer of responsibility, the House of Lords confirmed a duty could in principle arise for negligent advice/information where a special relationship of proximity and reasonable, known reliance exists). The special-relationship requirement is applied narrowly, informed by Caparo's fair/just/reasonable stage: it typically requires the defendant to have **assumed responsibility** for the accuracy of the statement, knowing (or it being reasonable to expect) the claimant would rely on it for the purpose in question (*Chaudhry v Prabhakar* [1989] 1 WLR 29 -- even informal, unpaid advice from a friend with claimed expertise about a car purchase could found the necessary special relationship, since the adviser held himself out as knowledgeable and knew reliance would follow).
