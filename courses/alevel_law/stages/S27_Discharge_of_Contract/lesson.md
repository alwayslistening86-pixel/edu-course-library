# S27_Discharge_of_Contract - Lesson: Performance and breach; frustration

## Goal
The learner explains discharge by performance and by breach (actual and anticipatory), and discharge by frustration including the effect of the Law Reform (Frustrated Contracts) Act 1943.

## Syllabus items taught here
- DIS.1 - Discharge of a contract: by performance (the general rule of complete and exact performance, and its exceptions) and by breach (actual and anticipatory breach, and when a breach entitles the innocent party to treat the contract as discharged)
- DIS.2 - Discharge of a contract: by frustration -- events making performance impossible, illegal or radically different from what was contracted for, and the effect of the Law Reform (Frustrated Contracts) Act 1943

## How to teach this
Ask: an event neither party caused or could have prevented makes performing the contract impossible -- who bears the loss? Teach each rule as: the precise legal test (statute section or case authority, stated exactly, since AQA rewards named, accurate authority), why it exists (the policy AQA's own specification names, e.g. public policy limits on the duty of care, freedom of contract vs consumer protection), and how it applies to facts -- AQA's application and scenario questions (worth over half the marks on every paper) need the rule applied to the specific facts given, not restated in the abstract. Use AQA's own command words precisely: 'explain'/'suggest' target AO1 (accurate, precise legal knowledge); 'advise'/'consider' target AO1+AO2+AO3 together (apply the law to a scenario and reach a reasoned conclusion); 'examine'/'discuss'/'assess' target AO3 (evaluate non-substantive law, or substantive law within a non-substantive framework, e.g. discussing judicial precedent's advantages through a case scenario). Push the learner to build a bank of exact case names, years and the precise principle each establishes -- a vague 'there was a case about...' earns nothing under AQA's marking. Law changes: this course states the law of England and Wales as understood to be current in September 2026; statutory thresholds, ongoing reform and any pending legislation should be checked against a live source (legislation.gov.uk, aqa.org.uk) before a real exam sitting.

#### DIS.1 Discharge of a contract: by performance (the general rule of complete and exact performance, and its exceptions) and by breach (actual and anticipatory breach, and when a breach entitles the innocent party to treat the contract as discharged)
**Discharge by performance and breach.** A contract is discharged by **performance** when both parties have completely and exactly carried out their obligations -- the general common law rule requires **complete and exact performance** before payment is due (though subject to exceptions the syllabus does not require in detail, e.g. substantial performance, and severable/divisible contracts where partial performance can still be paid for proportionately). **Breach** occurs where a party fails to perform an obligation as agreed, without lawful excuse. **Actual breach** is a failure to perform when performance is due (which, depending on whether the term breached is a condition, warranty, or innominate term with a sufficiently serious effect -- see TERMS.1 -- may or may not entitle the innocent party to terminate). **Anticipatory breach** occurs where a party, before performance is due, makes clear (by words or conduct) that they do not intend to perform -- the innocent party may either **accept the anticipatory breach immediately** and sue at once for damages, without waiting for the performance date to arrive (*Hochster v De La Tour* (1853) 2 E & B 678 -- a courier engaged for a future summer job, told before the job began that his services were no longer required, could sue for breach immediately, without having to wait for the date performance was actually due), or **affirm the contract**, keep it alive, and wait for the performance date, continuing to press for performance where practicable (*White and Carter (Councils) Ltd v McGregor* [1962] AC 413 -- an advertiser, told in advance that the other party no longer wanted the advertising contract performed, was nonetheless entitled to ignore the repudiation, continue performing as agreed, and claim the full contract price, since an innocent party generally has a genuine choice whether to accept a repudiatory breach or affirm the contract and perform anyway, at least where they do not need the other party's cooperation to do so).

#### DIS.2 Discharge of a contract: by frustration -- events making performance impossible, illegal or radically different from what was contracted for, and the effect of the Law Reform (Frustrated Contracts) Act 1943
**Discharge by frustration.** A contract is **discharged automatically** (neither party's fault, and without either needing to terminate it) where, after formation, an unforeseen event makes performance **impossible**, **illegal**, or **radically different** from what was originally undertaken, without either party's fault. **Impossibility**: the classic case is destruction of the specific subject matter (*Taylor v Caldwell* (1863) 3 B & S 826 -- a music hall hired for a series of concerts burnt down, without either party's fault, before the first concert, frustrating the contract, since its entire subject matter had been destroyed). **Radical difference from what was contracted for** (sometimes described as frustration of a common, shared purpose central to the contract): where an event does not make performance literally impossible but removes the entire foundation/purpose both parties understood the contract to be for (*Krell v Henry* [1903] 2 KB 740 -- a room hired specifically to view the King's coronation procession, which was then cancelled due to the King's illness, was frustrated, since watching the procession was the entire, mutually understood commercial purpose of the hire, even though the room itself remained perfectly available to use for other purposes); contrast a case where the event affects the *profitability* or a party's *motive* for contracting, without destroying a shared, essential purpose the contract was expressly or impliedly for (*Herne Bay Steam Boat Co v Hutton* [1903] 2 KB 683 -- hiring a boat to view a naval review, cancelled for the same reason as the coronation procession in Krell, was **not** frustrated, since the boat could still be used to cruise round the fleet and see the ships generally, a purpose the contract could still substantially serve, distinguishing it from Krell's single, specific, now-impossible purpose). The general test, from ***Davis Contractors Ltd v Fareham Urban District Council* [1956] AC 696**, asks whether the new circumstances are so radically different from what was undertaken that it would be unjust to hold the parties to the literal terms in the new situation -- mere increased cost, delay or hardship, without more, is not enough. **Effect**: the contract is discharged automatically at the moment of the frustrating event, and, under the **Law Reform (Frustrated Contracts) Act 1943**, money paid before the frustrating event is generally recoverable (subject to the court's discretion to allow the payee to retain/recover expenses reasonably incurred), and sums due but unpaid before that event cease to be payable, providing a fairer allocation of loss than the earlier common law position, which could leave the loss falling arbitrarily on whichever party happened to have paid (or not yet paid) at the moment of frustration.
