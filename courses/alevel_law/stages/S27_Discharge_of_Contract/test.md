## Test items
1. Explain, with authority, the distinction drawn between Krell v Henry [1903] 2 KB 740 and Herne Bay Steam Boat Co v Hutton [1903] 2 KB 683 on frustration of a shared purpose. [8 marks]
2. A wedding photography company is booked, months in advance, to photograph a large outdoor wedding at a specific historic venue on a set date; a week before the wedding, new government regulations, introduced without warning in response to a public health emergency, make gatherings of that size at that type of venue unlawful for the foreseeable future, and the couple are forced to cancel entirely. Using IRAC, advise the photography company on whether the contract is frustrated. [10 marks]

## Answer key (for the tutor only)
1. [8] M2 Krell v Henry: a room hired specifically to view the coronation procession, then cancelled, was frustrated since viewing the procession was the entire, mutually understood, single purpose of the hire; M2 Herne Bay Steam Boat Co v Hutton: hiring a boat to view a naval review, cancelled for the same reason, was not frustrated, since the boat could still be used to cruise and view the fleet more generally, a purpose the contract could still substantially serve; M2 the distinguishing principle: frustration requires the specific, essential, shared purpose of the contract to be destroyed, not merely one possible use or motive among others; M2 credit the general Davis Contractors [1956] AC 696 test (whether the new circumstances are radically different from what was undertaken) as the underlying standard both cases apply.
2. [10] Mark using issue-rule-application-conclusion (IRAC): identify the issue(s), state the rule precisely with its authority (case or statute, named exactly), apply it to the facts given, and reach a supported conclusion. Credit: issue = whether the new regulations frustrate the photography contract; rule = frustration discharges a contract automatically where an unforeseen, post-formation event makes performance impossible, illegal, or radically different from what was undertaken, without either party's fault (Taylor v Caldwell (1863) 3 B & S 826; Davis Contractors Ltd v Fareham UDC [1956] AC 696); performance that becomes genuinely illegal is a recognised frustrating event; application = the new regulations making the specific gathering unlawful render performance of photographing that particular large gathering at that venue on that date genuinely illegal, not merely more difficult, expensive or less convenient, which is a stronger basis for frustration than a case like Herne Bay where an alternative substantially similar purpose remained available; conclusion = the contract is likely frustrated by supervening illegality, discharging both parties automatically from further performance; under the Law Reform (Frustrated Contracts) Act 1943, any deposit already paid by the couple would generally be recoverable (subject to the court's discretion to allow the photography company to retain reasonably incurred expenses, e.g. non-refundable pre-booking costs), and any balance not yet paid would cease to be due.
