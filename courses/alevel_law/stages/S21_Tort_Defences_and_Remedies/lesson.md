# S21_Tort_Defences_and_Remedies - Lesson: Contributory negligence and consent; damages and injunctions

## Goal
The learner explains the tort defences of contributory negligence and consent, and the remedies of compensatory damages and injunctions.

## Syllabus items taught here
- TDEF.1 - Tort defences: contributory negligence (Law Reform (Contributory Negligence) Act 1945, apportionment) and consent/volenti non fit injuria (full defence, requiring genuine free and informed agreement to the risk)
- REM.1 - Tort remedies: compensatory damages (special/general damages for physical injury, property and economic loss, and the duty to mitigate loss) and injunctions (to restrain a continuing tort such as nuisance)

## How to teach this
Ask: a claimant partly caused their own injury -- does that defeat their claim entirely, or just reduce it? Teach each rule as: the precise legal test (statute section or case authority, stated exactly, since AQA rewards named, accurate authority), why it exists (the policy AQA's own specification names, e.g. public policy limits on the duty of care, freedom of contract vs consumer protection), and how it applies to facts -- AQA's application and scenario questions (worth over half the marks on every paper) need the rule applied to the specific facts given, not restated in the abstract. Use AQA's own command words precisely: 'explain'/'suggest' target AO1 (accurate, precise legal knowledge); 'advise'/'consider' target AO1+AO2+AO3 together (apply the law to a scenario and reach a reasoned conclusion); 'examine'/'discuss'/'assess' target AO3 (evaluate non-substantive law, or substantive law within a non-substantive framework, e.g. discussing judicial precedent's advantages through a case scenario). Push the learner to build a bank of exact case names, years and the precise principle each establishes -- a vague 'there was a case about...' earns nothing under AQA's marking. Law changes: this course states the law of England and Wales as understood to be current in September 2026; statutory thresholds, ongoing reform and any pending legislation should be checked against a live source (legislation.gov.uk, aqa.org.uk) before a real exam sitting.

#### TDEF.1 Tort defences: contributory negligence (Law Reform (Contributory Negligence) Act 1945, apportionment) and consent/volenti non fit injuria (full defence, requiring genuine free and informed agreement to the risk)
**Tort defences: contributory negligence and consent.** **Contributory negligence** is a **partial defence** under the **Law Reform (Contributory Negligence) Act 1945**: where the claimant's own carelessness contributed to their damage, the court **apportions** liability, reducing the damages awarded by whatever percentage is "just and equitable having regard to the claimant's share in the responsibility for the damage" (s1(1)) -- it does not defeat the claim entirely, only reduces the sum recovered. A classic illustration is *Froom v Butcher* [1976] QB 286 (a claimant injured in a car accident caused by the defendant's negligent driving, who was not wearing a seatbelt, had their damages reduced -- not because the accident was their fault, but because their own failure to take an obvious, easy precaution contributed to the severity of the injury they suffered, the Court of Appeal setting out now-standard guideline reductions for seatbelt cases). **Consent (volenti non fit injuria)** is a **complete defence** (a full bar to the claim) where the claimant **freely and voluntarily** agreed, with full knowledge of the nature and extent of the risk, to accept the risk of the specific harm that occurred -- mere knowledge of a risk is not enough; there must be genuine, informed agreement to run it (*Morris v Murray* [1991] 2 QB 6 -- a passenger who chose to fly with a pilot he knew to be very drunk, in a light aircraft, was volens to the risk, having freely and fully appreciated the obvious danger). Where an employee is under an employer's instructions and simply carries out their job despite an awareness of some risk, courts are generally reluctant to find true volenti, since the employee's continuing to work is not always a genuinely free choice given the pressures of employment -- but a clear, deliberate breach of known safety rules by employees acting entirely outside management's control can still found volenti (*ICI Ltd v Shatwell* [1965] AC 656 -- two experienced shotfirers who, contrary to clear statutory safety procedure and their employer's express instructions, tested detonators without taking cover, were volens to the risk that resulted, since their own deliberate, informed disregard of known safety rules -- not any fault of the employer -- caused the injury).

#### REM.1 Tort remedies: compensatory damages (special/general damages for physical injury, property and economic loss, and the duty to mitigate loss) and injunctions (to restrain a continuing tort such as nuisance)
**Tort remedies: damages and injunctions.** The primary remedy in tort is **compensatory damages**, aiming to put the claimant, so far as money can, in the position they would have been in had the tort not been committed (*Livingstone v Rawyards Coal Co* (1880) 5 App Cas 25 sets out this restorative principle). Damages are categorised as **special damages** (quantifiable, pre-trial financial losses, e.g. lost earnings to date, medical/repair bills already incurred, calculated precisely) and **general damages** (losses not precisely quantifiable at the date of trial, e.g. pain, suffering and loss of amenity for personal injury, and future losses such as future lost earnings or care costs, assessed by the court using guidelines/comparable awards). Damages can cover **physical injury** (pain, suffering, loss of amenity, medical expenses, lost earnings), **property damage** (the cost of repair, or diminution in value if repair is uneconomic), and, where recoverable at all (see ECO.1), **economic loss**. The claimant is under a **duty to mitigate** their loss -- they cannot recover for losses they could reasonably have avoided by taking reasonable steps (e.g. seeking alternative income, or reasonably necessary medical treatment), and the defendant bears the burden of showing the claimant failed to mitigate reasonably. **Injunctions** are an equitable, discretionary remedy, typically used to restrain a **continuing** tort (most commonly private nuisance) rather than to compensate for past harm -- a court may grant a **prohibitory injunction** (ordering the defendant to stop an activity) where damages alone would not adequately protect the claimant's rights, though courts have discretion to award damages *in lieu* of an injunction where the interference is modest, the harm to the claimant small, and it would be oppressive to the defendant to grant an injunction (*Miller v Jackson* [1977] QB 966 -- cricket balls occasionally landing in a neighbouring garden constituted a nuisance, but the Court of Appeal (by majority) declined to grant an injunction that would have stopped a long-established village cricket club from playing at all, awarding damages instead, given the public interest in the club continuing and the relatively modest interference); contrast *Kennaway v Thompson* [1981] QB 88 (a club's motorboat racing, causing more serious and persistent noise nuisance to a neighbouring landowner, was restrained by injunction limiting the level and frequency of racing permitted, the interference there being considered too serious for damages alone to be an adequate substitute).
