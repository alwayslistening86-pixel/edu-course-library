## Test items
1. Explain, with authority, the claimant's duty to mitigate loss when calculating tort damages. [5 marks]
2. A small residential care home's noisy, poorly maintained generator, run daily for several hours, causes persistent, significant noise disturbance to an elderly neighbour with no obvious way to reduce the impact except running it less or better maintaining it, which the home has refused to do despite repeated complaints over a year. Using IRAC, advise the neighbour on the most appropriate remedy if a nuisance claim succeeds. [10 marks]

## Answer key (for the tutor only)
1. [5] B2 the claimant cannot recover for losses they could reasonably have avoided by taking reasonable steps, e.g. reasonably seeking alternative income or necessary medical treatment; B2 the burden is on the defendant to show the claimant failed to mitigate reasonably; B1 mitigation applies alongside the general compensatory principle of putting the claimant, so far as money can, in the position they would have been in had the tort not occurred (Livingstone v Rawyards Coal Co (1880) 5 App Cas 25).
2. [10] Mark using issue-rule-application-conclusion (IRAC): identify the issue(s), state the rule precisely with its authority (case or statute, named exactly), apply it to the facts given, and reach a supported conclusion. Credit: issue = whether damages or an injunction is the more appropriate remedy for an established, persistent nuisance; rule = courts may award a prohibitory injunction to restrain a continuing nuisance where damages would not adequately protect the claimant's rights, but retain discretion to award damages in lieu where the interference is modest and an injunction would be oppressive (Miller v Jackson [1977] QB 966); a more serious, persistent interference is more likely to attract an injunction (Kennaway v Thompson [1981] QB 88); application = the noise here is persistent (daily, over a year), significant, and the home has refused reasonable remedial steps despite repeated complaints, closer to the serious, ongoing interference in Kennaway than the more occasional, lower-impact interference in Miller v Jackson; conclusion = an injunction (potentially limiting the hours/frequency the generator may be run, rather than banning it outright, mirroring Kennaway's partial restriction) is likely the more appropriate remedy here, since damages alone would not stop the ongoing interference and the home's refusal to act on repeated complaints weakens any argument that an injunction would be unduly oppressive.
