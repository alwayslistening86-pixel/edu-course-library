# S08_Murder_and_Loss_of_Control - Lesson: Murder; voluntary manslaughter -- loss of control

## Goal
The learner states the actus reus and mens rea of murder, and explains the partial defence of loss of control -- its qualifying triggers and the objective standard of self-restraint expected -- including why it reduces murder to voluntary manslaughter rather than acquitting.

## Syllabus items taught here
- FO.1 - Fatal offences: murder -- the actus reus (unlawful killing of a person under the King's Peace) and mens rea (intention to kill or cause grievous bodily harm)
- FO.2 - Fatal offences: voluntary manslaughter -- loss of control under s54 Coroners and Justice Act 2009 (qualifying triggers, the objective standard of self-restraint expected)

## How to teach this
Ask: two people kill in near-identical circumstances, but one is provoked by sustained abuse just before -- should the law ever treat that as something less than murder? Teach each rule as: the precise legal test (statute section or case authority, stated exactly, since AQA rewards named, accurate authority), why it exists (the policy AQA's own specification names, e.g. public policy limits on the duty of care, freedom of contract vs consumer protection), and how it applies to facts -- AQA's application and scenario questions (worth over half the marks on every paper) need the rule applied to the specific facts given, not restated in the abstract. Use AQA's own command words precisely: 'explain'/'suggest' target AO1 (accurate, precise legal knowledge); 'advise'/'consider' target AO1+AO2+AO3 together (apply the law to a scenario and reach a reasoned conclusion); 'examine'/'discuss'/'assess' target AO3 (evaluate non-substantive law, or substantive law within a non-substantive framework, e.g. discussing judicial precedent's advantages through a case scenario). Push the learner to build a bank of exact case names, years and the precise principle each establishes -- a vague 'there was a case about...' earns nothing under AQA's marking. Law changes: this course states the law of England and Wales as understood to be current in September 2026; statutory thresholds, ongoing reform and any pending legislation should be checked against a live source (legislation.gov.uk, aqa.org.uk) before a real exam sitting.

#### FO.1 Fatal offences: murder -- the actus reus (unlawful killing of a person under the King's Peace) and mens rea (intention to kill or cause grievous bodily harm)
**Murder.** A common law offence (never codified by statute), defined as the unlawful killing of a human being under the King's/Queen's Peace, with malice aforethought. **Actus reus**: the unlawful killing of a person (not, e.g., a lawful killing in self-defence -- see DEF.3) who is 'in being' (born alive and independent of the mother) and dies within the (now largely academic, since medical causation can usually be proved regardless) year-and-a-day at common law, caused by the defendant's act, applying the ordinary causation rules (GE.1). **Mens rea**: 'malice aforethought' means **intention to kill (express malice)** or **intention to cause grievous bodily harm (implied malice)** -- GBH-intent alone is sufficient for murder even without any intention to kill (*R v Vickers* [1957] 2 QB 664, approved by the House of Lords in *DPP v Smith* [1961] AC 290 and reaffirmed since, e.g. in *R v Cunningham* [1982] AC 566, a different Cunningham from the recklessness case). Oblique intention (GE.2, *R v Woollin* [1999] 1 AC 82) can also satisfy the mens rea for murder where death or GBH was a virtually certain consequence of the defendant's act and the defendant appreciated that. The mandatory sentence for murder is life imprisonment, with a judge setting a minimum term before parole eligibility.

#### FO.2 Fatal offences: voluntary manslaughter -- loss of control under s54 Coroners and Justice Act 2009 (qualifying triggers, the objective standard of self-restraint expected)
**Voluntary manslaughter: loss of control.** A **partial defence to murder only** (reducing murder to voluntary manslaughter, not an acquittal) under **s54 Coroners and Justice Act 2009**, replacing the old common law defence of provocation. The defendant must show: (1) a **loss of self-control** (not necessarily sudden, unlike the old law, but genuinely a loss of control, not a considered revenge killing -- s54(4) expressly excludes a defence where the defendant acted in a considered desire for revenge); (2) the loss of control had a **qualifying trigger** under s55 -- either the defendant's **fear of serious violence** from the victim against the defendant or another identified person, or things done or said (or both) which constituted circumstances of an **extremely grave character** and caused the defendant to have a **justifiable sense of being seriously wronged** (s55(4)); **sexual infidelity alone** is expressly excluded as a qualifying trigger by s55(6)(c), though it may still be considered as part of the wider factual context alongside another qualifying trigger (*R v Clinton* [2012] EWCA Crim 2 -- sexual infidelity could not stand alone as the trigger, but could be considered together with other circumstances, such as taunting about the infidelity combined with other grave conduct); and (3) a **person of the defendant's sex and age, with a normal degree of tolerance and self-restraint and in the defendant's circumstances**, might have reacted in a similar way (an objective standard, though the defendant's circumstances -- excluding those bearing only on their general capacity for tolerance/self-restraint -- are taken into account, s54(3)). The defence exists because the old law of provocation was widely criticised as too narrow (requiring a sudden loss of control ill-suited to, e.g., victims of sustained domestic abuse who might snap after a period of build-up rather than instantly) and as capable of excusing a quick-tempered killer too readily; the 2009 reforms sought a fairer, more carefully structured partial defence reflecting genuine reduced culpability rather than full moral condemnation as a murderer.
