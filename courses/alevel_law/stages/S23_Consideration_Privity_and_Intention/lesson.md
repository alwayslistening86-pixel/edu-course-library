# S23_Consideration_Privity_and_Intention - Lesson: Consideration and promissory estoppel, privity; intention to create legal relations

## Goal
The learner explains the rules on consideration (including promissory estoppel and privity), and the rebuttable presumptions on intention to create legal relations in social/domestic and commercial contexts.

## Syllabus items taught here
- FORM.3 - Essential requirements of a valid contract: consideration -- must have some value but need not be adequate, must move from the promisee, and cannot be past; promissory estoppel as a limited exception where a promise is relied on; privity of contract and the Contracts (Rights of Third Parties) Act 1999
- FORM.4 - Essential requirements of a valid contract: intention to create legal relations -- the rebuttable presumptions in social/domestic and commercial agreements, and how each presumption can be displaced on the facts

## How to teach this
Ask: someone promises to waive part of a debt, then changes their mind and demands the full amount -- can a promise with 'nothing given in return' ever still bind the promisor? Teach each rule as: the precise legal test (statute section or case authority, stated exactly, since AQA rewards named, accurate authority), why it exists (the policy AQA's own specification names, e.g. public policy limits on the duty of care, freedom of contract vs consumer protection), and how it applies to facts -- AQA's application and scenario questions (worth over half the marks on every paper) need the rule applied to the specific facts given, not restated in the abstract. Use AQA's own command words precisely: 'explain'/'suggest' target AO1 (accurate, precise legal knowledge); 'advise'/'consider' target AO1+AO2+AO3 together (apply the law to a scenario and reach a reasoned conclusion); 'examine'/'discuss'/'assess' target AO3 (evaluate non-substantive law, or substantive law within a non-substantive framework, e.g. discussing judicial precedent's advantages through a case scenario). Push the learner to build a bank of exact case names, years and the precise principle each establishes -- a vague 'there was a case about...' earns nothing under AQA's marking. Law changes: this course states the law of England and Wales as understood to be current in September 2026; statutory thresholds, ongoing reform and any pending legislation should be checked against a live source (legislation.gov.uk, aqa.org.uk) before a real exam sitting.

#### FORM.3 Essential requirements of a valid contract: consideration -- must have some value but need not be adequate, must move from the promisee, and cannot be past; promissory estoppel as a limited exception where a promise is relied on; privity of contract and the Contracts (Rights of Third Parties) Act 1999
**Consideration.** Something of value given by each party in exchange for the other's promise, required (alongside offer, acceptance and intention) for a **simple contract** to be enforceable (a **deed**, by contrast, needs no consideration). The core rules: consideration **need not be adequate** (the courts do not police whether the bargain is a fair, market-value exchange) but **must have some value** in the eyes of the law (*Chappell & Co Ltd v Nestlé Co Ltd* [1960] AC 87 -- chocolate bar wrappers, worthless in themselves, were still good consideration as part of the price for a record, since they had some value to Nestlé commercially, even though trivial; *Thomas v Thomas* (1842) 2 QB 851 -- a nominal £1 annual rent was sufficient consideration for a promise to allow a widow to live in a house, illustrating that adequacy is irrelevant provided some value exists). Consideration **must move from the promisee** (though not necessarily to the promisor). **Past consideration is no consideration**: an act done before a promise was made cannot generally be consideration for that later promise, since it was not given *in exchange for* it (*Re McArdle* [1951] Ch 669 -- improvements made to a house before a later promise by the family to reimburse the cost were past consideration, so the promise to pay was unenforceable), though an exception exists where the earlier act was done **at the promisor's request**, with **both parties understanding payment would follow**, so that a later promise merely fixes the amount already impliedly agreed (*Pao On v Lau Yiu Long* [1980] AC 614). **Existing contractual/public duties**: performing an existing duty already owed under the same contract to the same party is generally not good consideration for a fresh promise of extra payment (*Stilk v Myrick* (1809) 2 Camp 317 -- sailors who agreed to complete a voyage short-handed for extra pay, after two crew members deserted, gave no fresh consideration, since they were already contractually bound to work the ship), but a **practical benefit** to the other party from performance (avoiding a penalty, avoiding the cost/delay of finding a replacement) can now itself count as good consideration in the absence of duress (*Williams v Roffey Bros & Nicholls (Contractors) Ltd* [1991] 1 QB 1 -- a contractor's promise of extra payment to a subcontractor to ensure timely completion, avoiding a penalty clause and the cost of finding a replacement, was supported by the practical benefit the contractor gained, even though the subcontractor did no more than the original contract already required). **Promissory estoppel** is a limited, equitable exception allowing a promise to reduce or suspend an existing legal right to be enforced despite the absence of fresh consideration, where the promisee **relied** on a clear promise and it would be **inequitable** for the promisor to go back on it (*Central London Property Trust Ltd v High Trees House Ltd* [1947] KB 130 -- a landlord's promise to accept reduced rent during wartime conditions, relied on by the tenant, could not later be reneged on to claim the full rent for the period the reduced-rent promise covered; the doctrine operates only as a "shield, not a sword" -- it cannot itself found a fresh cause of action, only prevent enforcement of a right inconsistent with the promise relied on). **Privity of contract**: traditionally, only parties to a contract can sue or be sued on it, so a third party who benefits from a contract's terms cannot enforce them (*Tweddle v Atkinson* (1861) 1 B & S 393; *Dunlop Pneumatic Tyre Co Ltd v Selfridge & Co Ltd* [1915] AC 847 -- no consideration having moved from the third party, it could not enforce a resale-price term made for its benefit). The **Contracts (Rights of Third Parties) Act 1999** creates a significant statutory exception: a third party may enforce a term if the contract expressly provides they may, or if the term purports to confer a benefit on them (unless, on the contract's proper construction, the parties did not intend the term to be enforceable by the third party).

#### FORM.4 Essential requirements of a valid contract: intention to create legal relations -- the rebuttable presumptions in social/domestic and commercial agreements, and how each presumption can be displaced on the facts
**Intention to create legal relations.** Even with offer, acceptance and consideration, a contract is only formed if both parties intended their agreement to be **legally binding** (as opposed to a purely social or moral arrangement). The courts apply **rebuttable presumptions** depending on context. In **social and domestic agreements**, there is a presumption of **no** intention to create legal relations, on the basis such arrangements are typically made in reliance on personal trust rather than legal enforceability (*Balfour v Balfour* [1919] 2 KB 571 -- a husband's promise to pay his wife a monthly allowance while he worked abroad, made while the marriage was still functioning normally, was presumed to be a purely domestic arrangement with no intention to create legal relations, and so unenforceable). This presumption can be **rebutted** by evidence the parties genuinely did intend legal consequences -- e.g. where the relationship has broken down and the parties are negotiating at arm's length, or where money/property genuinely at stake makes a business-like intention more plausible (*Merritt v Merritt* [1970] 1 WLR 1211 -- a written agreement between a separated husband and wife about the matrimonial home, made once the relationship had broken down and the parties were dealing at arm's length rather than in a spirit of mutual trust, was held to be legally binding, the domestic presumption being displaced by the changed, more formal circumstances). In **commercial agreements**, the presumption runs the other way: an intention to create legal relations is presumed, reflecting that businesses generally do intend their bargains to be enforceable (*Edwards v Skyways Ltd* [1964] 1 WLR 349 -- an employer's promise of an "ex gratia" redundancy payment to airline pilots was still held to carry a presumed commercial intention to be bound, the word "ex gratia" alone not being enough to rebut the strong commercial presumption). This commercial presumption can itself be rebutted by clear words or context showing the parties did **not** intend legal enforceability -- e.g. an express "honour clause", or, in a suitable context, a promotional scheme whose commercial context and clear wording make plain no binding contract was intended for a minor collectable element of it (*Esso Petroleum Co Ltd v Commissioners of Customs and Excise* [1976] 1 WLR 1, a case ultimately decided on the facts by a divided House of Lords over whether free World Cup coins given with petrol were "sold", but often discussed alongside intention-to-create-legal-relations analysis for promotional schemes, illustrating how context-sensitive the commercial presumption can be at its margins).
