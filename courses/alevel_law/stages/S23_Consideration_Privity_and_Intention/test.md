## Test items
1. Explain how Williams v Roffey Bros & Nicholls (Contractors) Ltd [1991] 1 QB 1 modified the traditional rule in Stilk v Myrick (1809) 2 Camp 317 on existing contractual duties as consideration. [8 marks]
2. A father tells his adult daughter, mid-divorce and needing somewhere to live, that she can stay rent-free in his second property 'for as long as you need', and she gives up her rented flat and moves in, spending money making the property her home. Six months later he demands she leave immediately. Using IRAC, advise the daughter on whether the arrangement is legally binding. [10 marks]

## Answer key (for the tutor only)
1. [8] M2 traditional rule (Stilk v Myrick): performing an existing contractual duty already owed to the same party is not good consideration for a fresh promise of extra payment; M3 Williams v Roffey: a promise of extra payment for performing an existing duty can be supported by consideration where the promisor obtains a practical benefit from that performance (e.g. avoiding a penalty clause, or the cost/delay of finding a replacement); M3 the practical-benefit exception applies provided the promise was not obtained through economic duress or fraud, and does not overrule Stilk v Myrick but narrows its practical effect in many modern commercial situations.
2. [10] Mark using issue-rule-application-conclusion (IRAC): identify the issue(s), state the rule precisely with its authority (case or statute, named exactly), apply it to the facts given, and reach a supported conclusion. Credit: issue = whether the promise carries intention to create legal relations despite being between family members; rule = social/domestic agreements are presumed not to carry intention to create legal relations (Balfour v Balfour [1919] 2 KB 571), but this presumption can be rebutted where the circumstances show a genuine, more formal or arm's-length intention (Merritt v Merritt [1970] 1 WLR 1211); application = the arrangement arises from genuine need (the daughter's divorce and housing crisis) rather than ordinary day-to-day family generosity, and she relied on the promise to her detriment by giving up her rented flat and investing money in the property, factors suggesting the presumption of no legal intent may be rebutted on these more serious, relied-upon facts, closer in spirit to Merritt's arm's-length, consequential arrangement than Balfour's routine domestic allowance; conclusion = the daughter has a reasonable, though not certain, argument that the domestic presumption is rebutted given the seriousness of the arrangement and her detrimental reliance, though the tutor should flag this is a genuinely arguable point rather than a clear-cut answer, and that a promissory estoppel argument might also be worth exploring in the alternative.
