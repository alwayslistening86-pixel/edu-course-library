# S15_Duress - Lesson: Duress by threats; duress of circumstances

## Goal
The learner explains the defences of duress by threats and duress of circumstances, their common requirements, and the offences for which duress is unavailable.

## Syllabus items taught here
- DEF.4 - Defences: necessity -- duress by threats and duress of circumstances

## How to teach this
Ask: someone commits a crime only because they were credibly threatened with death if they refused -- should the law ever excuse that entirely? Teach each rule as: the precise legal test (statute section or case authority, stated exactly, since AQA rewards named, accurate authority), why it exists (the policy AQA's own specification names, e.g. public policy limits on the duty of care, freedom of contract vs consumer protection), and how it applies to facts -- AQA's application and scenario questions (worth over half the marks on every paper) need the rule applied to the specific facts given, not restated in the abstract. Use AQA's own command words precisely: 'explain'/'suggest' target AO1 (accurate, precise legal knowledge); 'advise'/'consider' target AO1+AO2+AO3 together (apply the law to a scenario and reach a reasoned conclusion); 'examine'/'discuss'/'assess' target AO3 (evaluate non-substantive law, or substantive law within a non-substantive framework, e.g. discussing judicial precedent's advantages through a case scenario). Push the learner to build a bank of exact case names, years and the precise principle each establishes -- a vague 'there was a case about...' earns nothing under AQA's marking. Law changes: this course states the law of England and Wales as understood to be current in September 2026; statutory thresholds, ongoing reform and any pending legislation should be checked against a live source (legislation.gov.uk, aqa.org.uk) before a real exam sitting.

#### DEF.4 Defences: necessity -- duress by threats and duress of circumstances
**Defences: duress by threats and duress of circumstances.** **Duress by threats** is a general defence (a full acquittal) where the defendant committed the offence because they were **threatened with death or serious injury** to themselves or someone for whom they reasonably felt responsible, and a **person of reasonable firmness**, sharing the defendant's relevant characteristics, would have responded in the same way. The leading modern authority, ***R v Hasan* [2005] UKHL 22**, restated and tightened the requirements: the threat must be of death or serious injury; it must be **operative on the defendant's mind at the time** of the offence; the defendant must reasonably believe there was **no safe avenue of escape** (e.g. going to the police); the threat must be **directly** connected to the crime committed (the defendant must be threatened with the relevant consequence if they do not commit *that* offence); and, crucially, the defence is **unavailable** where the defendant **voluntarily associated with people they knew or ought to have known might subject them to compulsion** (e.g. voluntarily joining a criminal gang), even if they did not foresee the specific threat that followed. The earlier two-stage test in *R v Graham* [1982] 1 WLR 294 (was the defendant impelled to act as they did because they reasonably believed they had good cause to fear death or serious injury; would a sober person of reasonable firmness, sharing the defendant's characteristics, have responded the same way) remains a useful summary of the objective/subjective structure, approved by the House of Lords in *R v Howe* [1987] AC 417. **Duress is not available for murder** (as principal or, per *Howe*, accessory) **or attempted murder**, reflecting a policy judgment that the sanctity of life means the law cannot excuse deliberately taking an innocent life even under extreme threat (a position that continues to attract academic criticism as potentially harsh, e.g. to a defendant genuinely faced with 'kill or be killed'). **Duress of circumstances** is essentially the same defence applied where the threat arises from **surrounding circumstances** rather than a direct human threat to commit a specific crime (*R v Conway* [1989] QB 290 -- driving dangerously to escape what the defendant genuinely, if perhaps mistakenly, believed was an imminent armed attack was capable of founding duress of circumstances); it requires the same elements (an imminent threat of death/serious injury, a reasonable belief, no safe avenue of escape, and the reasonable-firmness objective test), and is subject to the same exclusion for murder/attempted murder. Both forms require the threat to be sufficiently **imminent** that the defendant had no realistic opportunity to seek help instead (*R v Hudson and Taylor* [1971] 2 QB 202 took a relatively relaxed early approach to imminence, later read more strictly in light of *Hasan*'s emphasis on a genuine absence of any safe avenue of escape).
