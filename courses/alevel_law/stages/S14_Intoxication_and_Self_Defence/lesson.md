# S14_Intoxication_and_Self_Defence - Lesson: Intoxication; self-defence and prevention of crime

## Goal
The learner explains the rules on voluntary and involuntary intoxication, and the defence of self-defence/prevention of crime under s76 Criminal Justice and Immigration Act 2008.

## Syllabus items taught here
- DEF.2 - Defences: capacity -- voluntary and involuntary intoxication and its effect on liability for specific and basic intent offences
- DEF.3 - Defences: necessity -- self-defence and the prevention of crime, s76 Criminal Justice and Immigration Act 2008 (the need to act, the degree of force reasonable in the circumstances as the defendant believed them to be)

## How to teach this
Ask: someone commits an offence while extremely drunk and says they didn't know what they were doing -- does that ever help them? Teach each rule as: the precise legal test (statute section or case authority, stated exactly, since AQA rewards named, accurate authority), why it exists (the policy AQA's own specification names, e.g. public policy limits on the duty of care, freedom of contract vs consumer protection), and how it applies to facts -- AQA's application and scenario questions (worth over half the marks on every paper) need the rule applied to the specific facts given, not restated in the abstract. Use AQA's own command words precisely: 'explain'/'suggest' target AO1 (accurate, precise legal knowledge); 'advise'/'consider' target AO1+AO2+AO3 together (apply the law to a scenario and reach a reasoned conclusion); 'examine'/'discuss'/'assess' target AO3 (evaluate non-substantive law, or substantive law within a non-substantive framework, e.g. discussing judicial precedent's advantages through a case scenario). Push the learner to build a bank of exact case names, years and the precise principle each establishes -- a vague 'there was a case about...' earns nothing under AQA's marking. Law changes: this course states the law of England and Wales as understood to be current in September 2026; statutory thresholds, ongoing reform and any pending legislation should be checked against a live source (legislation.gov.uk, aqa.org.uk) before a real exam sitting.

#### DEF.2 Defences: capacity -- voluntary and involuntary intoxication and its effect on liability for specific and basic intent offences
**Defences: intoxication.** Not a true defence so much as a rule about whether the defendant had the required mens rea. **Voluntary intoxication** (the defendant chose to take drink/drugs, knowing their potential effect) is **no defence to basic intent offences** (offences whose mens rea is satisfied by recklessness, e.g. battery, s47, s20, manslaughter) -- the reasoning, from ***DPP v Majewski* [1977] AC 443**, is that the act of voluntarily becoming intoxicated is itself reckless (a reckless course of conduct), so that recklessness substitutes for the mens rea the specific offence would otherwise require. For **specific intent offences** (those requiring proof of intention alone, e.g. murder, s18, theft), voluntary intoxication **can** be raised as evidence that the defendant lacked the necessary intention (if too intoxicated to form the specific intent, they cannot be guilty of the specific-intent offence) -- but this typically only results in conviction of a lesser, basic-intent alternative offence instead (e.g. a defendant too drunk to form the intent to kill for murder may still be convicted of manslaughter, since intoxication cannot excuse the underlying reckless conduct). An exception: 'Dutch courage' -- a defendant who deliberately gets drunk *in order to* carry out a crime they had already decided on cannot use the resulting intoxication to deny intention (*Attorney-General for Northern Ireland v Gallagher* [1963] AC 349). **Involuntary intoxication** (the defendant did not know they were taking an intoxicating substance, e.g. a drink was secretly spiked) is only a defence if it in fact prevented the defendant from forming the mens rea required -- if the defendant still formed the necessary intent despite the involuntary intoxication, the defence fails (*R v Kingston* [1995] 2 AC 355 -- a defendant whose drink was secretly spiked, lowering his inhibitions so he committed an indecent assault, was still liable, since he was shown to have formed the necessary intent at the time despite the intoxication; involuntary intoxication is not itself an excuse, only relevant if it actually negates mens rea).

#### DEF.3 Defences: necessity -- self-defence and the prevention of crime, s76 Criminal Justice and Immigration Act 2008 (the need to act, the degree of force reasonable in the circumstances as the defendant believed them to be)
**Defences: self-defence and prevention of crime.** A **general defence** to any crime involving the use of force (leading to a **full acquittal** if successful), available where the defendant used force to defend themselves, another, or property, or to prevent a crime or assist a lawful arrest, now largely codified by **s76 Criminal Justice and Immigration Act 2008** (which restates and clarifies, rather than replaces, the common law/statutory defence). Two questions: (1) was the **use of force necessary** in the circumstances **as the defendant genuinely believed them to be** -- even if that belief was, viewed objectively, mistaken or unreasonable, provided it was genuinely (honestly) held (s76(3), reflecting the earlier common law position in *R v Williams (Gladstone)* (1984) 78 Cr App R 276 -- a mistaken but genuine belief that the defendant was intervening to stop what looked like an unlawful assault was judged on the facts as he believed them to be, even though his belief turned out to be wrong); note that where the mistaken belief is due to **voluntary intoxication**, s76(5) provides the defendant cannot rely on a mistaken belief induced by their own voluntary intoxication; and (2) was the **degree of force used reasonable** in those circumstances -- judged objectively but making allowance for the difficulty of judging exactly the right level of force in the heat of the moment (s76(7), reflecting that a defendant cannot be expected to 'weigh to a nicety the exact measure of any necessary action'). Disproportionate, excessive force defeats the defence even where some force was justified (*R v Clegg* [1995] 1 AC 482 -- a soldier who fired a final, fatal shot at a car that had already passed and no longer posed a threat used disproportionate force, since by that point the danger, if any, had passed, defeating the defence even though earlier shots may have been justified). Where the defendant is a 'householder' defending against a trespasser in their own home, s76(5A)-(8A) applies a modified test (force that was 'grossly disproportionate' defeats the defence, a slightly more generous standard than for other self-defence scenarios).
