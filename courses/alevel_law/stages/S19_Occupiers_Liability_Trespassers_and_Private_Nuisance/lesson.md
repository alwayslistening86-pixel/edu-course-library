# S19_Occupiers_Liability_Trespassers_and_Private_Nuisance - Lesson: Occupiers' liability to trespassers; private nuisance

## Goal
The learner explains the more limited duty owed to trespassers under the Occupiers' Liability Act 1984, and the tort of private nuisance including the factors courts weigh and its defences.

## Syllabus items taught here
- OL.2 - Occupiers' liability to trespassers under the Occupiers' Liability Act 1984: the conditions for a duty to arise (awareness of the danger, knowledge/reasonable grounds to believe someone is in the vicinity, the risk is one the occupier can reasonably be expected to protect against) and its more limited content
- NUI.1 - Private nuisance: an unlawful, indirect interference with a person's use or enjoyment of land, the factors courts weigh (locality, duration, sensitivity, malice), and defences (prescription, statutory authority)

## How to teach this
Ask: a trespasser is injured on someone else's land -- does the occupier owe them nothing at all, or something less than a lawful visitor gets? Teach each rule as: the precise legal test (statute section or case authority, stated exactly, since AQA rewards named, accurate authority), why it exists (the policy AQA's own specification names, e.g. public policy limits on the duty of care, freedom of contract vs consumer protection), and how it applies to facts -- AQA's application and scenario questions (worth over half the marks on every paper) need the rule applied to the specific facts given, not restated in the abstract. Use AQA's own command words precisely: 'explain'/'suggest' target AO1 (accurate, precise legal knowledge); 'advise'/'consider' target AO1+AO2+AO3 together (apply the law to a scenario and reach a reasoned conclusion); 'examine'/'discuss'/'assess' target AO3 (evaluate non-substantive law, or substantive law within a non-substantive framework, e.g. discussing judicial precedent's advantages through a case scenario). Push the learner to build a bank of exact case names, years and the precise principle each establishes -- a vague 'there was a case about...' earns nothing under AQA's marking. Law changes: this course states the law of England and Wales as understood to be current in September 2026; statutory thresholds, ongoing reform and any pending legislation should be checked against a live source (legislation.gov.uk, aqa.org.uk) before a real exam sitting.

#### OL.2 Occupiers' liability to trespassers under the Occupiers' Liability Act 1984: the conditions for a duty to arise (awareness of the danger, knowledge/reasonable grounds to believe someone is in the vicinity, the risk is one the occupier can reasonably be expected to protect against) and its more limited content
**Occupiers' liability to trespassers, Occupiers' Liability Act 1984.** A **trespasser** is someone on premises without permission (express, implied, or because they have exceeded the scope of permission given). At common law, occupiers historically owed trespassers only a limited duty not to act with reckless disregard, developed into a more structured (though still limited) common law duty in *British Railways Board v Herrington* [1972] AC 877 (a child trespasser, injured on an electrified railway line the defendant knew children regularly trespassed onto through a poorly maintained fence, was owed a duty despite trespassing, given the defendant's actual knowledge of the danger and of trespassers' presence), which the 1984 Act now puts on a statutory footing. A duty arises under **s1(3)** only where: (a) the occupier is **aware of the danger**, or has reasonable grounds to believe it exists; (b) the occupier **knows, or has reasonable grounds to believe**, that a person is or may come into the vicinity of the danger (whether or not they have any right to be there); and (c) the risk is one against which, **in all the circumstances**, the occupier **may reasonably be expected to offer some protection**. If a duty does arise, it is again to take such care as is reasonable **in the circumstances** to see the trespasser does not suffer injury by reason of the danger (a more limited duty than the 1957 Act's -- it covers only personal injury, not damage to property, and can more easily be discharged by a warning given the lower baseline expectation owed to an uninvited entrant). Courts are cautious about extending the duty to **obvious risks a person has freely chosen to run** (*Tomlinson v Congleton Borough Council* [2003] UKHL 47 -- a young man who dived into a shallow lake on the defendant's land, despite signs warning against swimming, and was catastrophically injured, was owed no duty; the risk of diving into shallow water was obvious, and it was not reasonable to expect the occupier to protect adult trespassers against risks they had freely and knowingly chosen to run by disregarding clear warnings -- a policy the House of Lords tied to not placing an excessive burden on landowners to guard against every self-inflicted risk).

#### NUI.1 Private nuisance: an unlawful, indirect interference with a person's use or enjoyment of land, the factors courts weigh (locality, duration, sensitivity, malice), and defences (prescription, statutory authority)
**Private nuisance.** An unlawful, **indirect** interference with a claimant's use or enjoyment of their land (contrast with trespass to land, a direct interference), which can involve physical damage to the land or, more commonly, interference with **enjoyment** of the land (e.g. noise, smell, vibration). Only a person with a sufficient **proprietary interest** in the land affected (an owner or tenant, not a mere licensee/guest) can sue (*Hunter v Canary Wharf Ltd* [1997] AC 655 -- residents without a proprietary interest in the affected properties (e.g. family members with no legal/equitable interest) could not sue in private nuisance for interference with television reception caused by a large building, confirming that private nuisance protects rights *in land*, not simply personal comfort as such). Courts weigh several factors in deciding whether an interference is unreasonable enough to be actionable: **locality/character of the area** (*Sturges v Bridgman* (1879) 11 Ch D 852 -- "what would be a nuisance in Belgrave Square would not necessarily be so in Bermondsey" -- the character of the neighbourhood affects what level of interference is reasonable to expect, though this factor does not apply where actual physical damage to property is caused, which is actionable regardless of locality); **duration/frequency** of the interference (a one-off event is less likely to be actionable than a persistent interference, though a sufficiently serious one-off event, e.g. a large escape of dangerous material, can still qualify, *Halsey v Esso Petroleum Co Ltd* [1961] 1 WLR 683, which also confirmed noise and acid smuts/deposits from an oil depot were actionable nuisances given their frequency and effect on the claimant's property); **sensitivity of the claimant** (an abnormally sensitive claimant/use cannot generally complain of an interference that would not trouble an ordinary occupier); and **malice**, which can turn an otherwise borderline interference into an actionable nuisance (*Christie v Davey* [1893] 1 Ch 316 -- a neighbour who deliberately banged on the party wall, blew whistles and shrieked, in retaliation for music lessons next door, was restrained by injunction, the deliberate, malicious motive tipping an otherwise arguably tolerable interference into an actionable nuisance). **Defences**: **prescription** (20 years' continuous interference, without the claimant objecting, can give a defence, though only once the claimant themselves became aware/affected, not simply once the activity started) and **statutory authority** (where the interfering activity is expressly or impliedly authorised by statute, e.g. for certain infrastructure works, though this is construed narrowly and does not authorise negligent conduct beyond what the statute permits).
