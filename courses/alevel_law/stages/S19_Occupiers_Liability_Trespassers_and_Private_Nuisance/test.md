## Test items
1. Explain, with authority, why the locality of an area is not always a relevant factor in deciding whether a private nuisance has occurred. [5 marks]
2. Teenagers regularly trespass through a gap in the fence around a disused, water-filled quarry that the landowner knows about but has not repaired, despite having received complaints. A trespassing teenager dives from a rock ledge into shallow water at the quarry's edge, ignoring a faded, barely legible old warning sign, and is seriously injured. Using IRAC, advise on the landowner's potential liability under the Occupiers' Liability Act 1984. [10 marks]

## Answer key (for the tutor only)
1. [5] B2 locality/character of the area is generally relevant to what level of interference with enjoyment is reasonable to expect in that neighbourhood (Sturges v Bridgman (1879) 11 Ch D 852: 'what would be a nuisance in Belgrave Square would not necessarily be so in Bermondsey'); B3 but locality is not a relevant factor where the interference causes actual physical damage to the claimant's property, which is actionable regardless of the character of the area -- physical damage cases are treated differently from pure amenity/enjoyment interference.
2. [10] Mark using issue-rule-application-conclusion (IRAC): identify the issue(s), state the rule precisely with its authority (case or statute, named exactly), apply it to the facts given, and reach a supported conclusion. Credit: issue = whether a duty arises under s1(3) and, if so, whether it was breached, and whether the obvious-risk principle in Tomlinson defeats the claim; rule = a duty requires awareness of the danger, knowledge/reasonable grounds that trespassers may be in the vicinity, and that the risk is one the occupier could reasonably be expected to guard against (s1(3), OLA 1984); but no duty extends to obvious risks freely and knowingly run (Tomlinson v Congleton Borough Council [2003] UKHL 47); application = known trespass through a known gap, plus known complaints, likely satisfies awareness of both the danger and the presence of trespassers; but diving from a rock ledge into unknown-depth water is arguably an obvious risk any adult (or older teenager) should appreciate, similar to Tomlinson, and the faded/barely legible sign is unlikely to change the obvious-risk analysis since the danger of diving into unknown water is inherently apparent, not dependent on a warning being read; conclusion = while a duty may technically arise under s1(3) given the landowner's knowledge, the claim is likely to fail (or succeed only with a significant reduction for contributory negligence) applying Tomlinson's reasoning that the occupier need not guard against an obvious risk the claimant freely chose to run, though the poor state of the sign and the fence weaken the landowner's position somewhat compared with Tomlinson's clear warning signs.
