# S22_Offer_and_Acceptance - Lesson: Offer; acceptance

## Goal
The learner distinguishes an offer from an invitation to treat, explains how an offer can be terminated, and states the requirements and special rules for a valid acceptance.

## Syllabus items taught here
- FORM.1 - Essential requirements of a valid contract: offer -- distinguished from an invitation to treat (goods on display, advertisements, auctions), how an offer may be terminated (revocation, rejection, lapse of time, counter-offer)
- FORM.2 - Essential requirements of a valid contract: acceptance -- a mirror-image, unconditional agreement to the offer's terms, communication of acceptance, the postal rule, and acceptance by electronic/instantaneous communication

## How to teach this
Ask: a shop displays a knife in its window with a price tag -- has the shop already made a legal offer to sell it to whoever walks in first? Teach each rule as: the precise legal test (statute section or case authority, stated exactly, since AQA rewards named, accurate authority), why it exists (the policy AQA's own specification names, e.g. public policy limits on the duty of care, freedom of contract vs consumer protection), and how it applies to facts -- AQA's application and scenario questions (worth over half the marks on every paper) need the rule applied to the specific facts given, not restated in the abstract. Use AQA's own command words precisely: 'explain'/'suggest' target AO1 (accurate, precise legal knowledge); 'advise'/'consider' target AO1+AO2+AO3 together (apply the law to a scenario and reach a reasoned conclusion); 'examine'/'discuss'/'assess' target AO3 (evaluate non-substantive law, or substantive law within a non-substantive framework, e.g. discussing judicial precedent's advantages through a case scenario). Push the learner to build a bank of exact case names, years and the precise principle each establishes -- a vague 'there was a case about...' earns nothing under AQA's marking. Law changes: this course states the law of England and Wales as understood to be current in September 2026; statutory thresholds, ongoing reform and any pending legislation should be checked against a live source (legislation.gov.uk, aqa.org.uk) before a real exam sitting.

#### FORM.1 Essential requirements of a valid contract: offer -- distinguished from an invitation to treat (goods on display, advertisements, auctions), how an offer may be terminated (revocation, rejection, lapse of time, counter-offer)
**Offer.** A clear, definite statement of terms by the **offeror**, made with the intention to be bound as soon as the **offeree** accepts it -- distinguished from an **invitation to treat**, a mere invitation for others to make offers, which does not itself bind the person making it. Common invitations to treat: **goods on display** in a shop window or on a shelf (*Fisher v Bell* [1961] 1 QB 394 -- a flick knife displayed in a shop window with a price tag was, on ordinary contract principles as well as on the statute's literal wording, an invitation to treat, not an offer for sale; *Pharmaceutical Society of Great Britain v Boots Cash Chemists (Southern) Ltd* [1953] 1 QB 401 -- goods on a self-service shelf are an invitation to treat, with the customer making the offer at the till, which the shop is free to accept or reject, a structure important for regulatory purposes -- e.g. ensuring a pharmacist supervises the "sale" at the point of acceptance); **advertisements** (generally invitations to treat, since a seller cannot realistically intend to be bound to supply unlimited buyers, *Partridge v Crittenden* [1968] 1 WLR 1204 -- a classified advert offering birds "for sale" was an invitation to treat, not an offer, so no offence of unlawfully "offering" a protected species for sale was committed); and a mere **statement of price** in response to an enquiry (*Harvey v Facey* [1893] AC 552 -- stating the lowest price the defendant "would accept" for land, in response to a telegram asking for the price, was not itself an offer capable of acceptance, merely information). An exception is a genuine **unilateral offer** made to the world at large, capable of acceptance by anyone who performs the stipulated act (*Carlill v Carbolic Smoke Ball Co* [1893] 1 QB 256 -- an advertisement promising £100 to anyone who used the product as directed and still caught influenza, backed by a statement that £1,000 had been deposited at a bank "shewing our sincerity", was held to be a genuine offer to the world, capable of acceptance by performance, not mere advertising puff). An offer may be **terminated** before acceptance by: **revocation** (withdrawal by the offeror, effective only once actually communicated to the offeree, even indirectly through a reliable third party, *Dickinson v Dodds* (1876) 2 Ch D 463; must occur before acceptance, *Routledge v Grant* (1828) 4 Bing 653; and cannot simply be by post alone without it being received, *Byrne & Co v Van Tienhoven & Co* (1880) 5 CPD 344); **rejection**; **lapse of time** (after a stated or, if none, a reasonable period); or a **counter-offer**, which destroys the original offer rather than merely proposing a variation (*Hyde v Wrench* (1840) 3 Beav 334 -- responding to an offer to sell a farm for £1,000 with a counter-offer of £950 destroyed the original £1,000 offer, so it could not later be accepted once the counter-offer was rejected).

#### FORM.2 Essential requirements of a valid contract: acceptance -- a mirror-image, unconditional agreement to the offer's terms, communication of acceptance, the postal rule, and acceptance by electronic/instantaneous communication
**Acceptance.** A final, unconditional agreement to **all** the terms of the offer, exactly mirroring it (the "**mirror image**" rule) -- a response purporting to accept but introducing new terms is a counter-offer, not an acceptance (*Hyde v Wrench*, above). Acceptance must generally be **communicated** to the offeror to be effective (silence alone cannot amount to acceptance, even if the offeree privately intends to accept: *Felthouse v Bindley* (1862) 11 CB (NS) 869 -- an uncle's letter to his nephew stating he would consider the nephew's horse his unless he heard otherwise did not create a contract merely because the nephew, in his own mind, intended to accept and told an auctioneer not to sell it; silence/inaction cannot be treated as acceptance without the offeree's own communication). For **instantaneous communication** (e.g. telephone, fax, and by extension most electronic communication such as email in real time), acceptance takes effect when and where it is actually **received** by the offeror, not merely sent (*Entores Ltd v Miles Far East Corp* [1955] 2 QB 327 -- a contract by telex was formed where and when the acceptance message was received, since instantaneous communication allows the offeror to know at once whether the message got through, unlike the post). The **postal rule** is a historic exception: where post is a reasonable, contemplated method of acceptance, the acceptance is complete and binding the moment the letter is properly **posted**, even if it is delayed, lost, or never arrives at all (*Adams v Lindsell* (1818) 1 B & Ald 681); the rule can be excluded by the offeror expressly requiring actual receipt of acceptance, and generally does not extend to genuinely instantaneous electronic communication, whose exact application to modern methods (e.g. email, which is not always truly "instantaneous" if unread) remains a live, examinable point of debate rather than settled by a single definitive case.
