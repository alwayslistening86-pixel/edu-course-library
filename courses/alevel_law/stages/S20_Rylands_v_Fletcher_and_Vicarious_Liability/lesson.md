# S20_Rylands_v_Fletcher_and_Vicarious_Liability - Lesson: The rule in Rylands v Fletcher; vicarious liability

## Goal
The learner explains the strict liability rule in Rylands v Fletcher and its modern limits, and the nature, purpose and modern tests for vicarious liability.

## Syllabus items taught here
- NUI.2 - The rule in Rylands v Fletcher (1868): strict liability for the escape of a dangerous thing accumulated on land in the course of a non-natural use, and its relationship with private nuisance
- VL.1 - Vicarious liability: the nature and public-policy purpose of the doctrine; the tests for employment status (the relationship 'akin to employment'); the 'close connection' test for whether a tort is committed in the course of employment

## How to teach this
Ask: a factory does everything reasonably careful, yet a dangerous chemical it stored still escapes and damages a neighbour -- can the neighbour still win without proving any fault at all? Teach each rule as: the precise legal test (statute section or case authority, stated exactly, since AQA rewards named, accurate authority), why it exists (the policy AQA's own specification names, e.g. public policy limits on the duty of care, freedom of contract vs consumer protection), and how it applies to facts -- AQA's application and scenario questions (worth over half the marks on every paper) need the rule applied to the specific facts given, not restated in the abstract. Use AQA's own command words precisely: 'explain'/'suggest' target AO1 (accurate, precise legal knowledge); 'advise'/'consider' target AO1+AO2+AO3 together (apply the law to a scenario and reach a reasoned conclusion); 'examine'/'discuss'/'assess' target AO3 (evaluate non-substantive law, or substantive law within a non-substantive framework, e.g. discussing judicial precedent's advantages through a case scenario). Push the learner to build a bank of exact case names, years and the precise principle each establishes -- a vague 'there was a case about...' earns nothing under AQA's marking. Law changes: this course states the law of England and Wales as understood to be current in September 2026; statutory thresholds, ongoing reform and any pending legislation should be checked against a live source (legislation.gov.uk, aqa.org.uk) before a real exam sitting.

#### NUI.2 The rule in Rylands v Fletcher (1868): strict liability for the escape of a dangerous thing accumulated on land in the course of a non-natural use, and its relationship with private nuisance
**The rule in Rylands v Fletcher (1868) LR 3 HL 330.** A distinct, **strict liability** tort (no need to prove negligence/fault) closely related to private nuisance, arising where the defendant: (1) **brings onto their land**, and **accumulates**, something likely to do mischief if it escapes; (2) in the course of a **non-natural use** of the land; (3) it **escapes**; and (4) causes **foreseeable** damage. In *Rylands* itself, water stored in a reservoir built on the defendant's land escaped through disused, unknown mineshafts and flooded the claimant's neighbouring mine -- the defendant was liable despite having taken all reasonable care in constructing the reservoir, since liability did not depend on fault. The rule has since been narrowed: an **escape** from the defendant's land (or land they control) onto land outside their control is required -- injury sustained *on* the defendant's own land does not trigger the rule (*Read v J Lyons & Co Ltd* [1947] AC 156 -- an injury to a worker caused by an explosion while she was on the defendant's own munitions factory premises could not found a Rylands claim, since nothing had "escaped" from the defendant's land to somewhere outside it). **Foreseeability of the type of damage** (though not of the escape itself) is now required, aligning the rule more closely with ordinary remoteness principles (*Cambridge Water Co Ltd v Eastern Counties Leather plc* [1994] 2 AC 264 -- chemical spillage that seeped into the ground and, over many years, contaminated a distant water borehole was not something the defendant could reasonably have foreseen at the time, since the way the damage occurred (slow percolation causing eventual contamination) was not a foreseeable type of harm from spillage occurring decades earlier under the practices then in use, so the claim failed). "**Non-natural use**" requires something out of the ordinary, not merely a use of land that brings some increased risk -- the House of Lords in ***Transco plc v Stockport Metropolitan Borough Council* [2003] UKHL 61** confirmed this must be an **extraordinary and unusual** use, judged in its context (piping water to a block of flats through an ordinary water main was an entirely ordinary use of land, even though a burst pipe caused significant damage, so Rylands did not apply), significantly narrowing the practical scope of the tort today.

#### VL.1 Vicarious liability: the nature and public-policy purpose of the doctrine; the tests for employment status (the relationship 'akin to employment'); the 'close connection' test for whether a tort is committed in the course of employment
**Vicarious liability.** A doctrine of **strict, secondary liability**: an employer can be made liable for a tort committed by an **employee**, even though the employer is not itself at fault, provided the tort was committed **in the course of employment**. Its **purpose** is largely policy-driven: an employer is usually better placed to bear the loss (through insurance/spreading the cost across their business), has more control over how the work is carried out, profits from the employee's work, and is often easier for a claimant to identify and pursue than an individual employee who may lack the means to pay damages. **Employment status**: the traditional control/integration tests have given way to a broader question of whether the relationship is "**akin to employment**" -- close enough in substance, even if not a formal contract of employment, to justify imposing vicarious liability (*Various Claimants v Catholic Child Welfare Society* [2012] UKSC 56 -- a Catholic institute whose brothers, though not formal employees, were integrated into and effectively directed by the institute's operations of a school, was vicariously liable for their abuse, since the relationship was akin to employment; *Cox v Ministry of Justice* [2016] UKSC 10 -- a prisoner working in a prison kitchen under close direction, though not an employee, was similarly close enough to employment to found vicarious liability for his negligence). Conversely, a genuinely **independent contractor**, engaged to exercise their own independent judgment/skill without that degree of integration and control, generally does **not** attract vicarious liability for the engager (*Barclays Bank plc v Various Claimants* [2020] UKSC 13 -- a doctor engaged by the bank on a self-employed basis to conduct medical examinations, using his own equipment/judgment and not otherwise integrated into or controlled by the bank's business, was an independent contractor, so the bank was not vicariously liable for his abuse of claimants during examinations). **"In the course of employment"** now turns on the "**close connection**" test: was the tort so closely connected with what the employee was authorised to do that it can fairly and properly be regarded as done in the course of their employment, even if it was an unauthorised, wrongful way of doing an authorised job, or an abuse of the position the employer had put the employee in (*Lister v Hesley Hall Ltd* [2001] UKHL 22 -- a warden's sexual abuse of boys in his care at a residential school was so closely connected with the very employment (caring for and having authority over the boys) that had been entrusted to him that it was fair to hold the employer vicariously liable, even though the abuse was obviously unauthorised; *Mohamud v WM Morrison Supermarkets plc* [2016] UKSC 11 -- a petrol station employee's unprovoked, violent assault on a customer, following an exchange that began within his job of serving customers at the till, was close enough to what he was employed to do (interacting with customers) to be in the course of employment, even though the violence itself was obviously never authorised).
