## Test items
1. Explain the 'close connection' test for whether a tort was committed in the course of employment, with authority. [6 marks]
2. A specialist chemical storage firm keeps a large tank of a volatile industrial solvent on its site, a use common to that specific industrial estate where several similar firms operate. Despite reasonable precautions, a defect causes the tank to leak, and solvent seeps onto a neighbouring farm, killing crops in a way that was a well-known and clearly foreseeable risk of that type of solvent escaping. Using IRAC, advise the farm on a claim under the rule in Rylands v Fletcher. [10 marks]

## Answer key (for the tutor only)
1. [6] B2 the test asks whether the tort was so closely connected with what the employee was authorised to do that it can fairly and properly be regarded as done in the course of employment, even if the specific act was unauthorised (Lister v Hesley Hall Ltd [2001] UKHL 22); B2 an unauthorised, wrongful way of doing an authorised job, or an abuse of a position of trust the employer created, can still be in the course of employment (Lister); B2 illustrated in Mohamud v WM Morrison Supermarkets plc [2016] UKSC 11, where a violent assault following an exchange begun within the employee's customer-facing duties was held sufficiently connected.
2. [10] Mark using issue-rule-application-conclusion (IRAC): identify the issue(s), state the rule precisely with its authority (case or statute, named exactly), apply it to the facts given, and reach a supported conclusion. Credit: issue = whether the elements of Rylands v Fletcher (1868) LR 3 HL 330 are satisfied, in particular non-natural use and foreseeability of the type of damage; rule = the four elements (accumulation of something likely to do mischief, non-natural use, escape, foreseeable damage), non-natural use requiring an extraordinary and unusual use judged in context (Transco plc v Stockport MBC [2003] UKHL 61), and foreseeability of the type of damage being required even though not of the escape itself (Cambridge Water Co Ltd v Eastern Counties Leather plc [1994] 2 AC 264); application = storing a large volume of volatile industrial solvent is plausibly a non-natural use even on an industrial estate, since Transco requires the use itself to be extraordinary/unusual, which storing hazardous chemicals in bulk generally is, regardless of how common it is among neighbouring firms; the escape onto the farm is established, and the crop damage is expressly stated to be a well-known, foreseeable type of harm from that solvent, satisfying Cambridge Water's foreseeability requirement; conclusion = the farm has a strong claim under Rylands v Fletcher, since all four elements are likely satisfied, without needing to prove any fault/negligence in how the tank was maintained.
