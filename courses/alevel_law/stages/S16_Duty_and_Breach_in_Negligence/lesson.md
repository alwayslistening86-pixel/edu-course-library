# S16_Duty_and_Breach_in_Negligence - Lesson: Duty of care; breach of duty

## Goal
The learner states and applies the Caparo test for duty of care and the objective standard of care for breach, including the factors courts weigh in setting it.

## Syllabus items taught here
- NEG.1 - Liability in negligence: duty of care -- the neighbour principle and the modern Caparo three-stage test (foreseeability, proximity, fair/just/reasonable to impose a duty)
- NEG.2 - Liability in negligence: breach of duty -- the objective standard of the reasonable person, and the factors courts weigh (risk, cost/practicability of precautions, special characteristics, benefit to society)

## How to teach this
Ask: a manufacturer never meets the ultimate consumer of its product directly, yet can still owe them a legal duty -- how did the law get there? Teach each rule as: the precise legal test (statute section or case authority, stated exactly, since AQA rewards named, accurate authority), why it exists (the policy AQA's own specification names, e.g. public policy limits on the duty of care, freedom of contract vs consumer protection), and how it applies to facts -- AQA's application and scenario questions (worth over half the marks on every paper) need the rule applied to the specific facts given, not restated in the abstract. Use AQA's own command words precisely: 'explain'/'suggest' target AO1 (accurate, precise legal knowledge); 'advise'/'consider' target AO1+AO2+AO3 together (apply the law to a scenario and reach a reasoned conclusion); 'examine'/'discuss'/'assess' target AO3 (evaluate non-substantive law, or substantive law within a non-substantive framework, e.g. discussing judicial precedent's advantages through a case scenario). Push the learner to build a bank of exact case names, years and the precise principle each establishes -- a vague 'there was a case about...' earns nothing under AQA's marking. Law changes: this course states the law of England and Wales as understood to be current in September 2026; statutory thresholds, ongoing reform and any pending legislation should be checked against a live source (legislation.gov.uk, aqa.org.uk) before a real exam sitting.

#### NEG.1 Liability in negligence: duty of care -- the neighbour principle and the modern Caparo three-stage test (foreseeability, proximity, fair/just/reasonable to impose a duty)
**Liability in negligence: duty of care.** Negligence protects against carelessly caused loss, requiring proof of duty, breach, and damage caused by the breach (not too remote). The modern starting point is the **neighbour principle** from ***Donoghue v Stevenson* [1932] AC 562** (a decomposed snail found in a bottle of ginger beer, bought by a friend for the claimant, who became ill -- the House of Lords held a manufacturer owes a duty of care to the ultimate consumer of its product, since you must take reasonable care to avoid acts/omissions you can reasonably foresee would injure your 'neighbour' -- persons so closely and directly affected by your act that you ought reasonably to have them in contemplation). For **established categories** of relationship (e.g. driver/road user, doctor/patient, employer/employee, manufacturer/consumer), a duty is simply recognised by precedent. For **novel situations**, courts apply the three-stage test from ***Caparo Industries plc v Dickman* [1990] 2 AC 605**: (1) was the harm **reasonably foreseeable**; (2) was there sufficient **proximity** (closeness) of relationship between claimant and defendant; and (3) is it **fair, just and reasonable** to impose a duty in the circumstances (a policy-based control mechanism, allowing courts to refuse a duty even where foreseeability and proximity exist, e.g. to avoid indeterminate liability, or to protect certain defendants, such as some public bodies exercising statutory functions, from an unduly wide duty). Caparo itself concerned auditors' statutory accounts: the House of Lords held no duty was owed to a takeover bidder who relied on the audited accounts to decide whether to invest, since the accounts were prepared for the company/shareholders' statutory purposes, not to guide outside investment decisions -- proximity for that specific purpose was absent.

#### NEG.2 Liability in negligence: breach of duty -- the objective standard of the reasonable person, and the factors courts weigh (risk, cost/practicability of precautions, special characteristics, benefit to society)
**Liability in negligence: breach of duty.** Once a duty is established, breach asks whether the defendant fell below the **standard of the reasonably competent/reasonable person** in their position -- an **objective** standard, not judged by the particular defendant's own best efforts (*Nettleship v Weston* [1971] 2 QB 691 -- a learner driver was held to the same objective standard as a reasonably competent qualified driver, since the public relying on road users needs a consistent standard regardless of individual experience; a professional is judged by the standard of a reasonably competent member of that profession, *Bolam v Friern Hospital Management Committee* [1957] 1 WLR 582). Courts weigh several factors to decide what a reasonable person would have done: **the degree of risk** -- a small, unlikely risk may not require special precautions (*Bolton v Stone* [1951] AC 850 -- a cricket ball hit out of a ground, injuring a passer-by on a road, on only a handful of occasions in decades, was such a small, unlikely risk that a reasonable cricket club need not have done more than it already had); **the cost and practicability of precautions** -- if eliminating a risk would be cheap and easy, a reasonable defendant should have done so, but not if the cost is disproportionate to the risk (*Latimer v AEC Ltd* [1953] AC 643 -- a flooded, slippery factory floor, treated with available sawdust, did not require the (disproportionately costly) closure of the whole factory, since reasonable precautions, not perfect safety, are what the law requires); **the defendant's own special characteristics/skill** -- a defendant claiming a particular skill is held to that higher standard, e.g. Nettleship above, or *Paris v Stepney Borough Council* [1951] AC 367 (an employer, aware an employee had only one eye, owed a higher duty to provide eye protection for that employee than an ordinary two-eyed worker, since the consequence of an eye injury for him was more serious); and **the social utility/benefit of the defendant's conduct**, which can justify a somewhat higher acceptable risk where the activity serves an important public purpose (e.g. emergency service activity).
