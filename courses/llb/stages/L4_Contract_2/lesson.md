# L4_Contract_2 - Lesson: Contract Law II — Terms, vitiating factors, discharge and remedies

## Goal
The learner classifies contract terms, identifies vitiating factors, and applies the rules on discharge and remedies to a problem question.

## Syllabus items taught here
- L4-Contract-6 - Express and implied terms: Distinguish express terms from terms implied by statute, custom and fact, and classify terms as conditions, warranties or innominate
- L4-Contract-7 - Exclusion and limitation clauses: Apply incorporation, construction and UCTA 1977/CRA 2015 controls to an exclusion clause
- L4-Contract-8 - Misrepresentation: Classify a false statement as fraudulent, negligent or innocent misrepresentation and identify the available remedies
- L4-Contract-9 - Mistake: Distinguish common, mutual and unilateral mistake and their effect on the validity of a contract
- L4-Contract-10 - Duress and undue influence: Identify economic duress and actual/presumed undue influence and their effect on a contract
- L4-Contract-11 - Discharge and remedies: Explain discharge by performance, breach and frustration, and select the appropriate remedy

## How to teach this
Open with: a business buys 200 specialist components "of merchantable quality, fit for outdoor use" from a catalogue with a clause excluding "all liability for defects howsoever arising." A third of the units corrode within weeks. Ask what the buyer can do, and against what obstacles.

#### L4-Contract-6 Express and implied terms
**Express terms** are those actually stated by the parties, orally or in writing. **Implied terms** arise from statute (e.g. Sale of Goods Act 1979 ss.12-15), from custom, or from fact — the officious bystander test (*Shirlaw v Southern Foundries* [1939] 2 KB 206) and the business efficacy test (*The Moorcock* (1889) 14 PD 64) ask whether a term is so obvious it goes without saying, or necessary to make the contract work commercially. Terms are classified as **conditions** (breach allows termination plus damages), **warranties** (damages only), or **innominate terms** (the remedy depends on the seriousness of the actual consequences of breach: *Hong Kong Fir Shipping Co v Kawasaki Kisen Kaisha* [1962] 2 QB 26).

#### L4-Contract-7 Exclusion and limitation clauses
An exclusion clause must first be **incorporated** (by signature, reasonable notice before or at the time of contracting — *Olley v Marlborough Court Hotel* [1949] 1 KB 532 — or a consistent course of dealing) and then construed to cover the loss that occurred, construed contra proferentem against the party relying on it where ambiguous. Statutory controls then apply: the **Unfair Contract Terms Act 1977** renders a clause excluding liability for death or personal injury caused by negligence void, and subjects other exclusions of negligence liability and, in business-to-business standard-term contracts, clauses on breach to a **reasonableness test**. For consumer contracts the **Consumer Rights Act 2015** instead applies a fairness test to a wide range of terms.

#### L4-Contract-8 Misrepresentation
A **misrepresentation** is a false statement of fact (not opinion or future intention, save exceptions) made by one party that induces the other to enter the contract. It is **fraudulent** if made knowingly, without belief in its truth, or recklessly (*Derry v Peek* (1889) 14 App Cas 337); **negligent** under s.2(1) Misrepresentation Act 1967 unless the representor proves reasonable grounds for belief; or **innocent** if neither. Remedies are rescission (available for all three, subject to bars such as affirmation, lapse of time, or an intervening third-party right) and damages (as of right for fraudulent and, functionally, negligent misrepresentation under s.2(1); discretionary in lieu of rescission for innocent misrepresentation under s.2(2)).

#### L4-Contract-9 Mistake
**Common mistake** (both parties share the same false assumption, e.g. the subject matter no longer exists) can render a contract void if the mistake is fundamental (*Bell v Lever Bros* [1932] AC 161; *Great Peace Shipping v Tsavliris Salvage* [2002] EWCA Civ 1407 confirmed no separate, wider equitable jurisdiction to rescind for common mistake). **Mutual mistake** (parties are at cross-purposes) and **unilateral mistake** (one party is mistaken and the other knows or ought to know it, often as to identity: *Cundy v Lindsay* (1878) 3 App Cas 459, contrasted with *Lewis v Averay* [1972] 1 QB 198 for face-to-face dealings) can also void a contract where the mistake negates true consent.

#### L4-Contract-10 Duress and undue influence
**Duress** requires illegitimate pressure (threats to the person, or, increasingly recognised, *economic duress* — illegitimate commercial pressure leaving no practical choice: *Universe Tankships v ITWF* [1983] 1 AC 366; *DSND Subsea v Petroleum Geo-Services* [2000] BLR 530) that is a significant cause of entering the contract. **Undue influence** is equitable: *actual* undue influence requires proof of overt pressure; *presumed* undue influence arises from a relationship of trust and confidence (automatically presumed in some categories, e.g. solicitor-client, or proved on the facts) plus a transaction calling for explanation, shifting the evidential burden to the dominant party to rebut it (*Royal Bank of Scotland v Etridge (No 2)* [2001] UKHL 44).

#### L4-Contract-11 Discharge and remedies
A contract is **discharged** by full performance, by agreement, by **breach** (actual or anticipatory, entitling the innocent party to terminate only for breach of condition or a sufficiently serious innominate-term breach), or by **frustration** — a supervening event, without either party's fault, that renders performance impossible, illegal, or radically different from what was undertaken (*Davis Contractors Ltd v Fareham UDC* [1956] AC 696), with financial adjustment under the Law Reform (Frustrated Contracts) Act 1943. **Remedies** for breach centre on **damages** to put the claimant in the position as if the contract had been performed, subject to the remoteness test from *Hadley v Baxendale* (1854) 9 Exch 341 and a duty to mitigate; **specific performance** and **injunctions** are discretionary equitable remedies granted where damages are an inadequate remedy, typically for unique subject matter such as land.
