# L5_Tort_Law - Lesson: Tort Law — Negligence, occupiers' liability, nuisance and vicarious liability

## Goal
The learner applies the negligence framework, occupiers' liability, private nuisance and vicarious liability to a tort problem, and selects defences and remedies.

## Syllabus items taught here
- L5-Tort-1 - Duty of care: Apply the Caparo/Robinson approach to establish whether a duty of care exists
- L5-Tort-2 - Breach of duty: Apply the reasonable person standard and the Bolton v Stone risk factors to decide breach
- L5-Tort-3 - Causation and remoteness: Apply the but-for test, novus actus interveniens and the Wagon Mound remoteness test
- L5-Tort-4 - Occupiers' liability: Apply the Occupiers' Liability Acts 1957 and 1984 to visitors and non-visitors
- L5-Tort-5 - Nuisance and Rylands v Fletcher: Distinguish private and public nuisance and apply the rule in Rylands v Fletcher
- L5-Tort-6 - Vicarious liability: Apply the employment/akin-to-employment and close-connection tests for vicarious liability
- L5-Tort-7 - Defences and remedies in tort: Apply contributory negligence, volenti and illegality, and select the appropriate remedy

## How to teach this
Open with: a supermarket mops a floor, puts out a small "wet floor" sign, then leaves the area unattended for 40 minutes. A customer slips and breaks her wrist. Ask whether the supermarket is liable, working through each element of negligence in order rather than jumping straight to a conclusion.

#### L5-Tort-1 Duty of care
The modern test for a novel duty situation comes from *Caparo Industries plc v Dickman* [1990] 2 AC 605: was harm **reasonably foreseeable**; was there sufficient **proximity** between claimant and defendant; and is it **fair, just and reasonable** to impose a duty. The Supreme Court in *Robinson v Chief Constable of West Yorkshire Police* [2018] UKSC 4 confirmed that established duty categories (driver-road user, occupier-visitor, employer-employee, doctor-patient) should simply be applied by analogy, with the full Caparo test reserved for genuinely novel situations, developed incrementally rather than from first principles each time.

#### L5-Tort-2 Breach of duty
Breach asks whether the defendant fell below the standard of the **reasonable person** in their position (*Blyth v Birmingham Waterworks Co* (1856) 11 Ex 781). Courts weigh the magnitude of foreseeable risk (probability and severity of harm), the cost and practicality of precautions, and any special social utility of the defendant's conduct (*Bolton v Stone* [1951] AC 850, a cricket ball escaping a ground only very rarely did not require higher fencing, given how small and infrequent the risk was; *Paris v Stepney Borough Council* [1951] AC 367, a known one-eyed employee was owed a higher standard of care for eye protection given the greater severity of harm to him specifically).

#### L5-Tort-3 Causation and remoteness
**Factual causation** uses the "but for" test: but for the defendant's breach, would the harm have occurred (*Barnett v Chelsea & Kensington Hospital Management Committee* [1969] 1 QB 428, a doctor's negligent failure to examine a patient did not cause death, because the patient would have died anyway from arsenic poisoning). **Legal causation** then asks whether the chain was broken by a **novus actus interveniens** (an intervening act, whether by a third party, the claimant, or a natural event) and, separately, whether the type of harm was reasonably foreseeable at all — **remoteness**, from *The Wagon Mound (No 1)* [1961] AC 388 (liability only for damage of a foreseeable kind, even if its full extent or manner of occurrence was not: *Hughes v Lord Advocate* [1963] AC 837).

#### L5-Tort-4 Occupiers' liability
The **Occupiers' Liability Act 1957** imposes a common duty of care on an occupier towards lawful **visitors**, to take such care as is reasonable to see the visitor is reasonably safe in using the premises for the permitted purpose. The **Occupiers' Liability Act 1984** imposes a more limited duty towards **non-visitors** (including trespassers) only where the occupier knows or has reasonable grounds to believe a danger exists, knows or has reasonable grounds to believe the non-visitor is or may be in its vicinity, and the risk is one the occupier might reasonably be expected to offer some protection against (*British Railways Board v Herrington* [1972] AC 877 established the earlier common-law duty this Act now codifies).

#### L5-Tort-5 Nuisance and Rylands v Fletcher
**Private nuisance** is an unlawful, indirect interference with a person's use or enjoyment of land, balancing the reasonableness of the defendant's conduct against the character of the locality, the duration and sensitivity of the interference (*Hunter v Canary Wharf Ltd* [1997] AC 655 restricts standing to those with a proprietary interest in the land). **Public nuisance** affects a class of Her Majesty's subjects and is actionable by an individual who suffers particular damage beyond the general inconvenience. The rule in ***Rylands v Fletcher*** (1868) LR 3 HL 330 imposes strict liability where a defendant brings onto land, for a non-natural use, something likely to do mischief if it escapes, and it does escape causing foreseeable damage (*Cambridge Water Co v Eastern Counties Leather plc* [1994] 2 AC 264 confirmed foreseeability of damage is required).

#### L5-Tort-6 Vicarious liability
An employer can be **vicariously liable** for torts committed by an employee (or a person in a relationship "akin to employment," *Various Claimants v Catholic Child Welfare Society* [2012] UKSC 56) where the tort was committed in the course of employment — assessed by whether the wrongful conduct was so closely connected with acts the employee was authorised to do that it can fairly be regarded as done in the course of employment (the "close connection" test, *Lister v Hesley Hall Ltd* [2001] UKHL 22; applied restrictively to an employer's own business purpose in *WM Morrison Supermarkets plc v Various Claimants* [2020] UKSC 12).

#### L5-Tort-7 Defences and remedies in tort
**Contributory negligence** under the Law Reform (Contributory Negligence) Act 1945 allows damages to be reduced by the proportion the claimant's own fault contributed to the damage. ***Volenti non fit injuria*** (voluntary assumption of risk) is a complete defence where the claimant freely and with full knowledge consented to the specific risk of the defendant's negligence, not merely to the general risk of the activity. **Illegality** (*ex turpi causa*) may bar a claim closely connected with the claimant's own serious criminal conduct. The principal **remedy** in tort is **damages**, aiming to restore the claimant to their pre-tort position, alongside injunctions (chiefly in nuisance) to restrain a continuing wrong.
