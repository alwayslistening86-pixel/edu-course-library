# L6_Jurisprudence_and_Legal_Theory - Lesson: Jurisprudence and Legal Theory

## Goal
The learner explains and applies natural law, positivism, Dworkin's rights thesis, critical legal studies and feminist legal theory to a jurisprudential problem.

## Syllabus items taught here
- L6-Jurisprudence-1 - Natural law theory: Explain the natural law claim that an unjust law may not be true law
- L6-Jurisprudence-2 - Legal positivism: Austin and Hart: Explain Austin's command theory and Hart's union of primary and secondary rules
- L6-Jurisprudence-3 - Dworkin's rights thesis: Explain Dworkin's principles-based critique of Hart and the one-right-answer thesis
- L6-Jurisprudence-4 - The Hart-Fuller-Devlin debates: Explain the Hart-Fuller debate on law and morality and the Hart-Devlin debate on law and enforced morality
- L6-Jurisprudence-5 - Critical legal studies: Explain the CLS claim that law reflects and entrenches power structures
- L6-Jurisprudence-6 - Feminist legal theory: Explain feminist critiques of apparently neutral legal categories

## How to teach this
Open with: a regime passes a law requiring citizens to report neighbours who criticise the government. Years later, after the regime falls, a court must decide whether people who complied with that law, and harmed others by doing so, can be held legally responsible now. Was the wartime law ever really "law" at all — and does that question even matter to how a court should rule? This closely tracks the real post-war Radbruch debate in Germany.

#### L6-Jurisprudence-1 Natural law theory
**Natural law** holds that law is bound up with morality: an unjust "law" that violates fundamental moral principles may not be true law at all — *lex injusta non est lex*, "an unjust law is not law." Rooted in thinkers from Aquinas onward, natural law thinking was revived after the Second World War, partly by Gustav Radbruch's own post-war shift, in response to the horror of regimes that were technically "lawful" under their own internal rules. A natural lawyer facing the reporting-law scenario would argue the law was never truly law at all given its gross injustice, supporting holding compliant citizens responsible now.

#### L6-Jurisprudence-2 Legal positivism: Austin and Hart
**Positivism** separates law from morality analytically: a rule can be valid law purely by having been made through the correct legal process, regardless of its moral content. **John Austin** framed law as the sovereign's commands, backed by sanctions and habitually obeyed. **H.L.A. Hart** refined this in *The Concept of Law* (1961) with the idea of a **union of primary and secondary rules**: primary rules impose duties, while secondary rules (the rule of recognition, rules of change, and rules of adjudication) let a legal system identify, alter and apply its own rules. Hart's **rule of recognition** is what ultimately makes a rule valid law in a given system, independent of its justice — a positivist would say the reporting law was valid law at the time, properly enacted and recognised by the system's own rule of recognition, though that does not settle whether compliant citizens should be held responsible now.

#### L6-Jurisprudence-3 Dworkin's rights thesis
**Ronald Dworkin** challenged Hart, arguing law is not just rules but also **principles** — moral standards that judges must weigh even in hard cases where no rule clearly applies. Dworkin argued there is, in his view, generally a single "right answer" that a sufficiently skilled judge (his hypothetical judge "Hercules") could reach by integrating law and political morality into the most coherent justification of the legal system as a whole, rather than simply exercising discretion where rules run out, as Hart's positivism suggested.

#### L6-Jurisprudence-4 The Hart-Fuller-Devlin debates
The **Hart-Fuller debate** (following Hart's 1958 Harvard Law Review article and Lon Fuller's reply) concerns whether law and morality are conceptually separate (Hart's position) or whether law necessarily embodies an "inner morality" of legality — such as clarity, prospectivity, and consistency — without which a system of rules cannot properly be called law at all (Fuller's position, illustrated by his own Nazi "grudge informer" case discussion). The separate **Hart-Devlin debate** concerns whether the law should enforce a society's shared morality even where conduct causes no direct harm to others (Devlin's position, from his critique of the Wolfenden Report's recommendations on decriminalising private homosexual conduct) against Hart's more liberal, harm-focused view of the proper limits of criminal law.

#### L6-Jurisprudence-5 Critical legal studies
**Critical legal studies (CLS)** argues that law is not a neutral, coherent, rationally-determined system, but reflects and entrenches existing structures of power, often sufficiently indeterminate that legal reasoning can be, and is, manipulated to serve dominant political and economic interests. CLS scholars are sceptical of the claim that legal doctrine constrains judicial outcomes in the way orthodox legal theory suggests, and instead emphasise the political choices embedded in supposedly technical legal reasoning.

#### L6-Jurisprudence-6 Feminist legal theory
**Feminist legal theory** examines how law has historically been shaped by, and continues to reinforce, gendered assumptions, questioning whether apparently neutral legal categories — the "reasonable person," freedom of contract, the public/private divide — are as neutral as they claim, or in fact encode a male-default perspective that disadvantages women in application. Different strands range from liberal feminist calls for formal equality within existing legal categories, to radical and cultural feminist critiques that argue the categories themselves need to be reconceived.
