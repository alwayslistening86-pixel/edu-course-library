# L4_Public_Law - Lesson: Public Law — Constitutional principles and judicial review

## Goal
The learner explains the core constitutional principles and applies the grounds and remedies of judicial review to a public-law problem.

## Syllabus items taught here
- L4-Public-1 - Parliamentary sovereignty: Explain the Diceyan doctrine of parliamentary sovereignty and its modern limits
- L4-Public-2 - The rule of law: Apply Dicey's and Bingham's conceptions of the rule of law to a fact pattern
- L4-Public-3 - Separation of powers: Identify the legislative, executive and judicial functions and evaluate their separation in the UK constitution
- L4-Public-4 - The royal prerogative: Identify a prerogative power and apply the limits on its use and justiciability
- L4-Public-5 - Human Rights Act 1998: Apply ss.2, 3, 4 and 6 HRA 1998 to a rights-compatibility problem
- L4-Public-6 - Grounds for judicial review: Apply illegality, irrationality and procedural impropriety, including legitimate expectation, to a public-law problem
- L4-Public-7 - Judicial review procedure and remedies: Identify standing, time limits and the discretionary remedies available on judicial review

## How to teach this
Open with: a government minister announces a new policy by press release, bypassing the consultation process the department's own guidance requires, and a business that would have objected is shut out entirely. Ask what can be done in law, and against whom.

#### L4-Public-1 Parliamentary sovereignty
The orthodox Diceyan view holds that Parliament can make or unmake any law, no person or body may override or set aside a valid Act of Parliament, and no Parliament can bind a future Parliament (the doctrine of implied repeal). This remains the foundational principle of the UK's uncodified constitution, though modern scholarship and cases such as *R (Jackson) v Attorney General* [2005] UKHL 56 (obiter suggestions that courts might not recognise an Act removing judicial review entirely) debate its outer limits, and "constitutional statutes" such as the Human Rights Act 1998 are now treated as immune from implied repeal (*Thoburn v Sunderland City Council* [2002] EWHC 195 (Admin)).

#### L4-Public-2 The rule of law
The **rule of law** broadly requires that government acts according to law, that law is accessible, clear and prospective, and that everyone, including the state, is subject to it and to the ordinary courts (Dicey's formal conception, refined by Lord Bingham's more substantive account in *The Rule of Law* (2010) to include access to justice and compliance with international human rights obligations). It underpins judicial review: if executive action need not comply with law, the rule of law is meaningless.

#### L4-Public-3 Separation of powers
The **separation of powers** divides legislative, executive and judicial functions, imperfectly in the UK's uncodified constitution: historically the Lord Chancellor straddled all three, and the creation of the UK Supreme Court in 2009 (replacing the House of Lords' Appellate Committee) was itself a separation-of-powers reform, hiving off the senior judicial function from the legislature. The executive is drawn from and accountable to the legislature (fusion, not strict separation), while judicial independence is protected by security of tenure and the sub judice convention.

#### L4-Public-4 The royal prerogative
The **royal prerogative** comprises residual, common-law executive powers exercisable without Parliament's specific authorisation, such as treaty-making and aspects of foreign affairs and defence. Prerogative power cannot be used to override or act inconsistently with statute (*Attorney-General v De Keyser's Royal Hotel Ltd* [1920] AC 508), and its exercise, or a decision not to exercise it, is increasingly subject to judicial review where the subject matter is justiciable (*R (Miller) v Secretary of State for Exiting the European Union* [2017] UKSC 5, holding the prerogative could not be used to trigger EU withdrawal without statutory authority because it would alter domestic law).

#### L4-Public-5 Human Rights Act 1998
The HRA 1998 gives further effect to the European Convention on Human Rights in domestic law. Section 6 makes it unlawful for a public authority to act incompatibly with a Convention right. Section 3 requires courts to read and give effect to legislation compatibly with Convention rights "so far as it is possible to do so." Section 4 lets higher courts issue a **declaration of incompatibility** where s.3 interpretation is not possible — this does not strike the statute down, respecting parliamentary sovereignty, but signals that Parliament should act. Section 2 requires courts to "take into account" Strasbourg jurisprudence, without being strictly bound by it.

#### L4-Public-6 Grounds for judicial review
A public body's decision can be challenged on the three classic grounds from *Council of Civil Service Unions v Minister for the Civil Service* [1985] AC 374 ("GCHQ"): **illegality** (acting outside legal power, or misapplying/misunderstanding it, including fettering discretion or acting for an improper purpose); **irrationality** or "Wednesbury unreasonableness" (a decision so unreasonable that no reasonable decision-maker could have made it: *Associated Provincial Picture Houses v Wednesbury Corporation* [1948] 1 KB 223); and **procedural impropriety** (failing to follow a required procedure or breaching the rules of natural justice — a fair hearing and the absence of bias — including breach of a **legitimate expectation** created by a body's own clear promise or established practice).

#### L4-Public-7 Judicial review procedure and remedies
A claim for judicial review is brought in the Administrative Court, requires the claimant to have **standing** (a "sufficient interest" in the matter, Senior Courts Act 1981 s.31(3)), and must generally be brought **promptly and in any event within three months** of the grounds first arising, with permission from the court required to proceed. Available remedies are discretionary: a **quashing order** (nullifying the decision), a **prohibiting order**, a **mandatory order** (compelling performance of a duty), a **declaration**, and, less commonly, damages (only where a separate private-law cause of action or a Convention right breach also exists).
