# L4_Criminal_1 - Lesson: Criminal Law I — General principles: actus reus, mens rea and homicide

## Goal
The learner identifies the actus reus and mens rea of an offence, applies the coincidence principle, and distinguishes murder from voluntary and involuntary manslaughter.

## Syllabus items taught here
- L4-Criminal-1 - Actus reus: Identify the conduct, circumstance and result elements of actus reus, including omissions and causation
- L4-Criminal-2 - Mens rea: Distinguish direct and oblique intention from subjective recklessness and apply the coincidence principle
- L4-Criminal-3 - Murder and voluntary manslaughter: Apply the definition of murder and the partial defences of loss of control and diminished responsibility
- L4-Criminal-4 - Involuntary manslaughter: Distinguish unlawful act manslaughter from gross negligence manslaughter

## How to teach this
Open with: Dev is driving within the speed limit when a child runs into the road. He swerves, avoids the child, but clips a parked car and drives off without stopping or reporting it, panicking. Ask which moments in the story actually matter in law, and why the sympathetic moment (avoiding the child) is not where liability sits.

#### L4-Criminal-1 Actus reus
The **actus reus** is the external, physical element of an offence: an act, a **failure to act** where the law imposes a duty to act (a duty can arise from statute, contract, a special relationship, voluntary assumption of care, or creation of a dangerous situation — *R v Miller* [1983] 2 AC 161, a squatter who accidentally started a fire and failed to take reasonable steps once aware of it), or a state of affairs. Conduct must generally be **voluntary**: a genuinely involuntary movement, such as a reflex or a seizure, cannot found liability. For result crimes, actus reus also requires **causation** in fact (the but-for test) and in law (an operating and substantial cause, unbroken by a novus actus interveniens).

#### L4-Criminal-2 Mens rea
The **mens rea** is the mental element: **intention** (direct, where the result is the defendant's purpose, or oblique, where the result is a virtually certain consequence of the defendant's act and the defendant appreciated that: *R v Woollin* [1999] 1 AC 82) or **recklessness** (assessed subjectively in English law — did *this* defendant actually foresee the risk and take it unreasonably: *R v G* [2003] UKHL 50, overruling the earlier objective *Caldwell* test), or, for some offences, negligence. The two elements must generally **coincide** in time, though courts take a flexible view where a continuing act overlaps with a later-formed guilty mind (*Fagan v Metropolitan Police Commissioner* [1969] 1 QB 439) or under the "single transaction" principle (*Thabo Meli v R* [1954] 1 WLR 228).

#### L4-Criminal-3 Murder and voluntary manslaughter
**Murder** is the unlawful killing of a human being under the Queen's/King's peace with **malice aforethought** — intention to kill or cause grievous bodily harm. Two partial defences reduce murder to **voluntary manslaughter**, both under the Coroners and Justice Act 2009: **loss of control** (s.54, requiring a qualifying trigger such as fear of serious violence or things said/done of an extremely grave character, and that a person of the defendant's age and sex with a normal degree of tolerance might have reacted similarly) and **diminished responsibility** (s.52, requiring an abnormality of mental functioning from a recognised medical condition that substantially impairs the defendant's ability to understand their conduct, form rational judgment, or exercise self-control, and provides an explanation for the killing).

#### L4-Criminal-4 Involuntary manslaughter
**Unlawful act (constructive) manslaughter** requires an intentional unlawful act, dangerous in the sense that a sober and reasonable person would recognise the risk of some harm (*R v Church* [1966] 1 QB 59), which causes death — no intention to cause harm is needed. **Gross negligence manslaughter** requires a duty of care, breach of that duty, a risk of death that was reasonably foreseeable, and a breach so bad in all the circumstances as to be characterised as criminal (*R v Adomako* [1995] 1 AC 171). Both categories differ from murder in requiring no intention to kill or cause GBH.

## Explicitly not here
Non-fatal offences, theft-based offences, defences and inchoate offences follow in L4_Criminal_2.
