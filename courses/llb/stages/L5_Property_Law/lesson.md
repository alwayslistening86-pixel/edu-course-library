# L5_Property_Law - Lesson: Property Law — Estates, registration, leases, easements, covenants and mortgages

## Goal
The learner identifies legal estates and interests in land, applies the registration rules, and distinguishes leases, easements, covenants and mortgages on the facts.

## Syllabus items taught here
- L5-Property-1 - Legal estates in land: Identify the freehold and leasehold legal estates under LPA 1925 s.1
- L5-Property-2 - Registered land and the LRA 2002: Apply the three registers, the mirror principle and overriding interests to a registered land problem
- L5-Property-3 - Leases and licences: Apply the exclusive possession test from Street v Mountford to distinguish a lease from a licence
- L5-Property-4 - Easements: Apply the Re Ellenborough Park requirements and prescription to an easement problem
- L5-Property-5 - Freehold covenants: Explain when the burden and benefit of freehold covenants run with the land
- L5-Property-6 - Mortgages: Identify the creation of a legal mortgage and the mortgagee's and mortgagor's principal rights
- L5-Property-7 - Co-ownership and trusts of land: Distinguish joint tenancy from tenancy in common and apply TOLATA 1996 to a co-ownership dispute

## How to teach this
Open with: Anna buys a house. After moving in, she discovers her elderly neighbour has used a path across her back garden "forever" to reach his shed, and a delivery company claims a right to park a van on part of her drive under an old, unregistered agreement with the previous owner. Ask whether Anna is bound by either.

#### L5-Property-1 Legal estates in land
English land law does not allow absolute ownership of land (all land is technically held of the Crown); instead a person holds an **estate**. Section 1(1) **Law of Property Act 1925** recognises only two legal estates: the **fee simple absolute in possession** (freehold — the largest estate, effectively perpetual ownership) and the **term of years absolute** (leasehold — ownership for a fixed, defined period, however short or long). Section 1(2) lists a closed set of interests capable of existing as legal interests (such as legal easements and legal mortgages); anything else can exist only as an equitable interest.

#### L5-Property-2 Registered land and the LRA 2002
Since the **Land Registration Act 2002**, most land in England and Wales is registered at HM Land Registry, which maintains three registers for each title: the **property register** (describing the land and rights benefiting it), the **proprietorship register** (who owns it and the class of title), and the **charges register** (burdens on the property, such as mortgages and restrictive covenants). The register aims to be a "mirror" of the rights affecting title, though an imperfect one: unregistered interests can still bind a purchaser as **interests that override**, most importantly the interests of persons in **actual occupation** under Schedule 3, paragraph 2 LRA 2002 (following the logic of *Williams & Glyn's Bank Ltd v Boland* [1981] AC 487 under the pre-2002 law).

#### L5-Property-3 Leases and licences
A **lease** creates a proprietary interest — **exclusive possession** for a certain term, generally at a rent — capable of binding third parties; a **licence** is merely personal permission to be on land, creating no proprietary interest and, ordinarily, not binding a purchaser. The key distinguishing factor is **exclusive possession** (*Street v Mountford* [1985] AC 809): if the occupier genuinely has exclusive possession for a certain term, it is a lease regardless of the label the parties chose, save for recognised exceptions such as genuine service occupancy or a clear lack of intention to create legal relations (e.g. a family arrangement, *Facchini v Bryson* [1952] 1 TLR 1386).

#### L5-Property-4 Easements
An **easement** is a right benefiting one piece of land (the dominant tenement) over another (the servient tenement) — such as a right of way. For a right to be capable of existing as an easement, the classic requirements from *Re Ellenborough Park* [1956] Ch 131 apply: there must be a dominant and a servient tenement, the easement must **accommodate** the dominant tenement (benefit the land itself, not merely the current owner personally), the two tenements must be owned or occupied by different people, and the right must be capable of forming the subject matter of a grant (sufficiently certain, not amounting to exclusive possession of the servient land). A **prescriptive easement** can arise from long, continuous use — broadly 20 years — that is open, without permission, force or secrecy (*nec vi, nec clam, nec precario*).

#### L5-Property-5 Freehold covenants
A **freehold covenant** is a promise about the use of land made between freeholders. At common law, the **burden** of a covenant does not run with the servient land to a successor in title (*Austerberry v Oldham Corporation* (1885) 29 Ch D 750), but equity allows the burden of a **restrictive** (negative) covenant to run if it touches and concerns the land, was intended to run, and the successor had notice (*Tulk v Moxhay* (1848) 2 Ph 774), now protected by registration as a land charge or notice on the register. The **benefit** of a covenant can run at law or in equity more readily, provided it touches and concerns the dominant land and was intended to benefit it.

#### L5-Property-6 Mortgages
A **legal mortgage** of registered land is created by a registered charge (Land Registration Act 2002 s.23, replacing the older methods of a long lease or a mortgage by demise). The **mortgagee** (lender) has, on default, the rights to sue on the personal covenant to repay, to take possession, to appoint a receiver, and, most significantly, a statutory **power of sale** (Law of Property Act 1925 s.101, arising once the power has become exercisable, typically on default) to realise the security. The **mortgagor** (borrower) retains an **equity of redemption** — the right to redeem the property on repayment — which cannot be unfairly restricted or excluded by a "clog" on the equity of redemption.

#### L5-Property-7 Co-ownership and trusts of land
Where land is co-owned, the legal title is always held as **joint tenants** (a maximum of four legal owners, with the right of survivorship applying automatically), holding on trust for the equitable owners, who may hold as **joint tenants** (each owns the whole, with survivorship) or **tenants in common** (each owns a distinct, severable share, with no survivorship) — distinguished by the "four unities" for a joint tenancy and by evidence of unequal contribution, an express declaration of shares, or commercial intent for a tenancy in common. The **Trusts of Land and Appointment of Trustees Act 1996 (TOLATA)** governs disputes between co-owners, including applications under s.14 for an order for sale or to determine the extent of a beneficial interest.
