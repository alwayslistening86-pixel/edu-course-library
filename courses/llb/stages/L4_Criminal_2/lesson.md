# L4_Criminal_2 - Lesson: Criminal Law II — Non-fatal and property offences, defences and inchoate liability

## Goal
The learner applies the non-fatal offences against the person, the main Theft Act offences, the general defences and the inchoate offences to a problem question.

## Syllabus items taught here
- L4-Criminal-5 - Non-fatal offences against the person: Distinguish assault, battery, ABH and GBH/wounding on the facts
- L4-Criminal-6 - Theft: Apply the five elements of theft under the Theft Act 1968
- L4-Criminal-7 - Robbery, burglary and fraud: Apply the elements of robbery, burglary and the main Fraud Act 2006 offence
- L4-Criminal-8 - General defences: self-defence, duress and necessity: Apply the tests for self-defence/reasonable force, duress by threats and necessity
- L4-Criminal-9 - General defences: insanity and intoxication: Apply the M'Naghten insanity rules and the voluntary/involuntary intoxication rules
- L4-Criminal-10 - Inchoate offences: attempt and conspiracy: Apply the actus reus and mens rea of criminal attempt and conspiracy

## How to teach this
Open with: three friends plan to break into a warehouse. One keeps watch, one disables an alarm, and one is caught inside holding stock before any of them leave the building. Ask what each has actually done in law, and whether the plan itself is already an offence before anyone enters.

#### L4-Criminal-5 Non-fatal offences against the person
**Assault** is causing the victim to apprehend immediate unlawful force; **battery** is the application of unlawful force. **Actual bodily harm (ABH)**, under s.47 Offences Against the Person Act 1861, requires an assault or battery causing harm that interferes with health or comfort, more than merely transient (*R v Miller* [1954] 2 QB 282). **Grievous bodily harm/wounding**, under s.20 (maliciously) or the more serious s.18 (with intent to cause GBH or resist arrest), requires "really serious harm" (*DPP v Smith* [1961] AC 290) or a wound (breaking of both layers of skin). The mens rea for s.20 requires only foresight of some harm, not the serious harm actually caused (*R v Mowatt* [1968] 1 QB 421); s.18 requires specific intent.

#### L4-Criminal-6 Theft
Theft under s.1 Theft Act 1968 requires: **dishonesty** (assessed by the objective standard from *Ivey v Genting Casinos* [2017] UKSC 67, now applied in criminal law via *R v Barton and Booth* [2020] EWCA Crim 575 — was the conduct dishonest by the standards of ordinary decent people, given the defendant's actual knowledge/belief), **appropriation** (any assumption of the owner's rights, s.3, which can occur even with the owner's consent: *R v Gomez* [1993] AC 442; *R v Hinks* [2001] 1 AC 241), **property** (s.4), **belonging to another** (s.5), and **intention permanently to deprive** (s.6, which can include a "borrowing" equivalent to outright taking).

#### L4-Criminal-7 Robbery, burglary and fraud
**Robbery** (s.8 Theft Act 1968) is theft accompanied by force or the threat of force immediately before or at the time of the theft, used in order to steal. **Burglary** (s.9) is entering a building (or part of one) as a trespasser with intent to steal, cause grievous bodily harm, or cause criminal damage (s.9(1)(a)), or, having entered as a trespasser, going on to steal or attempt to steal or inflict/attempt to inflict grievous bodily harm (s.9(1)(b)). The main **Fraud Act 2006** offence, fraud by false representation (s.2), requires a false representation, made dishonestly, with intent to make a gain or cause a loss — it is a conduct crime, requiring no actual gain or loss to be caused.

#### L4-Criminal-8 General defences: self-defence, duress and necessity
**Self-defence** (partly codified, s.76 Criminal Justice and Immigration Act 2008) requires the defendant to have honestly believed force was necessary (even if that belief was mistaken, provided genuinely held) and to have used force that was reasonable in the circumstances as the defendant believed them to be. **Duress by threats** requires a threat of death or serious injury to the defendant or someone close to them, causing the defendant reasonably to believe they had no safe avenue of escape, judged against a person of reasonable firmness sharing the defendant's characteristics (*R v Graham* [1982] 1 WLR 294; *R v Hasan* [2005] UKHL 22 restricts the defence where the defendant voluntarily associated with violent people). **Necessity** is a narrow, rarely successful defence recognised chiefly in extreme circumstances (*Re A (Conjoined Twins)* [2001] Fam 147).

#### L4-Criminal-9 General defences: insanity and intoxication
**Insanity** (the M'Naghten Rules (1843) 10 Cl & Fin 200) requires a defect of reason from a disease of the mind such that the defendant did not know the nature and quality of the act, or did not know it was wrong. **Voluntary intoxication** is no defence to crimes of basic intent (where recklessness suffices for mens rea) but may negate the specific intent required for crimes of specific intent, such as murder or s.18 GBH (*DPP v Majewski* [1977] AC 443). **Involuntary intoxication** may negate mens rea for any offence if the defendant genuinely lacked the required mental element, though it is no defence if the defendant still formed the necessary intent despite being involuntarily intoxicated.

#### L4-Criminal-10 Inchoate offences: attempt and conspiracy
**Criminal attempt** (Criminal Attempts Act 1981 s.1) requires an act that is "more than merely preparatory" to the commission of the full offence, done with the intention (or, for some offence elements, the same mens rea as the full offence) to commit it — *R v Gullefer* [1990] 1 WLR 1063 sets the "embarked on the crime proper" test. **Conspiracy** (Criminal Law Act 1977 s.1, or common law conspiracy to defraud) requires an agreement between two or more people to pursue a course of conduct that, if carried out, would necessarily amount to or involve the commission of an offence by at least one of them, with intention that the offence be committed.
