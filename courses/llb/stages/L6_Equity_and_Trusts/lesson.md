# L6_Equity_and_Trusts - Lesson: Equity and the Law of Trusts

## Goal
The learner applies the three certainties, resulting and constructive trust doctrine, trustees' duties, breach of trust and the equitable remedies to a trusts problem.

## Syllabus items taught here
- L6-Equity-1 - Nature and maxims of equity: Explain the historical development of equity and apply its core maxims
- L6-Equity-2 - The three certainties: Apply the certainties of intention, subject matter and objects from Knight v Knight
- L6-Equity-3 - Formalities and constitution of trusts: Apply the formality requirements and the rule that equity will not perfect an imperfect gift
- L6-Equity-4 - Resulting trusts: Apply the presumptions of resulting trust and advancement to a contribution-to-purchase-price problem
- L6-Equity-5 - Constructive trusts: Apply the common-intention-plus-detrimental-reliance framework from Stack v Dowden and Jones v Kernott
- L6-Equity-6 - Trustees' duties and powers: Apply the fiduciary duties and the Trustee Act 2000 standard of care
- L6-Equity-7 - Breach of trust: Identify a breach of trust and the trustee's personal liability to restore the fund
- L6-Equity-8 - Equitable remedies: Select between injunction, specific performance, rescission, rectification and tracing on the facts

## How to teach this
Open with: a woman transfers her house into her brother's name "to keep it safe from creditors" while continuing to live in it, pay the bills, and treat it as her own, with an informal understanding he will transfer it back later. The brother later refuses, claiming the house is legally his. Ask what, if anything, equity can do.

#### L6-Equity-1 Nature and maxims of equity
Equity developed historically in the Court of Chancery to soften the rigidity of the common law, granting discretionary relief "in personam" against a defendant's conscience where the common law offered no adequate remedy. Its core **maxims** capture this character: "equity will not suffer a wrong to be without a remedy," "he who comes to equity must come with clean hands," "equity looks to intent rather than form," "equity will not assist a volunteer," and "delay defeats equity" (laches). These maxims explain why equitable remedies remain **discretionary**, unlike common law damages, which are available as of right once liability is established.

#### L6-Equity-2 The three certainties
For an express trust to exist, three certainties (from *Knight v Knight* (1840) 3 Beav 148) must be present: **certainty of intention** (a clear intention to impose a binding trust obligation, not merely a moral obligation, hope, or outright gift — precatory words such as "in full confidence that" are usually insufficient), **certainty of subject matter** (the trust property must be clearly identified, and for a trust over part of a larger bulk, the share must be a defined proportion or the specific part segregated), and **certainty of objects** (the beneficiaries must be identified or ascertainable — a fixed trust requires a complete list, while a discretionary trust need only satisfy the "is or is not" test from *McPhail v Doulton* [1971] AC 424). Failing any one certainty means no valid express trust arises.

#### L6-Equity-3 Formalities and constitution of trusts
A trust of land must be evidenced in signed writing (Law of Property Act 1925 s.53(1)(b)), though a trust of personalty can be created orally. A trust must also be **constituted** — the trust property must actually be vested in the trustee, either by an effective transfer or by the settlor validly declaring themselves trustee. **Equity will not assist a volunteer**: if a transfer to a trustee is incomplete, equity will not perfect an imperfect gift merely because the intended recipient is a beneficiary (*Milroy v Lord* (1862) 4 De GF & J 264), subject to limited exceptions such as the rule in *Strong v Bird* (1874) LR 18 Eq 315 and the broader unconscionability-based exception recognised in *Pennington v Waine* [2002] EWCA Civ 227.

#### L6-Equity-4 Resulting trusts
A **resulting trust** arises where property is transferred but beneficial ownership "results back," presumptively, to reflect the transferor's actual intention or contribution — classically where someone contributes to the purchase price of property without taking full legal title, the beneficial interest results back proportionally to that contribution (the "purchase price resulting trust"), subject to the **presumption of advancement** in some family relationships (such as father to child), which itself presumes a gift rather than a resulting trust, though that presumption is increasingly criticised as outdated and easily rebutted on modern facts.

#### L6-Equity-5 Constructive trusts
A **constructive trust** is imposed by equity regardless of the parties' express intentions, often where it would be **unconscionable** for the legal owner to deny another's beneficial interest. For family homes, this is classically where there is a common intention (express or inferred from conduct) that both parties should have a beneficial interest, and the claimant has acted to their detriment in reliance on it (*Lloyds Bank plc v Rosset* [1991] 1 AC 107), refined by *Stack v Dowden* [2007] UKHL 17 and *Jones v Kernott* [2011] UKSC 53, which confirmed that once a common intention to share beneficially is established, the court can infer or, failing that, impute an intention as to the size of each share, looking at the whole course of dealing between the parties.

#### L6-Equity-6 Trustees' duties and powers
Trustees owe strict **fiduciary duties**: to act in the beneficiaries' best interests, avoid conflicts of interest, not profit personally from the trust without authorisation, act unanimously (unless the trust instrument provides otherwise), and invest trust property properly under the **Trustee Act 2000**, which imposes a statutory duty of care (heightened for professional trustees) and a duty to have regard to the standard investment criteria (suitability and diversification) when exercising the statutory power of investment.

#### L6-Equity-7 Breach of trust
A **breach of trust** occurs whenever a trustee acts outside their powers or fails to perform their duties properly, whether dishonestly or merely negligently. A trustee in breach is personally liable to restore the trust fund to the position it would have been in absent the breach, and co-trustees may be jointly and severally liable unless a specific defence applies (such as the court's statutory power to relieve a trustee who acted honestly and reasonably, Trustee Act 1925 s.61, or the beneficiaries' informed consent to the breach).

#### L6-Equity-8 Equitable remedies
Because equity is discretionary, its remedies are not awarded as of right the way common law damages are. **Injunctions** (prohibitory, restraining conduct, or mandatory, compelling it) restrain or compel specific conduct. **Specific performance** compels actual performance of a contract, typically for unique subject matter such as land, where damages would be an inadequate remedy. **Rescission** sets a transaction aside and restores the parties to their pre-contract position (subject to bars such as affirmation or an intervening third-party right). **Rectification** corrects a written document that fails to reflect the parties' true agreement. **Tracing** allows a claimant to follow trust property, or its substitute or proceeds, into the hands of a third party (including through mixed funds) to recover it or a proprietary claim over its traceable product.
