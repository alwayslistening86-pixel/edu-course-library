# L5_Family_Law - Lesson: Family Law — Formation and breakdown of relationships, finances and children

## Goal
The learner applies the rules on marriage validity, no-fault divorce, financial remedies, parental responsibility and the welfare principle to a family problem.

## Syllabus items taught here
- L5-Family-1 - Marriage, civil partnership and nullity: Distinguish void and voidable marriages/civil partnerships and their grounds
- L5-Family-2 - No-fault divorce procedure: Apply the Divorce, Dissolution and Separation Act 2020 procedure and timeline
- L5-Family-3 - Financial remedies on divorce: Apply the s.25 MCA 1973 factors and the sharing/needs/compensation principles
- L5-Family-4 - Child arrangements and the welfare principle: Apply s.1 Children Act 1989 and the welfare checklist to a child arrangements dispute
- L5-Family-5 - Parental responsibility: Identify who holds parental responsibility and how it is acquired
- L5-Family-6 - Domestic abuse and protective orders: Identify non-molestation and occupation orders and their statutory basis

## How to teach this
Open with: after 12 years of marriage, Ellie and Mark separate. Ellie gave up a promising career to raise their two children and run the home; Mark's career flourished. Ask whether Ellie gets half the assets, or whether "who earned what" matters.

#### L5-Family-1 Marriage, civil partnership and nullity
A marriage or civil partnership is **void** (treated as never having existed, no decree needed though one may be sought for clarity) where a fundamental requirement is missing, such as one party already being married, the parties being within prohibited degrees of relationship, or non-compliance with the formalities. It is **voidable** (valid until annulled) on grounds such as non-consummation (marriage only), lack of valid consent (duress, mistake, unsoundness of mind), or one party having a venereal disease or being pregnant by another at the time, unknown to the other party. The distinction matters because a void marriage can be treated as never binding for some purposes, while a voidable one has full legal effect unless and until annulled.

#### L5-Family-2 No-fault divorce procedure
Since the **Divorce, Dissolution and Separation Act 2020** (in force April 2022), England and Wales operate a **no-fault divorce** system: a party (or both jointly) need only state that the marriage has irretrievably broken down, with no need to prove fault (adultery, unreasonable behaviour) or a lengthy separation period as previously required under the Matrimonial Causes Act 1973's old fault-based facts. There is a minimum **20-week "reflection period"** between the application and the conditional order (formerly decree nisi), and a further **6 weeks** before the final order (formerly decree absolute) can be applied for.

#### L5-Family-3 Financial remedies on divorce
Courts have wide discretion under **s.25 Matrimonial Causes Act 1973** to redistribute assets on divorce, guided by a non-exhaustive list of factors: the parties' income and earning capacity, financial needs, standard of living during the marriage, ages, duration of the marriage, contributions (including non-financial contributions such as homemaking and childcare, given explicit equal weight in principle since *White v White* [2000] UKHL 54), and conduct (only in exceptional, obvious cases). The **"yardstick of equality"** from *White* means an equal starting point is often the reference for long marriages, though it can be departed from based on needs or other s.25 factors; *Miller v Miller; McFarlane v McFarlane* [2006] UKHL 24 further developed the **sharing, needs and compensation** principles as the modern analytical framework for outcomes.

#### L5-Family-4 Child arrangements and the welfare principle
Where children are involved, the **welfare principle** under **s.1 Children Act 1989** makes the child's welfare the court's **paramount** consideration in any decision about their upbringing — not merely an important factor to weigh against others, but the single overriding one. The **welfare checklist** (s.1(3)) guides this: the child's ascertainable wishes and feelings (weighted by age and understanding), physical, emotional and educational needs, the likely effect of any change in circumstances, age, sex, background and relevant characteristics, any harm suffered or at risk of suffering, and the capability of each parent (or relevant person) to meet the child's needs.

#### L5-Family-5 Parental responsibility
**Parental responsibility (PR)** is the bundle of rights, duties, powers and responsibilities a parent has in relation to a child and their property. A mother automatically acquires PR on the child's birth. A father acquires PR automatically if married to the mother at the time of birth (or later marries her), or otherwise by being registered as the father on the birth certificate (for births after 1 December 2003), by a parental responsibility agreement with the mother, or by court order. Courts start from a presumption that involvement of both parents is generally in a child's welfare, unless evidence shows that involvement would put the child at risk.

#### L5-Family-6 Domestic abuse and protective orders
The **Family Law Act 1996** provides two principal civil protective orders: a **non-molestation order**, prohibiting a person from molesting an associated person (a defined category including spouses, cohabitants and relatives), and an **occupation order**, regulating who may occupy the family home, which can exclude an abusive party even where they hold a legal interest in the property. The **Domestic Abuse Act 2021** introduced a statutory definition of domestic abuse (covering physical, emotional, coercive/controlling and economic abuse), created the offence of controlling or coercive behaviour, and established the Domestic Abuse Commissioner role, alongside enhanced protections for victims in family proceedings, including a presumption against direct cross-examination of a victim by their alleged abuser.
