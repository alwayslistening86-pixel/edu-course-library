# L6_Alternative_Dispute_Resolution - Lesson: Alternative Dispute Resolution

## Goal
The learner distinguishes negotiation, mediation, arbitration and expert determination, and applies the courts' encouragement of ADR to a costs problem.

## Syllabus items taught here
- L6-ADR-1 - Negotiation theory and practice: Distinguish positional from interest-based negotiation
- L6-ADR-2 - Mediation: Explain the mediation process, its confidentiality and the enforceability of a settlement
- L6-ADR-3 - Arbitration: Apply the Arbitration Act 1996 framework and the narrow grounds to challenge an award
- L6-ADR-4 - Expert determination and adjudication: Distinguish expert determination and statutory construction adjudication from arbitration
- L6-ADR-5 - Court encouragement of ADR: Apply the Halsey factors on costs sanctions for unreasonable refusal to engage in ADR

## How to teach this
Open with: two small businesses are locked in a £40,000 contract dispute. Litigation would cost more than half that in legal fees and take 18 months. Both want to preserve their working relationship if possible. Ask what their real options are short of court, and why a court itself might push them toward one.

#### L6-ADR-1 Negotiation theory and practice
**Negotiation** — direct discussion between parties or their representatives aiming at a mutually acceptable outcome, with no third party involved — is the most basic and common form of dispute resolution. Effective negotiation theory, particularly **principled or interest-based negotiation** associated with the Harvard Negotiation Project (Fisher and Ury, *Getting to Yes*), distinguishes parties' stated **positions** (what they say they want) from their underlying **interests** (why they want it), often finding solutions a purely positional, adversarial negotiation would miss entirely.

#### L6-ADR-2 Mediation
A neutral third-party **mediator** facilitates discussion and helps the parties reach their own agreement; the mediator has no power to impose a binding outcome, unlike a judge or arbitrator. Mediation is typically confidential and conducted "without prejudice," meaning what is said cannot be used in later litigation if the mediation fails, and it results in a settlement agreement that becomes a binding contract once signed — not automatically enforceable as a court judgment unless it is subsequently converted into a consent order approved by the court.

#### L6-ADR-3 Arbitration
Arbitration produces a **binding** decision (an "award") from a neutral arbitrator or panel chosen by the parties, under the framework of the **Arbitration Act 1996**. It is often agreed in advance via an arbitration clause in a contract, and once validly agreed the courts will generally stay any court proceedings brought in breach of it. Arbitral awards are enforceable much like court judgments, and the grounds to challenge one are deliberately narrow (ss.67-69 of the Act: lack of substantive jurisdiction, serious irregularity, or, if not excluded by the parties, an appeal on a point of law), reflecting arbitration's design as a genuine alternative to litigation, not merely a rehearsal for it.

#### L6-ADR-4 Expert determination and adjudication
**Expert determination** is a contractually agreed process where an independent expert (rather than a lawyer-arbitrator) resolves a dispute, typically on a technical or valuation question, producing a decision that is binding as a matter of contract but generally cannot be challenged on the merits, only for fraud, bias, or a failure to perform the contractually agreed task. **Statutory construction adjudication**, under the Housing Grants, Construction and Regeneration Act 1996, gives parties to most construction contracts the right to refer a dispute to adjudication at any time, with a fast (typically 28-day) timetable producing a decision binding on an interim basis pending final resolution by litigation or arbitration — designed to keep cash flowing during a project rather than to finally determine rights.

#### L6-ADR-5 Court encouragement of ADR
English civil procedure actively pushes parties toward ADR: the pre-action protocols and the Practice Direction on Pre-Action Conduct expect parties to consider ADR before issuing proceedings, and courts can impose **costs sanctions** on a party who unreasonably refuses to engage with ADR, even if that party ultimately wins the case. *Halsey v Milton Keynes General NHS Trust* [2004] EWCA Civ 576 set out the framework for when a refusal is unreasonable, considering factors including the nature of the dispute, the merits of the case, the extent to which other settlement methods had been attempted, whether the costs of ADR would be disproportionately high, and whether any delay in setting up ADR would prejudice the trial. *Churchill v Merthyr Tydfil County Borough Council* [2023] EWCA Civ 1416 went further, confirming courts can stay proceedings and order parties to engage in a non-court-based dispute resolution process, provided it does not impair the right to a fair trial.
