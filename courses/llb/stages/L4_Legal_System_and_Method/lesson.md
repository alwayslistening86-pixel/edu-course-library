# L4_Legal_System_and_Method - Lesson: The English Legal System and Legal Method

## Goal
The learner explains the sources of English law, applies precedent and the rules of statutory interpretation, traces a case through the court system, and cites legal sources correctly.

## Syllabus items taught here
- L4-LSM-1 - Sources of English law: Identify legislation, case law, retained EU law and custom as sources and explain their hierarchy
- L4-LSM-2 - Precedent and the court hierarchy: Apply stare decisis, ratio/obiter and the Practice Statement 1966 to a court hierarchy problem
- L4-LSM-3 - Statutory interpretation: the rules: Apply the literal, golden and mischief rules to a statutory wording problem
- L4-LSM-4 - Statutory interpretation: the purposive approach and aids: Apply the purposive approach and intrinsic/extrinsic aids including Pepper v Hart
- L4-LSM-5 - Civil and criminal court structure and process: Trace a claim or prosecution through the correct court structure from first instance to appeal
- L4-LSM-6 - Legal research and citation: Locate primary legal sources and cite them accurately using OSCOLA

## How to teach this
Open with: a newly enacted statute bans "vehicles" from a public park. A council officer wants to fine someone riding an electric scooter there. Nobody defined "vehicle" in the Act. Ask how a court would even go about answering that, and why the answer, once given, binds every later case with the same question.

#### L4-LSM-1 Sources of English law
English law derives from several sources: **legislation** (primary Acts of Parliament and delegated/secondary legislation made under a parent Act, subject to challenge only for exceeding the parent Act's powers, not for its content, given parliamentary sovereignty), **case law** (judge-made law through decided cases), **retained EU law** (post-Brexit, a reducing but still real body of EU-derived law preserved and now capable of departure by UK courts under the Retained EU Law (Revocation and Reform) Act 2023), and **custom** as a minor, largely historical source. Legislation is supreme over case law: a court cannot strike down a valid Act of Parliament, only interpret it.

#### L4-LSM-2 Precedent and the court hierarchy
The doctrine of **stare decisis** means a court is generally bound by the decisions of courts above it in the hierarchy (Supreme Court, then Court of Appeal, then High Court, then lower courts), and by its own past decisions unless an exception applies. The Court of Appeal binds itself subject to the exceptions in *Young v Bristol Aeroplane Co* [1944] KB 718 (conflicting past decisions, an implicitly overruled decision, or a decision given per incuriam). The Supreme Court can depart from its own past decisions "when it appears right to do so" (Practice Statement [1966] 1 WLR 1234). Only the **ratio decidendi** — the legal reasoning necessary to the decision — binds; **obiter dicta**, things said in passing, are persuasive only.

#### L4-LSM-3 Statutory interpretation: the rules
Where statutory words are unclear, courts have traditionally used the **literal rule** (plain, ordinary meaning even if the result seems odd, as in *Whiteley v Chappell* (1868) LR 4 QB 147), the **golden rule** (literal meaning unless it produces an absurdity, in which case modify it just enough to avoid that, as in *Re Sigsworth* [1935] Ch 89), and the **mischief rule** (identify the mischief the statute was passed to remedy, per *Heydon's Case* (1584) 3 Co Rep 7a, and construe the words to suppress it, as in *Smith v Hughes* [1960] 1 WLR 830).

#### L4-LSM-4 Statutory interpretation: the purposive approach and aids
The modern **purposive approach**, increasingly favoured by UK courts especially for EU-derived or rights-affecting legislation, asks what Parliament was actually trying to achieve and construes the words to fit that purpose, even departing from literal wording (*Pepper v Hart* [1993] AC 593 also permits limited use of Hansard parliamentary debates as an extrinsic aid where legislation is ambiguous or obscure and a clear ministerial statement resolves the ambiguity). Courts also use **intrinsic aids** (the Act's own long title, preamble, and interpretation/definitions sections) and other **extrinsic aids** (dictionaries, explanatory notes, and pre-existing case law on the same words).

#### L4-LSM-5 Civil and criminal court structure and process
Civil claims typically begin in the **County Court** or **High Court** (depending on value/complexity), with appeals to the Court of Appeal (Civil Division) and then, on a point of general public importance, the Supreme Court. Criminal cases begin in the **Magistrates' Court**; summary offences are tried there, either-way offences may go to the Crown Court depending on plea and allocation, and indictable-only offences go straight to the Crown Court for trial by judge and jury, with appeals from the Crown Court to the Court of Appeal (Criminal Division) and, exceptionally, the Supreme Court.

#### L4-LSM-6 Legal research and citation
Competent legal research locates primary sources directly — the actual statute on legislation.gov.uk (checking it is the version currently in force) and the actual case report, not a secondary summary — before relying on textbooks or journal articles for orientation. **OSCOLA** (the Oxford University Standard for Citation of Legal Authorities) is the standard citation convention for English legal writing: cases are cited by name in italics, then neutral or law-report citation (e.g. *Donoghue v Stevenson* [1932] AC 562); statutes by short title and year; footnotes rather than in-text citation are used for sources.
