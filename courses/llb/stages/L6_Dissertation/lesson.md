# L6_Dissertation - Lesson: Dissertation — Independent legal research and sustained argument

## Goal
The learner formulates a narrow, contestable legal research question, synthesises primary and secondary sources critically, and structures a sustained, well-referenced argument.

## Syllabus items taught here
- L6-Dissertation-1 - Formulating a research question: Turn a broad area of interest into a narrow, contestable research question
- L6-Dissertation-2 - Literature review and source synthesis: Read primary and secondary sources critically and synthesise them rather than summarise in sequence
- L6-Dissertation-3 - Research methodology: Choose and justify a doctrinal, comparative or socio-legal research method
- L6-Dissertation-4 - Structuring a sustained argument: Build an introduction, developed argument and conclusion that actually answers the research question
- L6-Dissertation-5 - Critical evaluation and engaging with uncertainty: Engage honestly with contested and unsettled areas of law rather than presenting one answer as settled
- L6-Dissertation-6 - Referencing and academic integrity: Apply OSCOLA citation and avoid plagiarism through accurate attribution

## How to teach this
Unlike every other stage in this course, the Dissertation is not graded on applying settled law to a hypothetical scenario — it is assessed as genuine independent research, per the QAA benchmark's emphasis on legal research skill and critical engagement with uncertainty. Open by asking the learner: "Which topic from this whole LLB struck you as genuinely unresolved, contested, or unsatisfying — a case where the reasoning seemed strained, an area where academics visibly disagree, or a gap between the law's stated aim and its actual effect?" That instinct, sharpened, is the seed of a good dissertation question.

#### L6-Dissertation-1 Formulating a research question
A good dissertation question is narrow enough to answer thoroughly within the word limit, but genuinely contestable — not a question with an obvious, uncontroversial answer. "What is the law on X?" is a description, not a research question. "Does the current legal test for X adequately address Y problem?" or "Should the law on X be reformed in light of Z development?" are research questions, because they require the writer to take and defend a position. A strong technique is to start from a case where the reasoning seemed strained, an area of visible academic disagreement, or a documented gap between a rule's stated aim and its actual effect, and sharpen that instinct into a precise, answerable question with clearly defined scope.

#### L6-Dissertation-2 Literature review and source synthesis
Independent research means going beyond what any single taught module covered: **primary sources** (cases, statutes and, where relevant, international instruments) should be read in full rather than via a secondary summary, and **secondary sources** (academic articles, monographs, official reports such as Law Commission papers) should be read critically, not merely cited as authority. **Synthesis** means actively showing how sources relate to each other — where they agree, where they conflict, and where the writer's own argument sits relative to the existing debate — rather than summarising each source in turn as an unconnected list.

#### L6-Dissertation-3 Research methodology
A dissertation should identify and justify its **methodology**: a **doctrinal** approach analyses what the law currently is, through cases and statutes, often to expose an inconsistency or gap; a **comparative** approach examines how another jurisdiction addresses the same problem, to illuminate choices the home jurisdiction's law makes implicitly; a **socio-legal** approach asks how law operates in practice, and its actual social effects, often drawing on empirical or documented evidence beyond the bare legal text. Naming and justifying the chosen method, rather than leaving it implicit, signals methodological self-awareness expected at this level.

#### L6-Dissertation-4 Structuring a sustained argument
A dissertation typically needs: an **introduction** stating the question and why it matters; a section **establishing the current legal position** accurately and fairly; a **critical section** identifying the problem, gap, or tension the writer has found; genuine engagement with the **strongest** counter-arguments to the writer's position, not only the weakest ones (a "steel-manned" opposing case makes the writer's own conclusion more credible, not less); and a **conclusion** that actually answers the question originally posed, rather than trailing off into vague, unsupported suggestions for "further research."

#### L6-Dissertation-5 Critical evaluation and engaging with uncertainty
The QAA benchmark explicitly values recognising uncertainty and ambiguity in legal knowledge. A dissertation that presents a genuinely contested area as though it has one obvious answer is weaker than one that honestly engages with why reasonable, competent lawyers disagree, and then still reaches a clearly defended conclusion of its own. Critical evaluation means weighing the actual strength of competing arguments — not simply listing them — and being explicit about which considerations the writer finds most persuasive and why.

#### L6-Dissertation-6 Referencing and academic integrity
All primary and secondary sources must be accurately cited, normally using **OSCOLA**, with a consistent footnote style and a full bibliography. Every idea, argument or form of words taken from another source — whether directly quoted or paraphrased — must be attributed; failing to do so, even unintentionally, is **plagiarism** and a serious academic integrity breach. Direct quotation should be used sparingly and only where the original wording itself matters (for example, quoting a precise statutory phrase or a judge's exact reasoning); most engagement with sources should be in the writer's own words, demonstrating genuine understanding rather than assembled quotation.
