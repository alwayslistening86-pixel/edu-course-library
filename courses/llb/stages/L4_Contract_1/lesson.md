# L4_Contract_1 - Lesson: Contract Law I — Formation: offer, acceptance, consideration and intention

## Goal
The learner identifies whether a binding contract has formed by working through offer, acceptance, consideration, estoppel and intention in IRAC form.

## Syllabus items taught here
- L4-Contract-1 - Offer and invitation to treat: Distinguish an offer from an invitation to treat and identify when an offer exists on the facts
- L4-Contract-2 - Acceptance, revocation and the postal rule: Apply the mirror-image rule, communication requirement and postal rule to decide whether acceptance occurred
- L4-Contract-3 - Consideration: Identify sufficient consideration and apply the rules on past consideration and part-payment of debts
- L4-Contract-4 - Promissory estoppel: Explain when equity will prevent a promisor going back on a promise despite no consideration
- L4-Contract-5 - Intention to create legal relations: Apply the commercial and domestic/social presumptions and explain how each is rebutted

## How to teach this
Open with the scenario: Maya posts on a local selling group "Selling my bike, £150, first come first served." Tom replies "I'll take it!" Maya doesn't reply — she's busy — and two days later sells the bike to someone who turns up in person with cash. Tom says they had a deal. Ask the learner to work through the building blocks one at a time before answering, and to use IRAC (Issue, Rule, Application, Conclusion) explicitly rather than narrating the law in general terms.

#### L4-Contract-1 Offer and invitation to treat
An **offer** is a clear statement of terms that the offeror is willing to be bound by if accepted, made with the intention that acceptance will conclude the bargain. An **invitation to treat** merely invites others to make offers, and is not itself capable of acceptance: a shop display is only an invitation to treat (*Pharmaceutical Society of Great Britain v Boots Cash Chemists* [1953] 1 QB 401), as is a newspaper advertisement (*Partridge v Crittenden* [1968] 1 WLR 1204) and, generally, an invitation to tender. The distinction turns on the precision of the terms and the surrounding context: Maya's post reads like an offer because it states a fixed price and a fixed allocation rule ("first come first served"), but genuinely ambiguous wording should be flagged as arguable rather than assumed.

#### L4-Contract-2 Acceptance, revocation and the postal rule
**Acceptance** must be a mirror-image, unconditional agreement to the offer's exact terms; a response that varies any term is a **counter-offer**, which destroys the original offer (*Hyde v Wrench* (1840) 3 Beav 334). Acceptance must generally be **communicated** to the offeror — silence is not acceptance (*Felthouse v Bindley* (1862) 11 CB (NS) 869) — though the **postal rule** makes acceptance effective on posting, not receipt, where post is a contemplated method of communication (*Adams v Lindsell* (1818) 1 B & Ald 681). An offer may be **revoked** at any time before acceptance provided revocation is actually communicated to the offeree, even via a reliable third party (*Byrne v Van Tienhoven* (1880) 5 CPD 344; *Dickinson v Dodds* (1876) 2 Ch D 463).

#### L4-Contract-3 Consideration
Each party must give **consideration** — something of value in the eyes of the law — for a promise to be enforceable. Consideration need not be *adequate* (a fair market price) but must be *sufficient*: even something of trivial economic worth counts if the promisor asked for it (*Chappell & Co Ltd v Nestlé Co Ltd* [1960] AC 87, chocolate bar wrappers). **Past consideration is no consideration**: an act done before a promise is made cannot support that later promise (*Re McArdle* [1951] Ch 669), subject to a narrow exception where the act was done at the promisor's request with an understanding of later payment (*Pao On v Lau Yiu Long* [1980] AC 614). Part-payment of a debt does not discharge the whole debt as a matter of consideration (*Foakes v Beer* (1884) 9 App Cas 605), a rule increasingly qualified by practical-benefit reasoning in *Williams v Roffey Bros* [1991] 1 QB 1 for the analogous rule on existing contractual duties.

#### L4-Contract-4 Promissory estoppel
Where there is no fresh consideration, equity may still prevent a promisor going back on a clear promise not to enforce their strict legal rights, if the promisee has relied on it and it would be inequitable to allow resiling (*Central London Property Trust Ltd v High Trees House Ltd* [1947] KB 130). Promissory estoppel is a shield, not a sword — it cannot itself create a new cause of action (*Combe v Combe* [1951] 2 KB 215) — and is generally suspensory rather than extinctive of the original right, unless it would be inequitable for the promisor to revert.

#### L4-Contract-5 Intention to create legal relations
Courts presume commercial agreements are intended to be legally binding, and presume domestic or social agreements are not (*Balfour v Balfour* [1919] 2 KB 571) — though both presumptions are rebuttable with clear evidence (*Merritt v Merritt* [1970] 1 WLR 1211, a separated couple's written agreement was held binding). A casual online exchange between strangers about a sale for money sits closer to the commercial end despite its informality, because money and a specific item are changing hands between people with no family relationship.
