# S10_Electrolysis - Lesson: Electrolysis of molten and aqueous substances (required practical 3)

## How to teach this
Ask: 'Why does an ionic solid not conduct, but the same substance melted does? And what happens to the ions when a current runs?'

#### 4.4.3.1 The process of electrolysis
Electrolysis uses an electric current to decompose an ionic compound when molten or dissolved (the electrolyte). Positive ions (cations) move to the negative electrode (cathode); negative ions (anions) move to the positive electrode (anode). At the electrodes the ions gain or lose electrons and are discharged as elements.

#### 4.4.3.2 Electrolysis of molten ionic compounds
When a simple ionic compound is molten, the metal forms at the cathode and the non-metal at the anode. Molten lead bromide gives lead at the cathode and bromine at the anode. The ions are free to move in the liquid.

#### 4.4.3.3 Using electrolysis to extract metals
Metals more reactive than carbon, such as aluminium, are extracted by electrolysis of molten compounds. Aluminium oxide is dissolved in molten cryolite to lower the operating temperature and save energy. Aluminium forms at the cathode; oxygen forms at the carbon anodes, which react with oxygen to form carbon dioxide and must be replaced regularly. Large amounts of electricity make the process costly.

#### 4.4.3.4 Electrolysis of aqueous solutions
Aqueous solutions contain H+ and OH- from water as well as the ions from the compound. At the cathode, hydrogen is produced if the metal is more reactive than hydrogen; otherwise the metal forms. At the anode, oxygen is produced unless a halide ion is present, in which case the halogen forms. Sodium chloride solution gives hydrogen, chlorine and sodium hydroxide solution; copper sulfate solution with inert electrodes gives copper and oxygen.

#### 4.4.3.5 Representation of reactions at electrodes as half equations
Half equations show electrons: at the cathode positive ions gain electrons (reduction), e.g. Al3+ + 3e- gives Al; 2H+ + 2e- gives H2. At the anode negative ions lose electrons (oxidation), e.g. 2Cl- gives Cl2 + 2e-; 2O2- gives O2 + 4e-. Higher tier: 4OH- gives O2 + 2H2O + 4e-.

#### RP3 Required practical 3: electrolysis of aqueous solutions
Investigate what happens when aqueous solutions are electrolysed with inert (graphite) electrodes. Form a hypothesis for predicted products for solutions such as copper sulfate, sodium chloride and dilute sulfuric acid. Set up a beaker with electrodes connected to a power supply, collect gases in inverted tubes if produced, and test them (lit splint for hydrogen, glowing splint for oxygen, damp litmus for chlorine). Note any coating on the electrode.
