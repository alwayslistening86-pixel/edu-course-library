## How to teach this
Ask: 'The label on a bottle of water says pure. A chemist would disagree. Why?' Then run an ink chromatogram.

#### 4.8.1.1 Pure substances
In chemistry a pure substance is a single element or compound, not mixed with any other substance. Pure elements and compounds melt and boil at specific, sharp temperatures; mixtures melt over a range and at different temperatures. In everyday language 'pure' often means a substance with nothing added (such as pure milk), which is a mixture in chemistry.

#### 4.8.1.2 Formulations
A formulation is a mixture designed as a useful product, made by mixing components in carefully measured quantities so the product has the required properties. Examples include fuels, cleaning agents, paints, medicines, alloys, fertilisers and foods. Each component has a purpose, for example a pigment, a binder and a solvent in paint.

#### 4.8.1.3 Chromatography
Chromatography separates mixtures and helps identify substances. It has a stationary phase (such as paper) and a mobile phase (the solvent). Substances separate by their different solubilities in the solvent and different attraction to the paper. A pure compound gives one spot; a mixture gives several. Rf = distance moved by substance divided by distance moved by solvent, always between 0 and 1. Compare Rf values with known references.

#### 4.8.2.1 Test for hydrogen
Hold a lit splint at the open end of a test tube of the gas: hydrogen burns rapidly with a squeaky pop, because it combines explosively with oxygen from the air to form water. A louder pop means a purer, larger sample of hydrogen was collected in the tube.

#### 4.8.2.2 Test for oxygen
Insert a glowing splint into a test tube of the gas: oxygen relights the glowing splint, because the higher concentration of oxygen makes the smouldering wood burn again. This distinguishes oxygen from air, in which a glowing splint just continues to glow.

#### 4.8.2.3 Test for carbon dioxide
Bubble the gas through limewater (calcium hydroxide solution): carbon dioxide turns limewater milky (cloudy), because it reacts to form insoluble calcium carbonate: CO2 + Ca(OH)2 gives CaCO3 + H2O. Bubbling too much gas through for too long makes the milkiness disappear again.

#### 4.8.2.4 Test for chlorine
Hold damp blue litmus paper in the gas: chlorine turns it red briefly then bleaches it white. Chlorine is toxic, so the test must be done in a fume cupboard.

#### RP6 Required practical 6: paper chromatography and Rf values
Draw a pencil baseline about 1 cm from the bottom of chromatography paper, place spots of the samples and known references, stand the paper in a beaker with solvent below the baseline, and cover. Remove when the solvent front is near the top and mark it in pencil. Measure the distance travelled by each spot and by the solvent and calculate Rf. Use pencil because ink would dissolve.
