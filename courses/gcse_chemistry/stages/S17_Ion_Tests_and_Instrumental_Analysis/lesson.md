# S17_Ion_Tests_and_Instrumental_Analysis - Lesson: Flame tests, precipitation tests, instrumental methods (required practical 7)

## Goal
The learner identifies metal and non-metal ions using flame tests and chemical tests, and describes the advantages of instrumental methods including flame emission spectroscopy.

## How to teach this
Present an unlabelled white solid and ask: 'What tests would tell us which metal and which negative ion it contains, and in what order?'

#### 4.8.3.1 Flame tests
Dip a clean nichrome wire in the sample and hold it in a blue Bunsen flame. Lithium gives a crimson flame, sodium yellow, potassium lilac, calcium orange-red and copper green. If a sample has a mixture of ions, the flame colours can be masked, so flame tests work best with a single metal ion.

#### 4.8.3.2 Metal hydroxides
Add sodium hydroxide solution to a solution of the metal ion. Aluminium, calcium and magnesium ions give white precipitates; aluminium's precipitate redissolves in excess sodium hydroxide, calcium's and magnesium's do not. Copper(II) gives a blue precipitate, iron(II) a green precipitate and iron(III) a brown precipitate. Write ionic equations such as Cu2+ + 2OH- gives Cu(OH)2.

#### 4.8.3.3 Carbonates
Add dilute acid to the substance: a carbonate gives effervescence (fizzing), and the gas turns limewater milky, showing carbon dioxide: CaCO3 + 2HCl gives CaCl2 + H2O + CO2. The same test works for any metal carbonate, such as sodium carbonate or copper carbonate.

#### 4.8.3.4 Halides
Add dilute nitric acid, then silver nitrate solution. A chloride gives a white precipitate of silver chloride, a bromide gives a cream precipitate, and an iodide a yellow precipitate. Nitric acid removes carbonates that could give a false result.

#### 4.8.3.5 Sulfates
Add dilute hydrochloric acid, then barium chloride solution. A sulfate gives a white precipitate of barium sulfate: Ba2+ + SO4 2- gives BaSO4. Hydrochloric acid removes carbonates that would also form white barium carbonate.

#### 4.8.3.6 Instrumental methods
Elements and compounds can be detected by machines. These methods are accurate, sensitive (detect very small amounts), rapid, and work with small samples, unlike many chemical tests, which are slower and less precise. Example: identifying pollutants in water or forensic analysis.

#### 4.8.3.7 Flame emission spectroscopy
A sample is put into a flame and the light given out passes through a spectroscope, producing a line spectrum. Each metal ion produces a characteristic set of lines, compared with reference spectra, and the intensity of the lines gives the concentration. It can identify several ions in a mixture, for example copper and sodium in one sample, which flame tests cannot.

#### RP7 Required practical 7: identifying ions in unknown ionic compounds
Use chemical tests to identify the ions in unknown single ionic compounds. Carry out a flame test for the metal, sodium hydroxide test for the metal ion, acid and limewater for carbonate, nitric acid and silver nitrate for halide, and hydrochloric acid and barium chloride for sulfate. Record all observations in a results table and combine them to name the compound. Safety: goggles, careful with acid and toxic barium compounds.
