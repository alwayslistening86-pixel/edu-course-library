# S11_Energy_Changes_and_Cells - Lesson: Exothermic and endothermic reactions, reaction profiles, bond energies, cells and fuel cells (required practical 4)

## Goal
The learner distinguishes exothermic and endothermic reactions, draws reaction profiles, calculates energy changes from bond energies, and explains chemical cells and fuel cells.

## How to teach this
Ask: 'Why does a hand warmer get hot and a sports injury pack get cold when both are just chemicals reacting?'

#### 4.5.1.1 Energy transfer during exothermic and endothermic reactions
Energy is conserved. An exothermic reaction transfers energy to the surroundings, so the temperature of the surroundings increases: combustion, oxidation reactions and neutralisation; used in hand warmers and self-heating cans. An endothermic reaction takes in energy from the surroundings, so the temperature falls: thermal decomposition and the reaction of citric acid with sodium hydrogencarbonate; used in some sports injury packs.

#### 4.5.1.2 Reaction profiles
A reaction profile shows the relative energies of reactants and products, with a hump for the activation energy (the minimum energy needed for particles to react). For an exothermic reaction the products are lower in energy than the reactants; for an endothermic reaction the products are higher. The overall energy change is the difference between products and reactants.

#### 4.5.1.3 The energy change of reactions (Higher tier)
Bond breaking is endothermic and bond making is exothermic. The overall energy change = energy needed to break bonds in reactants minus energy released making bonds in products. For H2 + Cl2 gives 2HCl with bond energies H-H 436, Cl-Cl 243 and H-Cl 432 kJ/mol: 436 + 243 = 679 in; 2 x 432 = 864 out; change = -185 kJ/mol, so exothermic.

#### 4.5.2.1 Cells and batteries
A simple cell has two different metals (electrodes) in an electrolyte; a chemical reaction pushes electrons round the circuit, giving a voltage. The voltage depends on the difference in reactivity of the metals and the electrolyte used. A battery is two or more cells in series. Non-rechargeable (alkaline) batteries stop working when the reactants are used up; rechargeable batteries reverse the reaction when connected to a charger.

#### 4.5.2.2 Fuel cells
A fuel cell is supplied with a fuel (hydrogen) and oxygen, and produces water, with the overall reaction 2H2 + O2 gives 2H2O. It produces a voltage continuously while supplied. Higher tier half equations (alkaline): at the anode 2H2 + 4OH- gives 4H2O + 4e-; at the cathode O2 + 2H2O + 4e- gives 4OH-. Compared with rechargeable cells, fuel cells produce only water and do not need charging, but hydrogen is hard to store and produce.

#### RP4 Required practical 4: variables affecting temperature changes in reacting solutions
Use a polystyrene cup with a lid in a beaker for insulation, measure a fixed volume of one reactant, record its starting temperature, add the second reactant (or a fixed mass of metal or carbonate) and stir, and record the maximum or final temperature. Investigate one variable, such as concentration, and keep others constant. The temperature rise is the dependent variable. Repeat for reliability.
