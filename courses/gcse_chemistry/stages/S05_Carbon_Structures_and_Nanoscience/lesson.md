# S05_Carbon_Structures_and_Nanoscience - Lesson: Diamond, graphite, graphene, fullerenes and nanoparticles

## Goal
The learner links the structures of the forms of carbon to their uses and describes the properties and uses of nanoparticles.

## Syllabus items taught here
- 4.2.3.1 - Diamond: learn and apply: diamond
- 4.2.3.2 - Graphite: learn and apply: graphite
- 4.2.3.3 - Graphene and fullerenes: learn and apply: graphene and fullerenes
- 4.2.4.1 - Sizes of particles and their properties: learn and apply: sizes of particles and their properties
- 4.2.4.2 - Uses of nanoparticles: learn and apply: uses of nanoparticles

## How to teach this
Ask: 'Diamond and the pencil lead are both pure carbon. Why is one the hardest natural material and the other soft enough to write?' Let structure explain.

#### 4.2.3.1 Diamond
In diamond each carbon atom forms four covalent bonds with other carbon atoms in a giant covalent structure. This makes diamond very hard with a very high melting point, and it does not conduct electricity because there are no free electrons. It is used in cutting tools and jewellery.

#### 4.2.3.2 Graphite
In graphite each carbon atom forms three covalent bonds, creating hexagonal layers with no covalent bonds between the layers; the layers can slide, so graphite is soft and slippery (a lubricant). One electron per carbon is delocalised, so graphite conducts electricity, which is why it is used for electrodes. It has a high melting point.

#### 4.2.3.3 Graphene and fullerenes
Graphene is a single layer of graphite, one atom thick, very strong and a good conductor, useful in electronics and composites. Fullerenes are hollow molecules of carbon atoms forming closed tubes or cages: Buckminsterfullerene (C60) is a sphere of 60 carbon atoms. Carbon nanotubes are cylindrical fullerenes with high tensile strength and electrical conductivity, used in nanotechnology, electronics and reinforcing materials such as tennis rackets.

#### 4.2.4.1 Sizes of particles and their properties
Nanoscience studies structures 1 to 100 nanometres in size (a few hundred atoms). Coarse particles (dust) are 10 000 to 2500 nm, fine particles 100 to 2500 nm, nanoparticles 1 to 100 nm. As the side of a cube decreases by a factor of 10, the surface area to volume ratio increases by a factor of 10. Nanoparticles therefore have different properties from bulk materials, and are used at smaller quantities.

#### 4.2.4.2 Uses of nanoparticles
Uses include medicine (drug delivery), electronics, cosmetics and sun creams (better coverage, no white marks), deodorants, and as catalysts because of their large surface area. New nanomaterials show promise, but their effect on health and the environment is not fully known, so risks and benefits must be weighed.
