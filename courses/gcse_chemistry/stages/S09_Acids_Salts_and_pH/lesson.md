#### 4.4.2.1 Reactions of acids with metals
Acid + metal gives a salt + hydrogen. Magnesium + hydrochloric acid gives magnesium chloride + hydrogen. Salts from hydrochloric acid are chlorides, from sulfuric acid sulfates, from nitric acid nitrates. Higher tier: ionic equation Mg + 2H+ gives Mg2+ + H2, in which magnesium is oxidised and hydrogen ions are reduced.

#### 4.4.2.2 Neutralisation of acids and salt production
Acids react with bases (metal oxides, hydroxides, carbonates) to give salts. Acid + metal oxide gives salt + water; acid + metal hydroxide gives salt + water; acid + metal carbonate gives salt + water + carbon dioxide. Hydrochloric acid + sodium hydroxide gives sodium chloride + water. Higher tier: in neutralisation, H+ + OH- gives H2O.

#### 4.4.2.3 Soluble salts
A soluble salt is made by reacting an acid with an insoluble base: add the solid to warm acid until no more reacts (excess), filter off the excess, then heat to evaporate some water and leave to crystallise. Soluble salts can also be made from an acid plus alkali, using a titration to find the exact volume and then crystallising.

#### 4.4.2.4 The pH scale and neutralisation
The pH scale runs from 0 (strongly acidic) to 14 (strongly alkaline), with 7 neutral. Acids form H+ ions in aqueous solution; alkalis form OH- ions. Indicators such as litmus, methyl orange and universal indicator show whether a solution is acid or alkali; a pH probe gives a numerical value. In neutralisation, H+ and OH- form water.

#### 4.4.2.5 Titrations
A titration finds the volume of one solution needed to neutralise a known volume of another. Use a pipette to measure the alkali into a conical flask, add indicator, add acid from a burette drop by drop near the end point, and record the volume. Repeat until concordant results (within 0.10 cm3) and take the mean. Higher tier: calculate concentration from moles = concentration x volume and the equation ratio.

#### 4.4.2.6 Strong and weak acids (Higher tier)
A strong acid (hydrochloric, nitric, sulfuric) is completely ionised in aqueous solution; a weak acid (ethanoic, citric, carbonic) is only partly ionised, an equilibrium. Strength is not the same as concentration: a dilute strong acid and a concentrated weak acid can both exist. For each drop of one pH unit, the hydrogen ion concentration is 10 times higher.

#### RP1 Required practical 1: preparation of a pure, dry sample of a soluble salt
React dilute sulfuric acid with excess copper(II) oxide (warm using a Bunsen burner and water bath), filter off the excess, heat the filtrate to evaporate about half the water, leave to crystallise, filter the crystals and dry with filter paper. Safety: acid is an irritant, take care with hot solutions; use goggles.

#### RP2 Required practical 2: titration of a strong acid and a strong alkali
Determine the reacting volumes of a strong acid and a strong alkali by titration. Rinse the burette with acid, pipette exactly 25.0 cm3 of alkali with indicator into a flask on a white tile, add acid dropwise near the end point, and read the meniscus at eye level. Do a rough titration first, then accurate ones until concordant.
