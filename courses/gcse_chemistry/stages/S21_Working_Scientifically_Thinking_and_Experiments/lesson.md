# S21_Working_Scientifically_Thinking_and_Experiments - Lesson: Working scientifically: scientific thinking and experimental skills

## Goal

## How to teach this
Use a practical the learner has already done and ask: 'What would you change if you had to convince a sceptic?' Draw the answers out before naming the skill.

#### WS1.1 Development of scientific methods and theories
Scientific theories develop over time as new evidence appears: for example, the plum pudding model gave way to the nuclear model as new experimental evidence appeared, and the periodic table was improved as new elements were found. Ideas are tested, some are rejected, and new methods and technologies enable new discoveries.

#### WS1.2 Use of models
Models simplify reality to explain observations and make predictions: the particle model of matter, dot-and-cross diagrams and ball-and-stick models of molecules are examples. Every model has limits, so it is important to say what a model does not show and where it stops working.

#### WS1.3 Power and limitations of science and ethical issues
Science can answer questions that can be tested by evidence, but cannot decide values questions such as whether a chemical process that makes a useful product but a hazardous waste should be used. Ethical, economic, social and environmental issues affect what is done with science, so the learner should present arguments on more than one side.

#### WS1.4 Applications of science and implications
The learner explains applications, for example using fertilisers, nanoparticles or fuel cells, and weighs their benefits against implications including cost, safety, fairness and effects on the environment and society, giving a balanced judgement.

#### WS1.5 Risks in practical science and society
A hazard is something that could cause harm; risk is the likelihood and severity of that harm. A practical risk assessment names a hazard, who is at risk and how to reduce it, such as dilute acid, a Bunsen burner and hot solutions, wearing goggles and using a water bath. In society, risk is judged using evidence and the way people perceive the risk.

#### WS1.6 Peer review and communicating results
New results are checked by other scientists before publication (peer review) and shared through journals and conferences. Other scientists can then repeat the work to see if they get the same results, and reproducibility strengthens confidence. Media reports may be unreliable, so the source and the evidence should be evaluated.

#### WS2.1 Using theories to develop hypotheses
A hypothesis is a testable prediction based on a theory: 'If temperature increases, then the rate of reaction between magnesium and acid increases, because more particles have enough energy to react.' A good hypothesis states the expected relationship between the independent and dependent variable, with a scientific reason.

#### WS2.2 Planning experiments
Identify the independent variable (changed), the dependent variable (measured) and control variables (kept constant). Include a control experiment where appropriate, choose a suitable range and interval for the independent variable, decide how many repeats, and state how results will be recorded and analysed.

#### WS2.3 Selecting apparatus and techniques
Match apparatus to purpose: a measuring cylinder or pipette for volumes, a balance for mass, a stopwatch, a thermometer, a gas syringe and a burette. Choose apparatus with a suitable resolution (smallest division) for the measurement and the right size for the quantity being measured.

#### WS2.4 Carrying out experiments safely and accurately
Follow the method exactly, use apparatus correctly (read a meniscus at eye level, zero the balance), wear the right protective equipment, and keep the working area tidy. Carry out the risk assessment first and dispose of chemicals and waste correctly.

#### WS2.5 Sampling techniques
A sample must be representative and large enough. For water analysis or soil testing, take samples from several places and mix or test each to avoid a biased result. Larger samples reduce the effect of chance and random error.

#### WS2.6 Making and recording observations and measurements
Record data in a table with clear headings and units in the heading, not in the body. Give readings to the same number of decimal places consistent with the apparatus resolution, and record repeats. Careful observations such as colour change, bubbling and drawings are recorded in words.

#### WS2.7 Evaluating methods and suggesting improvements
Evaluate by looking for sources of random error (which give scatter) and systematic error (a consistent shift, for example from a badly calibrated instrument). Suggest specific improvements: use a more precise instrument, control temperature with a water bath, increase the number of repeats or widen the range of values.
