## How to teach this
Ask: 'Petrol, diesel and bitumen all come from the same barrel of crude oil. What must differ between them to let you separate them by heating?'

#### 4.7.1.1 Crude oil, hydrocarbons and alkanes
Crude oil is a finite resource formed over millions of years from the remains of ancient biomass, mainly plankton, buried in mud. It is a mixture of many hydrocarbons: compounds of hydrogen and carbon only, most of them alkanes (general formula CnH2n+2). The first four are methane CH4, ethane C2H6, propane C3H8 and butane C4H10. Alkanes are saturated: all single bonds.

#### 4.7.1.2 Fractional distillation and petrochemicals
Crude oil is heated to vaporise and enters a fractionating column, hot at the bottom and cooler at the top. Molecules condense where the temperature falls below their boiling point, so short chains come off at the top and long chains at the bottom. Fractions include LPG, petrol, kerosene, diesel, fuel oil and bitumen. They are used as fuels, and as feedstock for the petrochemical industry to make solvents, lubricants, polymers and detergents.

#### 4.7.1.3 Properties of hydrocarbons
As chain length increases, boiling point rises, viscosity increases (they flow less easily) and flammability decreases, because larger molecules have stronger intermolecular forces. Hydrocarbons burn in plenty of air (complete combustion): carbon dioxide and water, releasing energy, for example CH4 + 2O2 gives CO2 + 2H2O. In limited oxygen, incomplete combustion gives carbon monoxide or carbon (soot).

#### 4.7.1.4 Cracking and alkenes
Long-chain hydrocarbons can be broken down into more useful shorter ones by cracking, because there is more demand for shorter chain fuels. Catalytic cracking uses a hot catalyst (about 550 degrees Celsius); steam cracking uses steam at high temperature. Products are shorter alkanes and alkenes; alkenes are used to make polymers and as starting materials. Balance the equation: C10H22 gives C8H18 + C2H4.
