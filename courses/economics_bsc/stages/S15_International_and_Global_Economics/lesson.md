# S15_International_and_Global_Economics - Lesson: International and global economics

## Goal
The learner derives comparative advantage and the gains from trade, applies the Heckscher-Ohlin model of factor endowments, calculates the welfare/deadweight-loss effects of a tariff, and evaluates globalisation and development, including the Human Development Index (Economics for a Changing World).

## How to teach this
Ask how a country can gain from trading with a partner that is more productive at making everything. Teach each topic by building on what GCSE and A-level Economics already gave the learner: name the A-level idea it extends, then show the calculus/formal derivation or statistical technique that intermediate/degree-level economics adds. Work every numerical example with the learner predicting a step before it is shown; every figure in this course was computed with Python when the course was built. For current economic data (interest rates, inflation, growth, exchange rates), check the latest official sources (ONS, Bank of England, IMF) rather than relying on any figure used here as illustration.

#### DD321-1 Comparative advantage and gains from trade
Comparative advantage (Ricardo): a country should specialise in producing the good for which its opportunity cost is lowest, even if another country is more efficient (has absolute advantage) at producing everything. *Example:* Country A can produce either 10 units of cloth or 5 units of wine per worker-day; Country B can produce either 6 units of cloth or 2 units of wine (A is more productive at both goods in absolute terms). A's opportunity cost of 1 wine is 10/5 = 2 units of cloth; B's is 6/2 = 3 units of cloth -- A has the lower opportunity cost of wine (comparative advantage in wine), while B, despite being less productive at everything, has the lower opportunity cost of cloth (5/10=0.5 wine for A vs 2/6=0.33 wine for B) and so comparative advantage in cloth. Both countries gain if A specialises in wine and B in cloth and they trade at a price between their two opportunity costs.

#### DD321-2 The Heckscher-Ohlin model
The Heckscher-Ohlin model explains the pattern of comparative advantage through factor endowments: a country will tend to have a comparative advantage in, and export, the good that uses intensively the factor of production it holds in relative abundance (e.g. a labour-abundant country exports labour-intensive goods; a capital-abundant country exports capital-intensive goods). This extends Ricardo's model (which leaves the source of differing opportunity costs unexplained) by grounding comparative advantage in differences in relative factor supplies between countries.

#### DD321-3 Tariffs and deadweight loss
A tariff (a tax on imports) raises the domestic price above the world price, creating deadweight loss: a production distortion (domestic firms expand output beyond the efficient level, given world prices) and a consumption distortion (consumers buy less than the efficient quantity), plus tariff revenue to the government (not a welfare loss, a transfer) and a fall in consumer surplus that is partly captured by domestic producers as extra producer surplus. *Example:* world price £10, domestic demand at £10 is 500 units, domestic supply 200 units (imports 300); a £2 tariff raises the price to £12, at which domestic supply rises to 260 and domestic demand falls to 440 (imports fall to 180); the two triangular deadweight-loss areas are roughly 0.5x(260-200)x2 = £60 (production distortion) plus 0.5x(500-440)x2 = £60 (consumption distortion), total deadweight loss approximately £120.

#### DD321-4 Globalisation and global value chains
Globalisation -- the growing economic interdependence of countries through trade, capital flows, multinational production and technology -- has been driven by falling transport/communication costs and trade liberalisation. Multinational firms increasingly organise production across global value chains, locating each stage of production (design, components, assembly) where it is most cost-effective, which raises efficiency but also creates new policy issues: trade in intermediate goods across many borders, tax base erosion via profit-shifting between jurisdictions, and vulnerability to supply-chain disruption (e.g. exposed during the Covid-19 pandemic).

#### DD321-5 Growth vs development and the Human Development Index
Growth (a rise in output/income) is not the same as development (a broader improvement in wellbeing, capabilities and living standards). The Human Development Index (HDI) combines income (GNI per capita, log-transformed to reflect diminishing returns to income for wellbeing), health (life expectancy at birth) and education (expected and mean years of schooling) into a single composite index, explicitly moving beyond GDP/GNI alone as a measure of a country's progress. Structural transformation -- the shift of an economy's employment and output from agriculture toward manufacturing and then services as it develops -- is a common empirical pattern, though its pace and sequencing vary considerably across countries.
