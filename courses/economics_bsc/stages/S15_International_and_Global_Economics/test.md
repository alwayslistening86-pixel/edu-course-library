## Test items
1. Country P can produce 12 units of textiles or 6 units of machinery per day; Country Q can produce 4 units of textiles or 4 units of machinery per day. Determine which country has comparative advantage in which good, and state a range of exchange rates (textiles per unit of machinery) at which both would gain from trade. [5 marks]
2. Explain, using a diagram description, why a tariff creates deadweight loss even though it raises tariff revenue for the government. [4 marks]
3. Explain, using the Heckscher-Ohlin model, why a labour-abundant developing country and a capital-abundant developed country might both gain from specialising according to comparative advantage. [4 marks]
4. Explain why the Human Development Index was created as an alternative to using GDP/GNI per capita alone to measure a country's progress. [3 marks]

## Answer key (for the tutor only)
1. [5] B1 comparative advantage: a country should specialise in the good for which its own opportunity cost is lower; M1 P's opp. cost of 1 machinery = 12/6 = 2 textiles; Q's opp. cost of 1 machinery = 4/4 = 1 textile; A1 Q has the lower opportunity cost of machinery (1 < 2), so Q has comparative advantage in machinery, and P has comparative advantage in textiles; M1 both gain from trade at any exchange rate strictly between the two machinery opportunity costs; A1 i.e. between 1 and 2 textiles per unit of machinery.
2. [4] B1 tariff revenue is a transfer from consumers to the government, not a loss to society as a whole; B1 but the tariff also causes domestic producers to expand output beyond the level that reflects true world (opportunity) cost -- a production-side inefficiency; B1 and causes domestic consumers to buy less than they would at the true world price -- a consumption-side loss of surplus with no offsetting gain to anyone; B1 these two triangular areas (production and consumption distortions) are real losses to society that the transferred tariff revenue does not compensate for -- the net welfare effect is negative.
3. [4] B1 Heckscher-Ohlin predicts a country has comparative advantage in the good that intensively uses its relatively abundant factor; B1 the labour-abundant country has a comparative advantage in labour-intensive goods (and a relatively low opportunity cost of producing them); B1 the capital-abundant country has a comparative advantage in capital-intensive goods; B1 specialising accordingly and trading lets each country consume beyond what its own production possibility frontier alone would allow, generating mutual gains from trade.
4. [3] B1 GDP/GNI per capita captures only income, but wellbeing depends on more than income alone (e.g. health, education); B1 the HDI combines income with life expectancy and education measures into one composite index; B1 so two countries with similar income can be ranked differently if they differ in health/education outcomes, better reflecting broader human development than income alone.
