# S16_Economic_Policy_Analysis_Capstone - Lesson: Economic policy analysis (capstone)

## Goal
The learner evaluates the monetary policy transmission mechanism and fiscal policy (automatic stabilisers, discretionary policy, multipliers, debt sustainability) in applied practice, appraises a public project using cost-benefit analysis and discounting, applies behavioural/pluralist critiques to policy design, and synthesises theory, evidence and evaluation into a written policy briefing (Economics in Practice, the degree's capstone applied strand).

## How to teach this
Ask what a central bank actually has to believe about how quickly and completely an interest-rate change passes through to spending before it changes rates. Teach each topic by building on what GCSE and A-level Economics already gave the learner: name the A-level idea it extends, then show the calculus/formal derivation or statistical technique that intermediate/degree-level economics adds. Work every numerical example with the learner predicting a step before it is shown; every figure in this course was computed with Python when the course was built. For current economic data (interest rates, inflation, growth, exchange rates), check the latest official sources (ONS, Bank of England, IMF) rather than relying on any figure used here as illustration.

#### DD226-1 The monetary policy transmission mechanism
The monetary policy transmission mechanism: a change in the central bank's policy rate affects market interest rates, asset prices (bonds, equities, house prices) and the exchange rate, which together affect consumption and investment (via the cost of borrowing and wealth effects) and net exports (via the exchange-rate channel from S10), ultimately affecting aggregate demand, output and inflation -- but with long, variable and uncertain lags (often cited as 18 months to 2 years to peak effect on inflation), which is why central banks act pre-emptively based on inflation forecasts rather than only current data, and why policy evaluation is inherently uncertain in real time.

#### DD226-2 Automatic stabilisers, discretionary policy and debt sustainability
Automatic stabilisers (progressive taxes, unemployment benefits) reduce the swings of the business cycle without any new policy decision: in a downturn, tax revenue falls and benefit spending rises automatically, cushioning the fall in disposable income and hence spending. Discretionary fiscal policy is a deliberate change in tax/spending (e.g. a stimulus package), subject to implementation lags (deciding, legislating, spending) that can mean it arrives after the downturn has already turned around -- a key practical critique of discretionary fiscal fine-tuning. Debt sustainability depends on the relationship between the interest rate on government debt (r) and the economy's growth rate (g): if r < g, a given primary deficit is more easily sustained (debt-to-GDP can stabilise or fall even with persistent, moderate deficits) than if r > g, other things equal.

#### DD226-3 Cost-benefit analysis and discounting
Cost-benefit analysis appraises a public project by comparing the present value of its full stream of costs and benefits (including non-market/social costs and benefits, not just direct financial ones) over its life. Future amounts are converted to present value by discounting: PV = FV/(1+r)^t. *Example:* a project promises a £50m social benefit in 5 years; at a discount rate of 3.5%, its present value is £50m/(1.035)^5 = £42.1m. Net present value (NPV) = sum of discounted benefits - sum of discounted costs; a project is worth undertaking (on efficiency grounds alone) if NPV > 0. The choice of discount rate matters greatly for long-horizon projects (e.g. climate policy, infrastructure), since a higher rate weights near-term costs/benefits much more heavily than distant ones.

#### DD226-4 Behavioural and pluralist critiques of policy design
Behavioural insights (S02 of Block 1) and pluralist perspectives (S14) apply directly to policy design in practice: a 'nudge unit' redesigning default pension enrolment, or simplifying benefit claim forms to reduce the cognitive/administrative burden that causes eligible households not to claim, are applied examples of choice architecture informing real government policy. Evaluating such policies requires being explicit about the normative framework used (e.g. libertarian paternalism's claim to preserve freedom of choice while improving outcomes is itself contested, since defaults are rarely neutral and shape behaviour by design) -- an evaluative, not purely technical, judgement.

#### DD226-5 Writing an integrative policy briefing
A policy briefing synthesises economic theory, empirical evidence and a reasoned evaluation into a document usable by a non-specialist decision-maker: it states the problem, the relevant theoretical mechanism(s), the available evidence (including its quality and limitations), the policy options with their costs/benefits/distributional effects, and a reasoned recommendation that acknowledges genuine uncertainty and trade-offs rather than overstating confidence -- the integrative capstone skill this degree builds toward, combining every earlier block (microeconomic incentives, macroeconomic context, quantitative evidence, and awareness that reasonable economists can disagree).
