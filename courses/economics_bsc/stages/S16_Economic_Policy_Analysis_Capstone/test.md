## Test items
1. Explain why monetary policy is described as operating with 'long, variable and uncertain lags', and one implication this has for how a central bank should set policy. [4 marks]
2. A flood-defence project costs £30m now and yields a social benefit of £45m in 8 years. At a discount rate of 3%, calculate the NPV and state whether the project should proceed on efficiency grounds. [4 marks]
3. Explain why r < g (interest rate below growth rate) makes a given government primary deficit easier to sustain than r > g, other things equal. [4 marks]
4. Using an example, explain why a policy designed using behavioural 'nudge' principles (e.g. automatic pension enrolment) still requires an explicit evaluative judgement, not just a technical one. [3 marks]

## Answer key (for the tutor only)
1. [4] B1 an interest-rate change takes time to pass through market rates, asset prices and the exchange rate into spending decisions, and then into output and inflation -- often well over a year to reach full effect; B1 'variable' means the size and speed of this pass-through differs across time and circumstances, so past experience is only a rough guide; B1 'uncertain' means the central bank cannot know the exact effect in advance; B1 implication: the bank must act pre-emptively on forecasts of future inflation rather than waiting for current inflation data, since acting only on current data risks acting too late.
2. [4] M1 PV of benefit = 45/(1.03)^8; A1 = £35.52m; M1 NPV = 35.52 - 30; A1 = £5.52m, which is positive, so the project should proceed on efficiency grounds (NPV > 0).
3. [4] B1 when the growth rate g exceeds the interest rate r on existing debt, the economy (and tax base) grows faster than the debt stock accrues interest; B1 debt-to-GDP can then stabilise, or even fall, despite a persistent (moderate) primary deficit, since GDP is growing faster than debt; B1 when r > g, interest costs compound faster than the economy (and revenue base) grows; B1 so the same primary deficit path leads to an ever-rising debt-to-GDP ratio, requiring larger future primary surpluses (or higher growth/lower rates) to stabilise it.
4. [3] B1 defaults are not neutral: whichever option is set as the default will influence behaviour simply because of inertia/status-quo bias, so 'no nudge' is not actually a neutral baseline; B1 designers must therefore make an active value judgement about which default serves people's genuine interests (e.g. assuming higher pension saving is what most people would choose if fully engaged); B1 this normative claim (what people would choose, or what is good for them) is contestable and goes beyond the purely technical/positive economics of how the nudge works.
