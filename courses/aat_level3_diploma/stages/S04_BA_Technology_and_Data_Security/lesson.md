# S04_BA_Technology_and_Data_Security - Lesson: New technologies in accounting and data protection/security

## Goal
The learner explains the impact of new accounting technologies (including cloud accounting), and explains data protection principles and information/cybersecurity.

## Syllabus items taught here
- BA4.1 - New technologies in accounting: automation, AI and machine learning, blockchain, e-filing/e-signing, data analytics, outsourcing/offshoring, and cloud accounting
- BA4.2 - Data protection and security: the UK GDPR principles, the impact of a data breach, information security controls and cybersecurity

## How to teach this
Ask the learner: what are two things cloud accounting software lets a business do that traditional desktop accounting software does not? Have the learner attempt every calculation (depreciation, accruals/prepayments, irrecoverable debts, inventory valuation, trial balance/ETB adjustments, sole trader and partnership financial statements, profitability ratios, incomplete records, FIFO/LIFO/AVCO, overhead absorption, marginal vs absorption costing, budgets, variances, breakeven, limiting factors, cash flow forecasts, VAT returns, PAYE/NIC and statutory payments) with full workings before checking the model answer -- these are computer-marked numeric-entry and multi-task items in the real AAT assessment, so exact figures matter. Every numeric example in this course was computed and verified in Python when the course was built. UK VAT is taken at the standard rate of 20% (reduced rate 5%, zero rate 0%) and the VAT registration threshold at £90,000 (deregistration £88,000) throughout, and payroll figures use the 2026/27 tax year -- check gov.uk for the current rates if they may have changed. AAT's assessments mix objective-test items (single- and multiple-select, numeric gap-fill, drag-and-drop/matching) with multi-task, human-marked written/journal-entry questions on Business Awareness; this course's items mirror the same calculation-and-entry style using clearly marked short-answer and multiple-choice items.

#### BA4.1 New technologies in accounting: automation, AI and machine learning, blockchain, e-filing/e-signing, data analytics, outsourcing/offshoring, and cloud accounting
New technologies affecting accounting: **automation** of routine processes (e.g. bank feeds automatically matching to transactions), **AI and machine learning** (e.g. flagging anomalies, predicting cash flow), **blockchain** (a shared, tamper-resistant ledger of transactions), **electronic filing** and **electronic signing** (submitting returns/documents and signing contracts digitally rather than on paper), and **data analytics** (using large volumes of data to find patterns and support decisions). **Outsourcing** (contracting a function like payroll to an external specialist) and **offshoring** (moving a function to a different country) can affect a business's cost structure (often lower cost), access to new markets, choice of location and how it interacts with stakeholders. **Automation and AI** are changing the accountant's role: reducing time spent on manual, repetitive data entry and reconciliation, and shifting the finance function's value toward analysis, interpretation and advisory work. **Cloud accounting** software is hosted online rather than on a local computer, giving: **remote access** (from any device, anywhere), online **data storage** (rather than a local hard drive), greater **automation** (e.g. automatic bank feeds), integration with **apps and plug-ins** (e.g. for expenses, payroll), easier interaction with stakeholders (e.g. an accountant and client viewing the same live data) and **real-time data** (figures update as transactions occur, rather than only at period end). Its **benefits** include collaboration, accessibility and up-to-date information; its **limitations** include dependence on a reliable internet connection, ongoing subscription cost, and data security/privacy concerns from storing data with a third party.

#### BA4.2 Data protection and security: the UK GDPR principles, the impact of a data breach, information security controls and cybersecurity
The UK GDPR sets out **data protection principles**: personal data must be processed **lawfully, fairly and transparently**; collected only for specified, legitimate purposes (**purpose limitation**); adequate, relevant and limited to what is necessary (**data minimisation**); kept **accurate** and up to date; kept no longer than necessary (**storage limitation**); processed with appropriate security (**integrity and confidentiality**); and the organisation must be able to demonstrate compliance with all of this (**accountability**). A **data breach** (personal data lost, stolen or wrongly disclosed) can impact **individuals** (identity theft, financial loss, distress) and **businesses** (regulatory fines, reputational damage, loss of customer trust, potential legal claims). **Information security** protects data through measures such as: **access levels** (only giving staff access to the data they need for their role), **firewalls** (blocking unauthorised network access) and **integrity controls** (ensuring data has not been tampered with). **Cybersecurity** specifically addresses risks from digital/online threats, since **cyberattacks** (e.g. phishing, ransomware, hacking) threaten both the confidentiality of data and the continued operation of the business's systems.
