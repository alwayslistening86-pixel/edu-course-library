# S03_BA_Ethics_and_Money_Laundering - Lesson: The ethical code, ethical conflicts and money laundering

## Goal
The learner names and applies the fundamental ethical principles, explains how ethical conflicts arise and are resolved, and explains the money laundering process and reporting duties.

## Syllabus items taught here
- BA3.1 - The fundamental principles of the ethical code: integrity, objectivity, professional behaviour, professional competence and due care, confidentiality, professional scepticism, and safeguards against threats
- BA3.2 - Ethical conflicts: how they arise, resolving them, disciplinary and legal consequences, and how to report unethical behaviour
- BA3.3 - Money laundering: placement, layering and integration, the offences of tipping off and failure to disclose, and reporting suspicious activity

## How to teach this
Ask the learner: a manager asks you to backdate an invoice so this quarter's figures look better. Which ethical principle does that threaten, and what should you do? Have the learner attempt every calculation (depreciation, accruals/prepayments, irrecoverable debts, inventory valuation, trial balance/ETB adjustments, sole trader and partnership financial statements, profitability ratios, incomplete records, FIFO/LIFO/AVCO, overhead absorption, marginal vs absorption costing, budgets, variances, breakeven, limiting factors, cash flow forecasts, VAT returns, PAYE/NIC and statutory payments) with full workings before checking the model answer -- these are computer-marked numeric-entry and multi-task items in the real AAT assessment, so exact figures matter. Every numeric example in this course was computed and verified in Python when the course was built. UK VAT is taken at the standard rate of 20% (reduced rate 5%, zero rate 0%) and the VAT registration threshold at £90,000 (deregistration £88,000) throughout, and payroll figures use the 2026/27 tax year -- check gov.uk for the current rates if they may have changed. AAT's assessments mix objective-test items (single- and multiple-select, numeric gap-fill, drag-and-drop/matching) with multi-task, human-marked written/journal-entry questions on Business Awareness; this course's items mirror the same calculation-and-entry style using clearly marked short-answer and multiple-choice items.

#### BA3.1 The fundamental principles of the ethical code: integrity, objectivity, professional behaviour, professional competence and due care, confidentiality, professional scepticism, and safeguards against threats
The fundamental principles of the professional ethical code: **integrity** -- being honest, transparent and fair in all professional relationships (threatened by e.g. self-interest, or familiarity with a long-standing client/colleague clouding judgement); **objectivity** -- not allowing bias, conflict of interest or undue influence to override professional judgement (a **conflict of interest** exists where personal or other interests could affect judgement; a financial interest in an outcome, or an incentive tied to a particular result, creates a self-interest threat -- **professional scepticism**, a questioning mind that critically assesses evidence rather than accepting it at face value, helps guard against this); **professional behaviour** -- complying with relevant law and regulation, but also avoiding any conduct that discredits the profession, which is a higher bar than the legal minimum; **professional competence and due care** -- maintaining the knowledge/skill needed (through continuing professional development, CPD) and acting diligently and in accordance with applicable technical and professional standards; **confidentiality** -- not disclosing information acquired professionally without proper authority, nor using it for personal advantage (affected by data protection law, which adds legal obligations on top of the ethical duty). An ethics framework can be **principles-based** (a set of principles applied using judgement to the situation, as above) or **rules-based** (a fixed list of prohibited/required actions) -- a principles-based approach is more flexible but relies more on professional judgement. Where a threat to a principle is identified, **safeguards** (documented organisational policies, e.g. segregation of duties, second-partner review) can eliminate or reduce the threat to an acceptable level; if no safeguard is sufficient, the professional should decline or withdraw from the situation.

#### BA3.2 Ethical conflicts: how they arise, resolving them, disciplinary and legal consequences, and how to report unethical behaviour
An **ethical conflict** arises when an individual's own values, an instruction from a manager/client, or organisational pressure (e.g. a deadline, a target) pulls against a fundamental ethical principle. To determine whether behaviour is ethical, consider: is it legal, does it comply with the organisation's own values and any relevant code of ethics, and would it stand up to public scrutiny? Organisational values commonly expect: transparency with customers and suppliers, timely and accurate financial reporting, clear policies on gifts and hospitality (to avoid undue influence), fair treatment of suppliers and employees, and responsible use of social media (avoiding disclosure of confidential information). Resolving an ethical conflict typically follows staged steps: gather the relevant facts, identify the ethical issues and affected parties, consider established internal procedures, evaluate alternative courses of action, and, if unresolved internally, seek advice from a professional body or independent adviser -- documenting the reasoning throughout. Ignoring an ethical conflict can lead to: **disciplinary action** by an employer or professional body (which can include being struck off/expelled), **professional negligence** claims (if the failure amounts to a breach of a professional duty of care) and possible **breach of contract** claims; many professionals carry **professional indemnity insurance (PII)** against such claims. Unethical behaviour observed by others should be reported: through the organisation's own internal reporting procedures, or by **whistle-blowing** if internal routes fail or are inappropriate -- a genuine, reasonable disclosure made in the public interest is a **protected disclosure** under whistleblowing law, and third-party/professional-body advice can be sought before deciding how to act.

#### BA3.3 Money laundering: placement, layering and integration, the offences of tipping off and failure to disclose, and reporting suspicious activity
**Money laundering** is the process of disguising the proceeds of crime as legitimate funds, in three typical stages: **placement** (getting the illegal cash into the financial system, e.g. paying it into a bank), **layering** (moving it through a series of transactions to obscure its origin) and **integration** (the now 'clean' funds re-enter the legitimate economy, e.g. buying property). Accountants face specific legal duties and offences: **tipping off** is an offence -- telling a client (or anyone) that a suspicion has been reported, which could prejudice an investigation; **failure to disclose** a reasonable suspicion of money laundering to the firm's Money Laundering Reporting Officer, or to the National Crime Agency/HMRC, is itself an offence. A disclosure made properly (in good faith and through the correct channel) is a **protected** and **authorised** disclosure, giving the discloser legal protection from breach-of-confidentiality claims. Accountancy firms are typically supervised for anti-money-laundering compliance by a **professional body** (e.g. AAT, ICAEW) or by **HMRC**. A **Suspicious Activity Report (SAR)** should be made promptly once a suspicion arises, including only the relevant facts needed to describe the suspicion -- not excessive detail that risks tipping off, and not delayed while further, unnecessary investigation is carried out.
