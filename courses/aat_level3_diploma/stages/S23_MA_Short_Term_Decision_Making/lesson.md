# S23_MA_Short_Term_Decision_Making - Lesson: Breakeven and CVP analysis, limiting factors and relevant costing

## Goal
The learner calculates breakeven point, margin of safety and target profit; ranks products by contribution per unit of limiting factor; and applies relevant costing to make-or-buy and discontinuation decisions.

## Syllabus items taught here
- MA6.1 - Cost-volume-profit analysis: contribution, calculating the breakeven point (units and sales value), margin of safety and target profit
- MA6.2 - Limiting factor analysis: identifying the scarce resource and ranking products by contribution per unit of limiting factor to find the optimal mix
- MA6.3 - Relevant costing: distinguishing relevant from irrelevant costs, and applying this to make-or-buy and discontinuation decisions

## How to teach this
Ask the learner: if a machine's capacity limits how much can be produced, should a business decide what to make based on total contribution per unit, or contribution per hour of machine time? Have the learner attempt every calculation (depreciation, accruals/prepayments, irrecoverable debts, inventory valuation, trial balance/ETB adjustments, sole trader and partnership financial statements, profitability ratios, incomplete records, FIFO/LIFO/AVCO, overhead absorption, marginal vs absorption costing, budgets, variances, breakeven, limiting factors, cash flow forecasts, VAT returns, PAYE/NIC and statutory payments) with full workings before checking the model answer -- these are computer-marked numeric-entry and multi-task items in the real AAT assessment, so exact figures matter. Every numeric example in this course was computed and verified in Python when the course was built. UK VAT is taken at the standard rate of 20% (reduced rate 5%, zero rate 0%) and the VAT registration threshold at £90,000 (deregistration £88,000) throughout, and payroll figures use the 2026/27 tax year -- check gov.uk for the current rates if they may have changed. AAT's assessments mix objective-test items (single- and multiple-select, numeric gap-fill, drag-and-drop/matching) with multi-task, human-marked written/journal-entry questions on Business Awareness; this course's items mirror the same calculation-and-entry style using clearly marked short-answer and multiple-choice items.

#### MA6.1 Cost-volume-profit analysis: contribution, calculating the breakeven point (units and sales value), margin of safety and target profit
**Contribution** = selling price - variable cost per unit; it contributes first to covering fixed costs, then to profit. The **breakeven point** is the level of activity at which total contribution exactly equals fixed costs (profit is zero): Breakeven (units) = Fixed costs / Contribution per unit; Breakeven (sales value) = Breakeven units x selling price. The **margin of safety** is how far budgeted/actual sales exceed the breakeven point, showing how much sales could fall before a loss is made: Margin of safety (units) = Budgeted sales - Breakeven sales; often expressed as a percentage of budgeted sales. For a **target profit**, the required sales volume = (Fixed costs + target profit) / Contribution per unit. *Worked example:* selling price £40/unit, variable cost £24/unit, fixed costs £64,000. Contribution = 40 - 24 = **£16/unit**. Breakeven = 64,000/16 = **4,000 units** (sales value = 4,000 x £40 = £160,000). If budgeted sales are 5,000 units, margin of safety = 5,000 - 4,000 = **1,000 units** (20% of budgeted sales: 1,000/5,000). For a target profit of £20,000: required units = (64,000 + 20,000)/16 = 84,000/16 = **5,250 units**.

#### MA6.2 Limiting factor analysis: identifying the scarce resource and ranking products by contribution per unit of limiting factor to find the optimal mix
Where a **limiting factor** (a scarce resource -- e.g. machine hours, labour hours, material availability) restricts how much can be produced/sold, and demand for products exceeds what the limiting factor allows for all of them, the business should not simply prioritise the product with the highest contribution *per unit* -- it should prioritise the product with the highest **contribution per unit of the limiting factor**, since that maximises total contribution (and therefore profit) from the scarce resource available. Method: calculate contribution per unit for each product; divide by the amount of the limiting factor each unit uses, to get contribution per unit of limiting factor; **rank** products from highest to lowest; allocate the scarce resource to the highest-ranked product first (up to its maximum demand), then the next, and so on, until the resource runs out. *Worked example:* machine hours are limited to 2,000 hours. Product X: contribution £30/unit, using 2 machine hours/unit -- contribution per hour = 30/2 = **£15**; maximum demand 500 units (needing 1,000 hours). Product Y: contribution £24/unit, using 1.5 machine hours/unit -- contribution per hour = 24/1.5 = **£16**; maximum demand 800 units (needing 1,200 hours). Since 1,000 + 1,200 = 2,200 hours would be needed but only 2,000 are available, machine hours are indeed the limiting factor. Y ranks higher (£16/hour vs £15/hour), so it is produced first: 800 units of Y uses 1,200 hours, leaving 2,000 - 1,200 = 800 hours for X, giving 800/2 = 400 units of X (within its 500-unit demand limit). Total contribution = (800 x £24) + (400 x £30) = 19,200 + 12,000 = **£31,200**.

#### MA6.3 Relevant costing: distinguishing relevant from irrelevant costs, and applying this to make-or-buy and discontinuation decisions
A **relevant cost** is a future, incremental cash cost that will change as a direct result of the decision being made; an **irrelevant cost** does not change with the decision, and so should be ignored -- common irrelevant costs include: **sunk costs** (already spent, unaffected by the decision), and **fixed costs that will be incurred regardless** of which option is chosen (e.g. fixed overhead that will still be paid whether the product is made in-house or bought in, or whether a division continues or is discontinued). For a **make-or-buy** decision, the relevant cost of making is the *avoidable* variable/incremental cost of production (unavoidable fixed overhead absorbed into the unit cost is excluded), compared with the price of buying in. For a **discontinuation** decision, a product/division should normally only be discontinued if the **contribution it earns** is less than the fixed costs that would actually be *saved* by discontinuing it (i.e. avoidable, not just apportioned, fixed costs) -- discontinuing something that still earns a positive contribution, where its fixed costs would be incurred anyway (e.g. shared head-office costs), simply loses that contribution without saving anything, reducing overall profit. *Worked example -- make or buy:* the absorbed cost to make a unit in-house is materials £8, labour £6, variable overhead £2 and fixed overhead absorbed £4, totalling £20/unit; an external supplier offers to supply at £17/unit. The relevant cost of making is only the *avoidable* cost: 8 + 6 + 2 = **£16/unit** (the £4 fixed overhead is unavoidable and irrelevant, since it will be incurred either way). Since £16 (relevant cost to make) is less than £17 (buying-in price), it remains **cheaper to make in-house** -- buying in would cost £1/unit more than necessary, even though the full absorbed cost of £20 makes buying in at £17 look attractive at first glance.
