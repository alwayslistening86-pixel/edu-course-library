# S05_BA_Information_Visualisation_and_Communication - Lesson: Information requirements, big data, visualisation and communication

## Goal
The learner explains the qualities of good information and big data, selects appropriate ways to visualise information, and chooses an appropriate communication method.

## Syllabus items taught here
- BA5.1 - Information requirements and big data: qualities of good information, the three organisational levels, the five Vs of big data, and its benefits, limitations and sources
- BA5.2 - Visualising information: choosing an appropriate chart/diagram/table/matrix, identifying patterns and anomalies, and communicating dashboards to non-technical stakeholders
- BA5.3 - Choosing an appropriate communication method: matching the medium to the stakeholder, ensuring validity and confidentiality

## How to teach this
Ask the learner: why might a line chart be a better way to show a year of monthly sales than a table of 12 numbers? Have the learner attempt every calculation (depreciation, accruals/prepayments, irrecoverable debts, inventory valuation, trial balance/ETB adjustments, sole trader and partnership financial statements, profitability ratios, incomplete records, FIFO/LIFO/AVCO, overhead absorption, marginal vs absorption costing, budgets, variances, breakeven, limiting factors, cash flow forecasts, VAT returns, PAYE/NIC and statutory payments) with full workings before checking the model answer -- these are computer-marked numeric-entry and multi-task items in the real AAT assessment, so exact figures matter. Every numeric example in this course was computed and verified in Python when the course was built. UK VAT is taken at the standard rate of 20% (reduced rate 5%, zero rate 0%) and the VAT registration threshold at £90,000 (deregistration £88,000) throughout, and payroll figures use the 2026/27 tax year -- check gov.uk for the current rates if they may have changed. AAT's assessments mix objective-test items (single- and multiple-select, numeric gap-fill, drag-and-drop/matching) with multi-task, human-marked written/journal-entry questions on Business Awareness; this course's items mirror the same calculation-and-entry style using clearly marked short-answer and multiple-choice items.

#### BA5.1 Information requirements and big data: qualities of good information, the three organisational levels, the five Vs of big data, and its benefits, limitations and sources
Good quality information is: relevant, reliable/accurate, timely, complete, cost-effective to obtain, understandable to its audience, and communicated to the right person. Information needs differ by organisational level: **operational** staff need detailed, frequent, mostly internal information for day-to-day tasks; **managerial** staff need summarised, periodic information for planning and control; **strategic** (senior management/board) level needs highly summarised information, often drawing on external as well as internal sources, for long-term decisions. **Big data** describes datasets too large or complex for traditional processing, characterised by the **five Vs**: **volume** (the sheer quantity of data), **variety** (many different formats/sources -- structured and unstructured), **velocity** (the speed data is generated and must be processed), **veracity** (how trustworthy/accurate the data is) and **value** (the usefulness of the insight it can generate). Its **benefits** include richer insight and better-informed decisions; **limitations** include the cost/complexity of processing it, and the risk of poor-quality (low-veracity) data leading to wrong conclusions. Big data can come from **internal sources** (e.g. transaction/sales systems) or **external sources** (e.g. social media, market data, government statistics) -- **professional scepticism** should be applied to big data just as to any other information, questioning its source and reliability rather than accepting an analysis at face value.

#### BA5.2 Visualising information: choosing an appropriate chart/diagram/table/matrix, identifying patterns and anomalies, and communicating dashboards to non-technical stakeholders
Choosing the right way to **visualise** information matters because the wrong format can obscure, rather than reveal, meaning. Options include **images**, **charts** (e.g. bar, line, pie), **diagrams**, **tables**, **matrices** and **graphs** -- each suits different data and different questions: a line chart suits a trend over time, a bar chart suits comparing discrete categories, a pie chart suits showing proportions of a whole, a table suits precise figures the reader needs to look up. Visualisation helps a reader identify **patterns** (e.g. seasonality) and **anomalies** (e.g. an unusual spike) that would be hard to spot in raw numbers. Accounting software increasingly presents information through **dashboards** -- a single visual summary of key figures -- which is especially useful for communicating to **non-technical stakeholders**, who need the story the numbers tell, not the underlying detail. Good visualisation should make relationships and trends easy to interpret at a glance.

#### BA5.3 Choosing an appropriate communication method: matching the medium to the stakeholder, ensuring validity and confidentiality
Choosing an appropriate **communication method** depends on: the audience/stakeholder (internal vs external, technical vs non-technical), the urgency and complexity of the message, and whether a permanent record is needed. Professional business communication should: meet the stakeholder's actual requirements (right level of detail, right format), use a medium appropriate to the message (e.g. a formal letter/report for significant matters, email for routine updates, a face-to-face/video meeting for sensitive or complex discussions), ensure the **validity** of the information communicated (accurate, up to date), and maintain **confidentiality** (not sharing sensitive information via an inappropriate or insecure channel).
