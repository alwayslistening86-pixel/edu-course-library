# S08_FA_Non_Current_Asset_Acquisition_and_Disposal - Lesson: Authorising capital expenditure, capital vs revenue expenditure, and recording acquisitions/disposals

## Goal
The learner explains the need to authorise capital expenditure, classifies expenditure as capital or revenue under IAS 16, and records the acquisition and disposal of non-current assets.

## Syllabus items taught here
- FA3.1 - The importance of prior authority for capital expenditure: why it is needed and who should give it
- FA3.2 - Capital vs revenue expenditure under IAS 16: cost, useful life, residual value, depreciable amount and carrying amount; what can and cannot be included in cost
- FA3.3 - Recording acquisitions and disposals: the non-current asset register, part-exchange, and gains/losses on disposal (including VAT treatment by registration status)

## How to teach this
Ask the learner: a business buys a new delivery van and also pays for a service and an oil change on an existing van. Which of these is capital expenditure and which is revenue expenditure? Have the learner attempt every calculation (depreciation, accruals/prepayments, irrecoverable debts, inventory valuation, trial balance/ETB adjustments, sole trader and partnership financial statements, profitability ratios, incomplete records, FIFO/LIFO/AVCO, overhead absorption, marginal vs absorption costing, budgets, variances, breakeven, limiting factors, cash flow forecasts, VAT returns, PAYE/NIC and statutory payments) with full workings before checking the model answer -- these are computer-marked numeric-entry and multi-task items in the real AAT assessment, so exact figures matter. Every numeric example in this course was computed and verified in Python when the course was built. UK VAT is taken at the standard rate of 20% (reduced rate 5%, zero rate 0%) and the VAT registration threshold at £90,000 (deregistration £88,000) throughout, and payroll figures use the 2026/27 tax year -- check gov.uk for the current rates if they may have changed. AAT's assessments mix objective-test items (single- and multiple-select, numeric gap-fill, drag-and-drop/matching) with multi-task, human-marked written/journal-entry questions on Business Awareness; this course's items mirror the same calculation-and-entry style using clearly marked short-answer and multiple-choice items.

#### FA3.1 The importance of prior authority for capital expenditure: why it is needed and who should give it
**Capital expenditure** (spending on non-current assets) normally requires **prior authorisation** before it is committed, because it is typically large, has a long-term effect on the business, and is harder to reverse than day-to-day spending. Authorisation is usually needed from someone with appropriate seniority/authority in the organisation -- e.g. a director, a finance manager, or as set by a formal capital expenditure policy/budget -- to ensure the expenditure is properly justified, budgeted for, and consistent with the organisation's strategy, before it is incurred.

#### FA3.2 Capital vs revenue expenditure under IAS 16: cost, useful life, residual value, depreciable amount and carrying amount; what can and cannot be included in cost
**IAS 16** (Property, Plant and Equipment) governs non-current assets. Key definitions: **cost** (the amount paid to acquire the asset and bring it to its working condition/location for its intended use); **useful life** (the period over which the asset is expected to be used); **residual value** (the estimated amount recoverable at the end of the useful life, e.g. from sale/scrap); **depreciable amount** (cost less residual value -- the amount to be depreciated over the useful life); **carrying amount** (cost less accumulated depreciation, the asset's value currently shown in the books). **Capital expenditure** improves or adds to a non-current asset, or brings it into use (e.g. purchase price, delivery, installation, legal fees on acquiring property, costs of testing the asset works properly) -- it is capitalised (added to the non-current asset's cost) rather than expensed immediately. **Revenue expenditure** maintains an asset in its existing condition or is part of normal running costs (e.g. repairs, servicing, fuel) and should be **excluded** from the non-current asset's cost -- it is expensed to the statement of profit or loss as incurred. Organisations usually set a **capitalisation policy threshold** (e.g. only capitalise items over £500) -- items below it are expensed even if they would otherwise qualify, to avoid the administrative cost of tracking small items as non-current assets. Getting this classification wrong affects both the statement of profit or loss (revenue expenditure wrongly capitalised understates that period's expenses and overstates profit) and the statement of financial position (understates/overstates non-current assets). *Worked example:* a machine has a list price of £18,000 with a 5% trade discount, plus £400 delivery and £600 installation. Discounted price = £18,000 x (1 - 0.05) = **£17,100**; total cost to capitalise = £17,100 + £400 + £600 = **£18,100**.

#### FA3.3 Recording acquisitions and disposals: the non-current asset register, part-exchange, and gains/losses on disposal (including VAT treatment by registration status)
Acquisitions and disposals are tracked in the **non-current asset register** (a record of each asset's cost, depreciation and carrying amount, which can be part of the accounting software or held independently on a spreadsheet), and posted through a **disposals account** in the general ledger: the asset's original **cost** is transferred out of the non-current asset cost account into the disposals account (debit disposals, credit cost account); its **accumulated depreciation** to date is removed (debit accumulated depreciation, credit disposals); the **sale proceeds** received are recorded (debit bank/receivable, credit disposals). The resulting balance on the disposals account is the **gain or loss on disposal**: if proceeds exceed the carrying amount (cost less accumulated depreciation), there is a **profit** on disposal; if proceeds are less than the carrying amount, there is a **loss** -- transferred to the statement of profit or loss at period end. A **part-exchange** (trading in an old asset against the price of a new one) is a different form of funding the purchase than paying entirely in cash or on credit -- the part-exchange allowance is treated as the 'proceeds' for the old asset's disposal, and reduces the amount owed/paid for the new asset. VAT on a disposal is treated according to the **registration status** of the business making the disposal (a VAT-registered business normally charges VAT on the disposal of a standard-rated asset it was entitled to reclaim input VAT on). *Worked example:* an asset originally cost £20,000, with accumulated depreciation of £14,000 (carrying amount £6,000), is sold for £8,000 cash. Disposals account: debit cost £20,000; credit accumulated depreciation £14,000 and credit proceeds £8,000 (£22,000 total credits). The credit balance of £22,000 - £20,000 = **£2,000** is a **profit on disposal** (proceeds £8,000 exceed the carrying amount £6,000 by £2,000).
