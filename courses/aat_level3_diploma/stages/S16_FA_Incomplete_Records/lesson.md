# S16_FA_Incomplete_Records - Lesson: Preparing accounting records from incomplete information, mark-up/margin, and reasonableness

## Goal
The learner identifies missing figures from incomplete accounting records by reconstructing control accounts, uses mark-up and margin to find missing figures, and assesses the reasonableness of figures.

## Syllabus items taught here
- FA9.1 - Identifying missing figures from incomplete records: reconstructing the receivables/payables control accounts, the VAT control account and the bank account
- FA9.2 - Mark-up and margin: the difference between them, and using each to calculate missing figures via cost of sales
- FA9.3 - Assessing the reasonableness of figures when information is incomplete, and why an actual and a calculated balance can differ

## How to teach this
Ask the learner: if you know a business's opening and closing receivables balances, and everything else that moved through the receivables control account except sales, how could you work out sales? Have the learner attempt every calculation (depreciation, accruals/prepayments, irrecoverable debts, inventory valuation, trial balance/ETB adjustments, sole trader and partnership financial statements, profitability ratios, incomplete records, FIFO/LIFO/AVCO, overhead absorption, marginal vs absorption costing, budgets, variances, breakeven, limiting factors, cash flow forecasts, VAT returns, PAYE/NIC and statutory payments) with full workings before checking the model answer -- these are computer-marked numeric-entry and multi-task items in the real AAT assessment, so exact figures matter. Every numeric example in this course was computed and verified in Python when the course was built. UK VAT is taken at the standard rate of 20% (reduced rate 5%, zero rate 0%) and the VAT registration threshold at £90,000 (deregistration £88,000) throughout, and payroll figures use the 2026/27 tax year -- check gov.uk for the current rates if they may have changed. AAT's assessments mix objective-test items (single- and multiple-select, numeric gap-fill, drag-and-drop/matching) with multi-task, human-marked written/journal-entry questions on Business Awareness; this course's items mirror the same calculation-and-entry style using clearly marked short-answer and multiple-choice items.

#### FA9.1 Identifying missing figures from incomplete records: reconstructing the receivables/payables control accounts, the VAT control account and the bank account
Where full records were not kept, missing figures relating to income, expenses, assets, liabilities or capital are found by using the **accounting equation** and by **reconstructing** the relevant ledger accounts from the data that is available -- selecting the relevant figures from daybooks and the cash book. Commonly reconstructed accounts: the **receivables ledger control account** (to find missing credit sales, or a missing closing receivables balance), the **payables ledger control account** (to find missing credit purchases), the **VAT control account** (to find a missing VAT figure, adjusting for VAT on sales/purchases) and the **bank account** (to find a missing receipt or payment). The unknown figure is the amount needed to make the account balance -- entered as the missing debit or credit, then labelled and calculated. *Worked example:* the receivables control account opens at £24,000; receipts from customers during the year are £186,000, discounts allowed £2,500, irrecoverable debts written off £1,200, and the closing balance is £27,300; credit sales for the year are unknown. Total credits (money leaving the account) = 186,000 + 2,500 + 1,200 + 27,300 = **£217,000**; since debits must equal credits, credit sales = 217,000 - 24,000 (opening balance) = **£193,000**.

#### FA9.2 Mark-up and margin: the difference between them, and using each to calculate missing figures via cost of sales
**Margin** expresses gross profit as a percentage of **revenue**; **mark-up** expresses gross profit as a percentage of **cost of sales** -- the same profit, expressed against a different base, so mark-up is always the larger percentage of the two for the same figures. Either can be used to calculate a missing figure when cost of sales (or revenue) and the percentage are known, which is common where inventory/purchase records exist but sales records are incomplete (or vice versa). *Worked example -- from mark-up:* cost of sales is £80,000 and mark-up is 25%: gross profit = £80,000 x 25% = **£20,000**; revenue = cost of sales + gross profit = 80,000 + 20,000 = **£100,000**. *Worked example -- from margin:* revenue is £150,000 and margin is 30%: gross profit = £150,000 x 30% = **£45,000**; cost of sales = revenue - gross profit = 150,000 - 45,000 = **£105,000**.

#### FA9.3 Assessing the reasonableness of figures when information is incomplete, and why an actual and a calculated balance can differ
Where records are incomplete, an accountant must judge whether a figure -- calculated, given, or produced by accounting software -- is **reasonable**. This means: comparing it to expectations from prior periods or industry norms, checking it is consistent with other information available (e.g. does a calculated gross margin look plausible for this type of business), and being alert that an **actual** balance (e.g. a physical inventory count, or a bank statement) and a **calculated** balance (built up from other records) can genuinely differ -- for reasons including theft, loss, spoilage, timing differences or a simple recording error, not necessarily because the calculation itself is wrong. **Professional scepticism** should be applied particularly to figures produced automatically by accounting software, which are only as reliable as the data entered into it -- a plausible-looking output can still be wrong if the underlying data was incomplete or miskeyed.
