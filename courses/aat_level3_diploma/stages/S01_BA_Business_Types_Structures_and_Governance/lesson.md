#### BA1.1 Types of business: sole trader, partnership, LLP, limited partnership, Ltd, PLC, not-for-profit; ownership/management separation, funding sources, organisational features, manufacturing vs service
The main UK business types: a **sole trader** is owned and run by one person, who has **unlimited liability** (personally liable for all business debts); a **partnership** is owned by two or more people (also unlimited liability, governed by the Partnership Act 1890 unless a partnership agreement says otherwise); a **limited liability partnership (LLP)** gives partners limited liability while keeping partnership-style flexibility; a **limited partnership** has at least one general partner (unlimited liability, manages the business) and at least one limited partner (liability capped at their investment, no management role); a **private limited company (Ltd)** and a **public limited company (PLC)** are separate legal persons from their owners (shareholders), giving **limited liability** -- a PLC can offer shares to the public and be listed on a stock exchange, a Ltd cannot; a **not-for-profit organisation** (e.g. a charity or community interest company) exists to serve a social purpose rather than to distribute profit to owners. In a sole trader or small partnership, ownership and management are usually the same people; in a company, ownership (shareholders) and management (directors) are typically **separated**, which is why governance -- how directors are held accountable to shareholders -- matters more as a business grows. Businesses are funded from: **new capital** introduced by owners, **retained profits** (reinvested rather than withdrawn), **lending** (bank loans, overdrafts), and **working capital** (managing the cash tied up in day-to-day operations, e.g. receivables/inventory/payables). Common organisational features across all of these: interrelated individuals working together, **goal congruence** (individual objectives aligned with the organisation's), cooperation, clearly defined roles, teamwork and division of work. A **manufacturing** business converts raw materials into finished goods (so needs production/inventory-costing information not needed by a pure **service** business, which sells expertise/time and has little or no physical inventory) -- this affects what information is available and how it is reported.

#### BA1.2 The legal framework: company legislation (shareholder rights, director duties, formation/reporting), partnership law (agreement contents, goodwill, changes in partners)
The **legal framework** for companies is set mainly by the **Companies Act 2006**: it sets out **shareholders' rights** (e.g. to vote at general meetings, to receive dividends declared, to receive the annual accounts), **directors' duties** (e.g. to act within their powers, to promote the success of the company, to exercise independent judgement and reasonable care/skill/diligence, to avoid conflicts of interest), and the rules for **company formation** (incorporation at Companies House) and **reporting** (filing annual accounts and a confirmation statement). **Partnership law** (the Partnership Act 1890, as often varied by a written **partnership agreement**) governs what the partnership agreement should contain -- capital contributions, profit-sharing ratios, interest on capital/drawings, salaries, and how decisions are made -- the treatment of **goodwill** (the value of the business's reputation/customer base above its net assets, which can be revalued when the partnership changes), and what happens on a **change of partners** (a new partner joining, or an existing partner leaving/retiring), which in strict law dissolves the old partnership and forms a new one, even if trading continues seamlessly.

#### BA1.3 Stakeholders: the eight stakeholder categories, their objectives, their contributions and relative significance
AAT identifies **eight stakeholder categories**: **customers** (want quality goods/services at a fair price, reliable supply), **suppliers** (want to be paid on time, an ongoing trading relationship), **finance providers** (banks/investors -- want their loan repaid with interest, or a return on their investment, and evidence the business can meet its obligations), **owners** (want a return on their investment -- profit, dividends, capital growth), **government** (wants tax paid, compliance with law and regulation, employment created), **employees** (want fair pay, job security, safe working conditions), **regulatory bodies** (want compliance with the rules of their sector, e.g. financial services regulation), and the **public** (wants the business to act responsibly -- environmentally, socially, ethically). Each stakeholder's **relative significance** and attitude to risk differs: a bank funding a large loan will demand more information and control than a small individual shareholder; stakeholders with **high power and high interest** in the business (e.g. a majority shareholder, a key lender) need to be managed most closely, since their objectives can conflict (e.g. employees wanting higher pay vs owners wanting higher profit).

#### BA1.4 Organisational structure and governance: functional/divisional/matrix structures, span of control, centralised vs decentralised, operational/managerial/strategic levels, the finance function's role, types of risk and how they are managed
**Organisational structures**: a **functional** structure groups staff by what they do (finance, sales, production, HR); a **divisional** structure groups staff by product line, geography or customer type, each division often near self-contained; a **matrix** structure has staff reporting to both a functional manager and a project/product manager at once. **Span of control** is how many people report directly to one manager -- a wide span (many direct reports) tends to produce a **flat** structure (fewer management layers); a narrow span tends to produce a **tall** structure (more layers, tighter control but slower communication). **Governance** -- the system by which an organisation is directed and controlled -- matters in organisations of every type and size, though its formality scales up (a sole trader has minimal formal governance; a PLC has a board, committees and a governance code to follow). Decision-making can be **centralised** (concentrated at the top) or **decentralised** (pushed down to local managers, who can respond faster to local conditions but with less central control). Organisations operate at three levels: **operational** (day-to-day tasks), **managerial/tactical** (medium-term planning and control) and **strategic** (long-term direction, senior management/board level). The **finance function** supports every part of the organisation -- operations, sales and marketing, HR, IT, distribution and logistics -- with financial information, control and advice. **Risk** is the possibility that an event will affect the achievement of objectives; types include **business risk** (general risk of operating, e.g. competition, demand), **financial risk** (e.g. inability to meet debts, interest rate/exchange rate exposure), **strategic risk** (poor high-level decisions, e.g. entering the wrong market) and **operational/cyber/reputational risk** (day-to-day process failures, cyberattacks, damage to public image). Risk is managed by identifying it, assessing its likelihood/impact, and responding -- **avoid**, **reduce**, **transfer** (e.g. insurance) or **accept** it.
