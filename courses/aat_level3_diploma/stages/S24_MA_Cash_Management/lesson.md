#### MA7.1 Cash flow forecasting: the difference between profit and cash, and preparing a cash flow forecast
A **cash flow forecast** predicts future cash inflows and outflows (usually month by month), so a business can anticipate cash shortages (and arrange finance in time) or surpluses (and decide how to use them) before they happen. It is essential because **profit and cash are not the same thing**: profit is recognised under the accruals principle (when earned/incurred), while cash moves only when money actually changes hands -- a profitable business can still run out of cash (e.g. because customers are slow to pay, or because it is investing heavily in inventory or non-current assets) and an unprofitable one can survive for a time on existing cash reserves. **Cash inflows** typically include cash sales, receipts from receivables, capital introduced, and loan proceeds; **cash outflows** typically include payments to payables, wages, overheads paid in cash, capital expenditure and loan repayments -- note that non-cash items like depreciation are never included in a cash flow forecast, since no cash moves. A cash flow forecast is prepared: Closing balance = Opening balance + Receipts - Payments, with each month's closing balance becoming the next month's opening balance. *Worked example:* a month's opening balance is £5,000; receipts for the month are £42,000; payments are £39,500. Closing balance = 5,000 + 42,000 - 39,500 = **£7,500** (which becomes the following month's opening balance).

#### MA7.2 Managing cash surpluses and deficits, and working-capital measures (current ratio, quick ratio, receivables/payables/inventory days)
Where a forecast shows a **cash deficit**, options include: arranging or extending a bank overdraft or short-term loan, delaying non-essential capital expenditure, negotiating longer payment terms with suppliers, or chasing receivables to collect cash faster. Where a forecast shows a **cash surplus**, options include: investing it short-term to earn a return, paying down existing debt to save interest, or bringing forward planned capital expenditure -- surplus cash sitting idle in a current account earns little and represents an opportunity cost. **Working capital** (current assets less current liabilities) is managed and monitored using ratios: the **current ratio** = Current assets / Current liabilities (how many times current liabilities are covered by current assets); the **quick (acid-test) ratio** = (Current assets - Inventory) / Current liabilities (a stricter test, excluding the least liquid current asset); **receivables days** = (Receivables / Credit sales) x 365 (how long, on average, customers take to pay); **payables days** = (Payables / Credit purchases) x 365 (how long the business takes to pay suppliers); **inventory days** = (Inventory / Cost of sales) x 365 (how long inventory is held before being sold). *Worked example:* current assets £86,000 (including inventory £26,000), current liabilities £52,000, credit sales £288,000 with receivables £24,000, credit purchases £219,000 with payables £18,000, and cost of sales £234,000. Current ratio = 86,000/52,000 = **1.65:1**. Quick ratio = (86,000-26,000)/52,000 = 60,000/52,000 = **1.15:1**. Receivables days = (24,000/288,000) x 365 = **30.4 days**. Payables days = (18,000/219,000) x 365 = **30.0 days**. Inventory days = (26,000/234,000) x 365 = **40.6 days**.
