# S21_MA_Budgeting_and_Variance_Analysis - Lesson: Preparing budgets and calculating/interpreting variances

## Goal
The learner prepares functional budgets (including flexing a budget), and calculates and interprets material, labour, overhead and sales variances.

## Syllabus items taught here
- MA4.1 - Budgeting: the purpose of budgets, fixed and flexible budgets, and preparing functional budgets
- MA4.2 - Variance analysis: material price/usage, labour rate/efficiency, variable and fixed overhead, and sales price/volume variances, and interpreting their causes

## How to teach this
Ask the learner: if a production budget needs to allow for a required increase in closing inventory, should the production budget be higher or lower than the sales budget? Have the learner attempt every calculation (depreciation, accruals/prepayments, irrecoverable debts, inventory valuation, trial balance/ETB adjustments, sole trader and partnership financial statements, profitability ratios, incomplete records, FIFO/LIFO/AVCO, overhead absorption, marginal vs absorption costing, budgets, variances, breakeven, limiting factors, cash flow forecasts, VAT returns, PAYE/NIC and statutory payments) with full workings before checking the model answer -- these are computer-marked numeric-entry and multi-task items in the real AAT assessment, so exact figures matter. Every numeric example in this course was computed and verified in Python when the course was built. UK VAT is taken at the standard rate of 20% (reduced rate 5%, zero rate 0%) and the VAT registration threshold at £90,000 (deregistration £88,000) throughout, and payroll figures use the 2026/27 tax year -- check gov.uk for the current rates if they may have changed. AAT's assessments mix objective-test items (single- and multiple-select, numeric gap-fill, drag-and-drop/matching) with multi-task, human-marked written/journal-entry questions on Business Awareness; this course's items mirror the same calculation-and-entry style using clearly marked short-answer and multiple-choice items.

#### MA4.1 Budgeting: the purpose of budgets, fixed and flexible budgets, and preparing functional budgets
**Budgets** plan future income and expenditure, providing a target to work towards, a basis for control (comparing actual results to budget), a way to coordinate different parts of the organisation, and a means of motivating and communicating expectations to managers. A **fixed budget** is set for one activity level and not revised; a **flexible budget** is recalculated for the *actual* level of activity achieved, adjusting variable costs proportionally while leaving fixed costs unchanged -- this gives a fairer comparison to actual results, since a fixed budget alone would make actual performance look worse (or better) purely because volume differed from what was originally planned. **Functional budgets** cover a specific area: the **sales budget** (units x selling price), the **production budget** (units needed = sales + required closing inventory - opening inventory), **material and labour budgets** (derived from the production budget), and the **cash budget** (timing of cash receipts and payments, S24). *Worked example -- production budget:* budgeted sales are 5,000 units; required closing inventory is 600 units; opening inventory is 400 units. Production required = 5,000 + 600 - 400 = **5,200 units**. *Worked example -- flexing a budget:* a fixed budget for 1,000 units shows variable cost £8/unit (£8,000) and fixed cost £5,000 (total £13,000). Flexed to actual output of 1,200 units: variable cost = 1,200 x £8 = £9,600 (fixed cost unchanged at £5,000); flexed budget total = **£14,600**.

#### MA4.2 Variance analysis: material price/usage, labour rate/efficiency, variable and fixed overhead, and sales price/volume variances, and interpreting their causes
A **variance** is the difference between a flexed budget (standard) figure and the actual result; it is **favourable** if it improves profit (e.g. lower cost than standard, or higher revenue than standard) and **adverse** if it reduces profit. Variances can be caused by: a genuine change in price/rate, a change in efficiency/usage, an incorrect original standard, or one-off/unusual events. Key formulae: **material price variance** = (standard price - actual price) x actual quantity; **material usage variance** = (standard quantity for actual output - actual quantity) x standard price; **labour rate variance** = (standard rate - actual rate) x actual hours; **labour efficiency variance** = (standard hours for actual output - actual hours) x standard rate; **sales price variance** = (actual price - standard price) x actual quantity sold; **sales volume variance** = (actual quantity sold - budgeted quantity) x standard profit per unit. *Worked example:* standard cost card per unit: materials 3kg @ £4.00/kg = £12; labour 2hrs @ £10.00/hr = £20; standard selling price £45.00/unit; fixed OAR £10.00/unit (so standard profit/unit = 45 - 12 - 20 - 10 = £3.00). Actual results for 1,900 units produced and sold: materials used 6,000kg costing £25,200 (£4.20/kg); labour 3,700 hours costing £36,260 (£9.80/hr); sales revenue £84,550 (£44.50/unit). **Material price variance** = (4.00 - 4.20) x 6,000 = **£1,200 adverse**. **Material usage variance** = (1,900 x 3 = 5,700 standard kg, vs 6,000 actual) x £4.00 = (5,700-6,000) x 4 = **£1,200 adverse**. **Labour rate variance** = (10.00 - 9.80) x 3,700 = **£740 favourable**. **Labour efficiency variance** = (1,900 x 2 = 3,800 standard hours, vs 3,700 actual) x £10.00 = (3,800-3,700) x 10 = **£1,000 favourable**. **Sales price variance** = (44.50 - 45.00) x 1,900 = **£950 adverse**. **Sales volume variance** (budgeted sales 2,000 units) = (1,900 - 2,000) x £3.00 = **£300 adverse**. Interpreting these: the adverse material variances and adverse sales variances suggest either a poor buying/pricing decision or a quality issue causing more material usage; the favourable labour variances suggest the workforce worked faster and/or was paid less than standard that period -- worth investigating whether faster working affected quality.
