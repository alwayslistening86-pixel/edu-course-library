# S10_FA_Accruals_Prepayments_and_Receivables - Lesson: Accruals, prepayments, irrecoverable debts and allowances for doubtful receivables

## Goal
The learner calculates and records accruals and prepayments of income and expenditure, and accounts for irrecoverable debts and allowances for doubtful receivables.

## Syllabus items taught here
- FA5.1 - Accruals and prepayments of income and expenditure: calculating and recording the adjustment, and reversing the prior period's accrual/prepayment
- FA5.2 - Irrecoverable debts and allowances for doubtful receivables: writing off, recovering a previously written-off debt, and specific vs general allowances

## How to teach this
Ask the learner: if a business pays a full year's insurance in advance, 4 months of which fall after the year end, how much of that payment is an expense for this year and how much is a prepayment? Have the learner attempt every calculation (depreciation, accruals/prepayments, irrecoverable debts, inventory valuation, trial balance/ETB adjustments, sole trader and partnership financial statements, profitability ratios, incomplete records, FIFO/LIFO/AVCO, overhead absorption, marginal vs absorption costing, budgets, variances, breakeven, limiting factors, cash flow forecasts, VAT returns, PAYE/NIC and statutory payments) with full workings before checking the model answer -- these are computer-marked numeric-entry and multi-task items in the real AAT assessment, so exact figures matter. Every numeric example in this course was computed and verified in Python when the course was built. UK VAT is taken at the standard rate of 20% (reduced rate 5%, zero rate 0%) and the VAT registration threshold at £90,000 (deregistration £88,000) throughout, and payroll figures use the 2026/27 tax year -- check gov.uk for the current rates if they may have changed. AAT's assessments mix objective-test items (single- and multiple-select, numeric gap-fill, drag-and-drop/matching) with multi-task, human-marked written/journal-entry questions on Business Awareness; this course's items mirror the same calculation-and-entry style using clearly marked short-answer and multiple-choice items.

#### FA5.1 Accruals and prepayments of income and expenditure: calculating and recording the adjustment, and reversing the prior period's accrual/prepayment
**Accruals and prepayments** apply the accruals principle: the amount recognised in the statement of profit or loss is the amount that relates to the period, which can differ from the amount actually paid or received during the period. An **accrual** is an expense incurred (or income earned) but not yet paid (or received) by the period end -- recognised as a **liability** (accrued expense) or **asset** (accrued income). A **prepayment** is an amount paid (or received) in advance of the period it relates to -- recognised as an **asset** (prepaid expense) or **liability** (income received in advance/deferred income). Accounting software can automate recurring accrual/prepayment entries. At the start of the next period, the prior period's accrual or prepayment is **reversed**, so the cycle restarts correctly. *Worked example -- prepayment:* a business pays £3,600 on 1 November for 6 months' insurance (to 30 April), with a 31 December year end. 2 months (Nov, Dec) relate to this period; 4 months (Jan-Apr) are a prepayment: prepayment = £3,600 x 4/6 = **£2,400**; expense for the period = £3,600 - £2,400 = **£1,200**. *Worked example -- accrual:* electricity is billed quarterly in arrears; the last bill, £900, covered September to November. By the 31 December year end, one further month (December) has been used but not yet billed: accrual = £900 / 3 x 1 = **£300**.

#### FA5.2 Irrecoverable debts and allowances for doubtful receivables: writing off, recovering a previously written-off debt, and specific vs general allowances
Allowances for doubtful receivables also apply the accruals principle -- anticipating a *likely* future loss so it is matched to the period in which the risk arose, not the (uncertain, later) period it is confirmed. An **irrecoverable (bad) debt** is a specific debt now considered definitely uncollectable -- written off by debiting **irrecoverable debts expense** and crediting **receivables** (removing it from the receivables balance and the specific customer's account). If a previously written-off debt is later recovered, the recovery is recorded as income (the reverse entries, or directly to irrecoverable debts recovered income). An **allowance for doubtful receivables** anticipates debts that are not yet definitely bad, but are considered at risk: a **specific allowance** targets named, identified doubtful balances; a **general allowance** applies a percentage to the remaining (good) receivables balance as a general estimate of risk. Increasing the allowance is charged as an expense; decreasing it (if risk has reduced) is credited as income; only the **change** in the allowance from one period to the next affects that period's profit -- the closing allowance itself is deducted from receivables on the statement of financial position. *Worked example:* receivables stand at £150,000; a specific £4,000 debt is written off as irrecoverable, and a general allowance of 2% is then required on the remaining balance. Remaining receivables = £150,000 - £4,000 = £146,000; new allowance = £146,000 x 2% = **£2,920**. If the opening allowance was £2,000, the increase charged to profit this period is £2,920 - £2,000 = **£920** (in addition to the £4,000 irrecoverable debts expense).
