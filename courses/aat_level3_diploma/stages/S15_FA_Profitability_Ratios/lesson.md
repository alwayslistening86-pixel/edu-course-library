# S15_FA_Profitability_Ratios - Lesson: Calculating and interpreting profitability ratios

## Goal
The learner calculates ROCE, gross profit margin, net profit margin and the expense/sales revenue percentage, and interprets what changes in these ratios might mean.

## How to teach this
Ask the learner: two businesses both have a 30% gross profit margin, but one has a 5% net profit margin and the other 15%. What does that difference most likely tell you? Have the learner attempt every calculation (depreciation, accruals/prepayments, irrecoverable debts, inventory valuation, trial balance/ETB adjustments, sole trader and partnership financial statements, profitability ratios, incomplete records, FIFO/LIFO/AVCO, overhead absorption, marginal vs absorption costing, budgets, variances, breakeven, limiting factors, cash flow forecasts, VAT returns, PAYE/NIC and statutory payments) with full workings before checking the model answer -- these are computer-marked numeric-entry and multi-task items in the real AAT assessment, so exact figures matter. #### FA8.1 Calculating profitability ratios: ROCE, gross profit margin, net profit margin and the expense/sales revenue percentage
Profitability ratios relate profit to revenue or to the capital used to generate it, making performance comparable across periods or businesses of different sizes. **Return on capital employed (ROCE)** = Net profit / Capital employed x 100 -- capital employed is broadly total equity plus any long-term (non-current) liabilities, i.e. the total long-term funds invested in the business; it shows how efficiently the business is generating profit from the funds invested in it. **Gross profit margin** = Gross profit / Revenue x 100 -- the proportion of revenue retained after covering the direct cost of sales; it links directly to **mark-up** (gross profit / cost of sales x 100), which is a different ratio expressing the same profit as a percentage of cost rather than of revenue (mark-up is always numerically higher than margin for the same figures, since cost of sales is smaller than revenue). **Net profit margin** = Net profit / Revenue x 100 -- the proportion of revenue left after *all* expenses, not just cost of sales. The **expense/sales revenue percentage** = a specific expense / Revenue x 100 -- used to see how a particular cost (e.g. wages) is trending as a proportion of revenue over time. *Worked example:* Revenue £150,000; gross profit £60,000; net profit £18,000; capital employed £120,000. Gross profit margin = 60,000/150,000 x 100 = **40%**; net profit margin = 18,000/150,000 x 100 = **12%**; ROCE = 18,000/120,000 x 100 = **15%**.

#### FA8.2 Interpreting profitability ratios: why they matter for planning/decisions/control, what causes them to change, and comparing them meaningfully
Because net profit = gross profit less operating expenses, and both feed from the statement of profit or loss, calculating ratios lets a user link performance across the statement of profit or loss and statement of financial position in one figure. Ratios support **planning, decision-making and control** -- e.g. a falling gross margin might prompt a business to review its pricing or its suppliers. Ratios can change because of: a genuine change in performance (e.g. costs rising faster than prices), a change in accounting policy (e.g. a different depreciation method, breaking **consistency** between periods), or one-off/unusual items distorting a single period. Whether a ratio is "better" or "worse" depends on what it is compared to: a **prior period** (trend over time), **industry benchmarks/standards** (how the business compares to similar businesses) or **different organisations** (though comparability is weaker if accounting policies, size or industry differ). **Professional scepticism** matters when interpreting ratios -- a single ratio in isolation can mislead; the underlying figures, any changes in accounting policy, and the wider business context should all be considered before drawing a conclusion.
