# S04_Benefits_and_Effects_of_Person_Centred_Values - Lesson: Benefits of person-centred values and the effects when they are not applied

## Goal
The learner can explain the benefits of applying person-centred values to providers and to service users, and analyse physical, intellectual, emotional and social effects when they are not applied.

## Syllabus items taught here
- R032-2.2a - Benefits for service providers of applying person-centred values: explain six benefits to providers
- R032-2.2b - Benefits for service users of having person-centred values applied: explain four benefits to service users
- R032-2.3a - Physical effects when person-centred values are not applied: explain physical harm caused by poor care
- R032-2.3b - Intellectual effects when person-centred values are not applied: explain intellectual harm caused by poor care
- R032-2.3c - Emotional effects when person-centred values are not applied: explain emotional harm caused by poor care
- R032-2.3d - Social effects when person-centred values are not applied: explain social harm and link the four PIES effects together

## How to teach this
Open with: 'What happens to a care home if staff follow no shared standards, and what happens to a resident who is never asked what she wants?' Then teach benefits as the mirror image of harms. The spec says the effects should be analysed and connections made between physical, intellectual, emotional and social (PIES). Teach the learner to write chains: one effect leads to another.

#### R032-2.2a Benefits for service providers of applying person-centred values
The spec lists six benefits for providers (the practitioners and organisations). It provides clear guidelines of the standards of care that should be given, so staff know what good care looks like. It improves job satisfaction, because staff see the positive difference they make and feel they are doing a good job. It maintains or improves quality of life (of the people they support, which is the purpose of the work). It supports the rights to choice and consultation, so the provider meets its duties. It supports service practitioners to develop their skills, for example through training and reflection. It enables the sharing of good practice between staff and settings. Exam tip: an example is needed. 'A care home that applies dignity gives a shared expectation, so new carers know to close curtains during personal care, which raises standards' earns more than 'it helps staff'.

#### R032-2.2b Benefits for service users of having the person-centred values applied
Four benefits for service users are named. It ensures standardisation of care, so every service user receives the same standard whichever member of staff is on duty. It improves the quality of care given, because care is tailored to their needs. It maintains or improves quality of life, for example by keeping them independent and connected. It supports service users to develop their strengths, for example a stroke patient encouraged to practise dressing regains independence. Link benefits to values: independence leads to strengths and self-esteem; individuality leads to better quality of care; choice leads to control. When the exam says 'explain how applying person-centred values benefits service users', state the value, the action, the benefit and its effect on health or wellbeing.

#### R032-2.3a Physical effects when person-centred values are not applied
Physical effects on a service user include pain if medication or treatment is not given, illness getting worse if care needs are ignored, malnutrition or illness because special dietary needs are not met (for example a coeliac patient given food containing gluten), dehydration due to no regular fluids, and injury (for example if moving and handling is rushed or a person who needs a walking aid is left without it). Teach the reasoning: values not applied (individuality, choice, respect) leads to needs not identified or ignored, which leads to physical harm. The spec says to apply examples across all settings, so practise one for a GP surgery, a hospital ward and a care home.

#### R032-2.3b Intellectual effects when person-centred values are not applied
Intellectual effects are lack of progress or skills development (for example a child with learning difficulties never given adapted activities), failure to achieve potential, loss of concentration and lack of mental stimulation (for example residents left in front of a television all day with no conversation, puzzles or activities). Link to values: if choice and individuality are ignored, activities do not match the person's interests, so they disengage. In exam answers, keep intellectual effects distinct from emotional ones. 'She is bored so her memory and concentration decline' is intellectual; 'she feels sad' is emotional.

#### R032-2.3c Emotional effects when person-centred values are not applied
Emotional effects include depression, feeling upset, low self-esteem or feeling inadequate, anger and frustration, and stress. Example: a patient spoken over by staff and not consulted may feel worthless and become angry because his opinion does not matter. Dignity and respect matter here: someone left exposed during personal care may feel humiliated and embarrassed, and lose self-esteem. Emotional effects can worsen physical ones: a depressed resident may eat less and lose weight. Have the learner produce a two-step chain for each effect rather than a list of feelings.

#### R032-2.3d Social effects when person-centred values are not applied
Social effects are feeling excluded, feeling lonely, lack of social interaction or poor social skills, and becoming withdrawn. Example: a deaf resident whose needs are not met at group activities stops joining in and eats alone, becoming lonely and withdrawn. Analyse connections across PIES: poor communication causes social exclusion, which causes low self-esteem (emotional), which reduces appetite (physical) and causes loss of interest (intellectual). The spec's breadth statement asks for analysing effects and making connections between the PIES areas, so the top answers do this explicitly with words such as 'as a result' and 'this could lead to'.
