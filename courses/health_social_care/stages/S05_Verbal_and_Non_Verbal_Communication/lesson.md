# S05_Verbal_and_Non_Verbal_Communication - Lesson: Verbal and non-verbal communication skills

## Goal
The learner can explain each verbal and non-verbal communication skill in the spec, say how and when to use it with service users, and state the benefit.

## Syllabus items taught here
- R032-3.1 - Verbal communication skills: explain nine verbal skills, how and when to use them and their benefits
- R032-3.2 - Non-verbal communication skills: explain non-verbal skills including positioning, space and body language

## How to teach this
Open with a role play: the learner explains a simple procedure to you in three ways: too fast in a flat voice with crossed arms, then well. Ask what changed and how you felt. Only then name the skills. The spec wants understanding of how and when each could be used and the benefits, so every skill needs three parts: what it is, when you would use it, and what it does for the service user.

#### R032-3.1 Verbal communication skills
The spec lists nine verbal skills. Adapting the type or method of communicating to meet the needs of the service user or situation (for example short sentences for a child, a quiet room for a distressed person). Clarity: speaking clearly and unambiguously, so instructions such as medication times are understood. Empathy: showing you understand and share feelings ('this must be worrying for you'), which builds trust. Patience: allowing time and repeating without frustration, vital with older people or those with learning disabilities. Using appropriate vocabulary: plain words and no medical jargon, so service users are reassured. Tone: warm and calm, not harsh or patronising. Volume: loud enough to be heard but not shouting at a person with hearing loss, and lowered for confidential matters. Pace: slow enough for the listener to take it in. Willingness to contribute to team working: sharing information at handovers and asking colleagues. Benefits include understanding, reassurance, trust, respect and safer care.

#### R032-3.2 Non-verbal communication skills
Non-verbal skills are also adapted to the service user or situation. Eye contact shows interest and honesty (though be aware some cultures find prolonged eye contact rude). Facial expressions show warmth, concern or reassurance; a smile can calm a nervous child. Gestures such as nodding or pointing can support words for someone who does not speak English. Positioning covers space, height and personal space: sitting at the same eye level as a wheelchair user or a child, and not standing over them; respecting personal space unless care requires closeness. Positive body language means no crossed arms or legs, open posture, leaning slightly forward. Sense of humour can put people at ease when used appropriately and sensitively. Benefits: the service user feels valued, respected and relaxed, and communication is clearer. Poor non-verbal communication, such as looking at a watch, tells a person they are unimportant.

## Explicitly not here
Active listening, special methods and the overall importance of communication (next stage).
