# S06_Active_Listening_Special_Methods_and_Why_Communication_Matters - Lesson: Active listening, special methods of communication and the importance of effective communication

## Goal
The learner can apply the active listening skills, match special methods to needs, and explain the impact of good and poor communication on service users.

## Syllabus items taught here
- R032-3.3 - Active listening: apply the active listening skills to a care situation
- R032-3.4 - Special methods of communication: match advocate, Braille, BSL, interpreters, Makaton and voice software to needs
- R032-3.5 - Importance of effective communication, and impact of poor communication: explain how good and poor communication affect service users

## How to teach this
Open with: 'Tell me about your favourite holiday for one minute. I will listen badly, then well.' Then ask which felt better and why. Build up to special methods with the question: 'How would you tell a Deaf patient that their appointment has been moved?'

#### R032-3.3 Active listening
Active listening is showing that you are hearing and understanding, not just waiting to talk. Spec skills: open, relaxed posture; eye contact and looking interested; nodding agreement; showing empathy and reflecting feelings ('you sound frightened about going home'); clarifying (asking questions to check you understood: 'do you mean the pain is worse at night?'); and summarising to show understanding of key points. It is used when taking a patient's history, when someone is upset or explains a need, or during a care plan review. Benefits: the service user feels valued and respected, gives more accurate information, so needs are properly identified, and trust grows. Listening links to consultation and to protecting people, because a person who feels listened to is more likely to disclose harm.

#### R032-3.4 Special methods of communication
Six special methods are named. An advocate speaks up for a person who cannot easily speak for themselves (for example someone with a learning disability or dementia at a care review), making sure their wishes are heard. Braille is a raised-dot reading system for people with sight loss, useful for medication labels or leaflets. British Sign Language (BSL) is a visual language with its own grammar for Deaf people. Interpreters translate between languages (or into BSL) so that someone who does not speak English can consult a GP or give consent. Makaton uses signs, symbols and speech to support people with learning or communication difficulties. Voice-activated software turns speech into text or commands, helpful for people with limited mobility or dexterity. For each, state who uses it, when and the benefit (independence, understanding, rights respected). Careful: the mark scheme does not credit sign language for someone who does not speak English.

#### R032-3.5 Importance of effective communication in health and social care settings
Effective communication supports person-centred values and rights: it brings empowerment (they can make informed choices), reassurance, feeling valued and respected, and trust. It helps meet needs (a person who can express pain gets treated) and protects rights, such as consultation and protection from harm. Impact of good communication: well-informed service users; actively listening to needs, concerns and opinions makes them feel valued and respected; appropriate vocabulary with no jargon aids understanding, so they feel reassured. Impact of poor communication: misunderstanding if information is not clearly explained; errors or danger to health due to inaccurate record keeping (wrong dose recorded); distress or upset if a service user feels patronised; and if speech is too fast the listener will not have time to take it in. Use these to build cause and effect chains for 6- and 8-mark answers.
