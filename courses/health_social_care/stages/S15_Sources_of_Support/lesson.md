# S15_Sources_of_Support - Lesson: R033 sources of support, practitioners and informal carers

## Goal
The learner can distinguish formal, informal and charity support, describe the roles of practitioners and informal carers, and match six ways of meeting needs to individuals.

## Syllabus items taught here
- R033-3.1a - Sources of support: formal, informal and charities: distinguish and give examples of the three kinds of support
- R033-3.1b - The roles of practitioners in providing support: describe what each practitioner does
- R033-3.1c - The roles of informal care givers: describe what family, friends and faith or cultural groups provide
- R033-3.1d - How practitioners meet individual needs: link the six ways of meeting needs to individuals

## How to teach this
Ask: 'If you broke your leg tomorrow, who would help you and in what way? Sort them into people who are paid or professional, family and friends, and organisations that run on donations.' Then name the categories. Encourage research habits: the learner should look at real services for a real place, not just lists.

#### R033-3.1a Sources of support: formal, informal and charities
Three sources are named. Formal support is provided by professionals and organisations, usually paid, regulated or statutory: hospitals, health centres, care homes, day centres, children's services, hospices, respite care and rehabilitation centres (addiction or injury). Informal support is unpaid help from people close to the individual: family and friends, and religion or culture (for example a faith community bringing meals). Charities are non-profit organisations that support specific groups: the spec gives Relate (relationship counselling), Gingerbread (single parents), Cruse (bereavement), Age UK (older people), Mind (mental health), and specialist charities. Discuss the trade-offs: formal support may be specialised but has waiting lists, informal support is immediate and personal but may lack training, charities fill gaps but depend on funding and availability. The spec's exemplification also says to consider whether provision is statutory or private, location, availability and costs.

#### R033-3.1b The roles of practitioners in providing support
Practitioners are trained professionals. The spec lists: GP (first point of contact, diagnoses, prescribes, refers), nurse (care, treatment, medicines, monitoring), midwife (care before, during and after birth), specialist doctor (consultant in a specific condition), physiotherapist (restores movement and mobility after injury or illness), dietician (advice on diet and nutrition for health conditions or weight), social worker (assesses needs and arranges support, protects vulnerable people), counsellor (talking therapy for emotional problems, grief or stress), occupational therapist (helps people carry out daily tasks and adapt their home with equipment), health care worker (personal care and basic health support) and charity workers (advice, practical and emotional support). For each, know one need they meet, for example a dietician for weight gain, a counsellor for grief, a social worker for arranging home care.

#### R033-3.1c The roles of informal care givers in providing support
Informal care givers are unpaid, usually family, friends and neighbours, sometimes people in a faith or cultural community. Roles: emotional support (listening, reassurance, companionship); practical help (shopping, cooking, cleaning, transport to appointments, childcare); personal care (help with washing or dressing); helping with money or forms; providing continuity and knowing the person well, so they can speak up for the person's preferences; and giving religious or cultural support. They are available at all hours, but may become exhausted (carer stress), lack training, or be affected by their own needs, which is why respite care and support for carers exist. In a recommendation, informal support is often combined with formal support, for example a daughter driving her father to physiotherapy.

#### R033-3.1d How practitioners meet individual needs
The spec names six ways. Enable or promote independence: giving equipment, teaching skills, adaptations, so the person can do things themselves (occupational therapist supplying a raised toilet seat). Medical or mental health support: diagnosis, treatment, medication, therapy (GP, nurse, counsellor, psychiatrist). Care support: help with daily living such as washing, dressing and meals (care workers, home care). Respite care: giving the usual carer a break, temporarily caring for the person at a day centre or short residential stay. Financial support: advice on benefits, debt and budgeting, or benefit payments. Advice and guidance: information and help to make decisions (for example a social worker or charity helpline). Practise by giving a need and asking the learner to name the way and the provider, and explain why it fits.
