# S20_R035_Delivering_and_Evaluating_a_Campaign - Lesson: R035 Topic Area 4: delivering a campaign and evaluating own performance

## Goal
The learner can describe how to introduce and deliver a campaign, collect feedback, and evaluate their own planning, communication and engagement using feedback and self-reflection, suggesting improvements.

## Syllabus items taught here
- R035-4.1a - Delivering a campaign: introducing it: welcome and settle the audience
- R035-4.1b - Delivering a campaign: delivering the content: communicate clearly, support and encourage participation
- R035-4.1c - Delivering a campaign: collecting feedback: collect feedback on own performance using an appropriate method
- R035-4.2a - Evaluating own performance: feedback, self-reflection, strengths and weaknesses: review planning, communication and engagement
- R035-4.2b - Evaluating own performance: suggesting improvements: say what you would do differently and why

## How to teach this
Role-play a 60-second campaign introduction with the learner, then critique it together against the descriptors below. This is knowledge and skill teaching only; the learner's own delivery and evaluation happen in the live, teacher-observed set assignment, which the tutor cannot script or stand in for.

#### R035-4.1a Delivering a campaign: introducing it
A good introduction welcomes the audience, states clearly what the session is about and why it matters to them, and settles the group so they are ready to engage (for example, asking everyone to put phones away, or a quick attention-getting question). Introductions should be brief, confident and match the tone to the audience (more formal for adults, more playful for young children). A weak introduction rushes in without context, mumbles, or fails to get the group's attention before starting the main content, which makes the rest of the delivery harder.

#### R035-4.1b Delivering a campaign: delivering the content
Delivering the content means communicating clearly (appropriate pace, volume, and language for the audience, avoiding jargon), supporting participants (checking understanding, answering questions, helping anyone who is struggling) and encouraging participation (asking questions, inviting responses, using the engagement methods planned earlier). Good delivery keeps to time, stays on the planned content, and adapts if the audience is not responding as expected, for example simplifying an explanation if faces look confused. Non-verbal communication skills from earlier stages (eye contact, tone, body language) apply directly here.

#### R035-4.1c Delivering a campaign: collecting feedback
During or immediately after delivery, the deliverer collects feedback using the method chosen at the planning stage (questionnaire, verbal feedback, rating scale or observation checklist). Collecting feedback well means giving the audience enough time to respond, asking clearly, and recording responses accurately and honestly, including negative feedback, since this is what makes the later evaluation genuine rather than one-sided. The OCR guidance for the set assignment is clear that the teacher must observe delivery and complete an individualised observation record, so feedback collection sits alongside, not instead of, that formal observation.

#### R035-4.2a Evaluating own performance: feedback, self-reflection, strengths and weaknesses
Evaluation uses both the feedback collected from the audience and the deliverer's own self-reflection. A structured evaluation reviews three areas in turn: planning (did the aims, timescale, resources and communication method work as intended?), communication skills (was delivery clear, well-paced and engaging?) and how individuals were engaged (did the chosen engagement methods actually hold attention and encourage participation?). For each area, the learner states a specific strength (with evidence, such as a feedback comment or an observed reaction) and a specific weakness, rather than a general 'it went well' or 'it went badly'.

#### R035-4.2b Evaluating own performance: suggesting improvements
Improvement suggestions must follow logically from the weaknesses already identified, stating what would be done differently and why it would help, for example: 'I would use a shorter video clip because two participants said their attention wandered during the five-minute clip; a two-minute clip would keep engagement higher.' Suggestions should make full use of the feedback collected, referring to specific comments or patterns in the responses rather than ignoring them. OCR's assessment guidance is explicit that this evaluation must be the learner's own written work, produced without the teacher leading them through the process of evaluating.

## Explicitly not here
Writing the assessed evidence for Task 3 and Task 4 (a later stage covers the evidence requirements). Planning strands, covered in the previous stage.
