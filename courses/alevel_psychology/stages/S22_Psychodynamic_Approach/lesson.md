#### APP5 The psychodynamic approach (the unconscious, id/ego/superego, defence mechanisms, psychosexual stages)
**The role of the unconscious.** **Freud** proposed the mind is divided into the conscious (thoughts we are currently aware of), the preconscious (thoughts/memories not currently in awareness but accessible with effort) and, most importantly, the **unconscious** -- a vast reservoir of biological drives/instincts, and repressed, threatening or traumatic memories/desires, inaccessible to conscious awareness but nonetheless exerting a powerful influence on behaviour, sometimes 'leaking out' in disguised forms such as dreams or slips of the tongue ('Freudian slips').

**The structure of personality.** Freud proposed personality has three parts, in constant dynamic conflict. The **id** is present from birth, operates entirely unconsciously, and works on the **pleasure principle** -- seeking immediate gratification of primitive, instinctual drives (including aggressive and sexual drives) with no regard for consequences or morality. The **ego** develops around age two, and works on the **reality principle**, mediating between the demands of the id, the superego, and the constraints of external reality, using defence mechanisms (below) to manage the anxiety this conflict produces. The **superego**, formed around age five (at the resolution of the phallic stage's Oedipus/Electra complex, through identification with the same-sex parent), operates on the **morality principle**, representing internalised societal/parental moral standards, and produces feelings of guilt when its standards are violated.

**Defence mechanisms.** The ego uses unconscious defence mechanisms to protect itself from the anxiety produced by conflict between the id, superego and reality. **Repression**: forcing a distressing/threatening thought or memory out of conscious awareness entirely, into the unconscious. **Denial**: refusing to acknowledge/accept the reality of an unpleasant or threatening piece of information. **Displacement**: redirecting an emotion (typically an unacceptable one, e.g. anger) from its true, often threatening source onto a safer, less threatening substitute target (e.g. someone angry at their boss shouting at a family member instead, related also to Adorno's scapegoating concept, see SI6).

**Psychosexual stages.** Freud proposed personality develops through five fixed stages in childhood, each focused on a different area of pleasure-seeking (an erogenous zone); unresolved conflict at any stage can produce **fixation**, leaving a lasting mark on adult personality. **Oral stage** (0-1 year): focus on the mouth (sucking, biting); fixation associated with, e.g., smoking or nail-biting in adulthood. **Anal stage** (1-3 years): focus on toilet-training/bowel control; fixation associated with either an anal-retentive (obsessively tidy, controlling) or anal-expulsive (disorganised, messy) adult personality. **Phallic stage** (3-6 years): focus on the genitals; involves the **Oedipus complex** (boys) or **Electra complex** (girls) -- unconscious desire for the opposite-sex parent and rivalry with the same-sex parent, resolved through identification with the same-sex parent, which is how the superego forms; fixation associated with narcissistic, reckless or (in some accounts) confused adult personality traits. **Latency stage** (6 years to puberty): earlier conflicts are largely dormant/repressed, sexual urges are relatively inactive. **Genital stage** (puberty onward): sexual desires become conscious and are directed, if development has proceeded successfully, toward mature, age-appropriate romantic/sexual relationships. Evaluation: the psychodynamic approach was hugely influential historically and introduced enduringly useful concepts (e.g. the unconscious mind, the role of childhood experience in adult personality); however, it is heavily criticised as **unfalsifiable/unscientific** (Popper) -- key concepts like the unconscious and the Oedipus complex are extremely difficult to objectively test or disprove -- and much of Freud's evidence came from a small number of detailed but idiosyncratic case studies (e.g. 'Little Hans'), raising concerns about subjectivity, researcher bias in interpretation, and limited generalisability.
