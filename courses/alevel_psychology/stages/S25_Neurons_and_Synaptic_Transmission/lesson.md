#### BIO2 Neurons (sensory, relay, motor) and synaptic transmission
**Neurons.** Neurons are specialised nerve cells that transmit electrical and chemical signals throughout the nervous system; there are estimated to be around 100 billion in the human nervous system, about 80% located in the brain. **Sensory neurons** carry nerve impulses from sensory receptors (e.g. in the eyes, skin) toward the CNS, converting sensory stimulation into neural impulses; they have long dendrites and relatively short axons. **Relay neurons** (also called interneurons) connect sensory neurons to motor (or other relay) neurons, and are found mainly within the CNS itself, allowing sensory and motor neurons to communicate; they have short dendrites and either short or long axons, and make up the vast majority (around 97%) of all neurons. **Motor neurons** carry nerve impulses from the CNS to effectors (muscles and glands), causing them to act (e.g. muscle contraction); they have short dendrites and long axons. Each neuron has a **cell body** (containing the nucleus), **dendrites** (branch-like extensions that receive signals from other neurons) and an **axon** (a long fibre along which the electrical impulse travels, often covered in a fatty **myelin sheath** that insulates the axon and speeds up transmission of the impulse; gaps in this sheath, called the **nodes of Ranvier**, allow the impulse to 'jump' between them, further speeding transmission).

**Synaptic transmission.** Neurons do not physically touch; a small gap, the **synapse**, separates the **presynaptic neuron** (sending the signal) from the **postsynaptic neuron** (receiving it). When an electrical impulse (action potential) reaches the end of the presynaptic neuron (the presynaptic terminal), it triggers the release of chemical messengers called **neurotransmitters** (stored in synaptic vesicles) into the synaptic gap; these diffuse across the gap and bind to specific receptors on the postsynaptic neuron's membrane, which converts the chemical signal back into an electrical one in the receiving neuron, allowing the impulse to continue. Different neurotransmitters have specialised functions (e.g. acetylcholine, involved in muscle action; serotonin, involved in mood; dopamine, involved in reward/movement -- see PSY4 and SCZ2). Neurotransmitters can have an **excitatory** effect (increasing the likelihood the postsynaptic neuron will 'fire', i.e. generate its own action potential, e.g. adrenaline) or an **inhibitory** effect (decreasing this likelihood, making the postsynaptic neuron less likely to fire, e.g. serotonin, GABA). Whether a postsynaptic neuron actually fires depends on **summation**: the net effect of all the excitatory and inhibitory inputs it receives simultaneously from multiple presynaptic neurons -- if the sum of excitatory postsynaptic potentials exceeds the sum of inhibitory postsynaptic potentials by enough to reach a threshold, the neuron fires.
