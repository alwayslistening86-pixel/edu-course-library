# S37_Reliability_and_Validity - Lesson: Reliability and validity

## Goal
The learner distinguishes types of reliability (test-retest, inter-observer) and validity (face, concurrent, ecological, temporal), and explains ways of assessing and improving each.

## How to teach this
Ask: could a bathroom scale give you the exact same (wrong) reading every single time you step on it -- and if so, what does that tell you about the difference between a measure being consistent and a measure being accurate? Every named study, theorist and theory in this course (e.g. Asch, Milgram, Zimbardo, Adorno, Moscovici, Atkinson and Shiffrin, Baddeley and Hitch, Loftus and Palmer, Johnson and Scott, Yuille and Cutshall, Geiselman, Schaffer and Emerson, Lorenz, Harlow, Bowlby, Ainsworth, Van Ijzendoorn and Kroonenberg, Rutter, Hazan and Shaver, Beck, Ellis, Wiles, Wundt, Pavlov, Skinner, Bandura, Freud, Maslow, Rogers, Maguire, Sperry, Siffre, Gottesman, Bateson, Meehl, Buss, Walster, Kerckhoff and Davis, Thibaut and Kelley, Rusbult, Duck, McCutcheon, Dollard, Tinbergen, Wilson and Daly, Irwin and Cressey, Sykes) is a genuine, correctly attributed piece of psychology, checked against real psychological knowledge and AQA's own published subject content when this course was built -- present it as real research, never as illustrative fiction, and correct the learner explicitly if they misattribute a study or misstate a finding. #### RM8 Reliability (test-retest, inter-observer) and validity (face, concurrent, ecological, temporal)
**Reliability** refers to the consistency of a measure -- whether it produces the same result under the same conditions on repeated occasions. **Test-retest reliability**: the same test/measure is given to the same participants on two separate occasions, and the two sets of scores are compared/correlated; a strong positive correlation between the two occasions indicates good reliability. **Inter-observer/inter-rater reliability**: the extent to which two or more independent observers/raters, observing/scoring the same behaviour or material, agree with each other; assessed by correlating the two sets of scores/ratings, with a strong positive correlation (conventionally, a correlation coefficient of at least +0.80 is often used as an acceptable threshold) indicating good inter-observer reliability. Reliability can be improved by standardising instructions/procedures (test-retest), or by ensuring behavioural categories used by observers are clearly, precisely and unambiguously operationalised, along with adequate observer training (inter-observer).

**Validity** refers to whether a measure genuinely measures what it claims/intends to measure, and, more broadly for a whole study, whether its findings are legitimate/genuine and can be meaningfully generalised. **Face validity**: a basic, subjective check of whether a measure appears, 'on the face of it', to measure what it is supposed to measure (e.g. does a depression questionnaire's items look, at a glance, like they are genuinely asking about depression?). **Concurrent validity**: established by comparing a new measure's scores against scores from an existing, already well-established and validated measure of the same construct, taken by the same participants at around the same time; a strong correlation between the two indicates good concurrent validity. **Ecological validity**: the extent to which findings from a study can be generalised to other, real-world settings and situations beyond the specific one in which the research took place -- a highly artificial, controlled setting (e.g. a laboratory) often, though not always, has lower ecological validity than a more natural setting, though ecological validity is not simply the same thing as 'realism of the location' (a task itself can be unrealistic even in a naturalistic setting). **Temporal validity** (also called historical validity): the extent to which findings from a study, or a theory, remain true/applicable over time, rather than being specific to the particular historical period/social context in which the original research was conducted (e.g. some classic studies of conformity or gender roles may reflect the particular social attitudes of the era they were conducted in, limiting how far the findings hold today). Validity can be improved through, e.g., using a control group to rule out extraneous explanations, standardising procedures to reduce investigator effects, or using a covert design/reducing demand characteristics to increase the naturalness of participants' behaviour.
