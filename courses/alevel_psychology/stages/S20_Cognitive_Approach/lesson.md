## How to teach this
Ask: if you can't directly observe someone else's thoughts, how could a scientist ever study something as invisible as 'memory' or 'attention' rigorously? Every named study, theorist and theory in this course (e.g. Asch, Milgram, Zimbardo, Adorno, Moscovici, Atkinson and Shiffrin, Baddeley and Hitch, Loftus and Palmer, Johnson and Scott, Yuille and Cutshall, Geiselman, Schaffer and Emerson, Lorenz, Harlow, Bowlby, Ainsworth, Van Ijzendoorn and Kroonenberg, Rutter, Hazan and Shaver, Beck, Ellis, Wiles, Wundt, Pavlov, Skinner, Bandura, Freud, Maslow, Rogers, Maguire, Sperry, Siffre, Gottesman, Bateson, Meehl, Buss, Walster, Kerckhoff and Davis, Thibaut and Kelley, Rusbult, Duck, McCutcheon, Dollard, Tinbergen, Wilson and Daly, Irwin and Cressey, Sykes) is a genuine, correctly attributed piece of psychology, checked against real psychological knowledge and AQA's own published subject content when this course was built -- present it as real research, never as illustrative fiction, and correct the learner explicitly if they misattribute a study or misstate a finding. Research Methods (4.2.3, at least 25% of assessment across all three papers, plus a minimum 10% mathematical-skills requirement) is examined throughout, not only in its own stages -- sampling, experimental design and inferential statistics can appear applied to any topic area. Every numerical worked example (mean, median, mode, range, standard deviation, the sign test) was computed and verified with Python when this course was built, and the learner should always show full working for a calculation, since AQA credits correct method as well as a correct final answer. Extended-writing ('Discuss...'/'Evaluate...') questions are marked holistically against AQA's levels-of-response scheme (see `rubric.json`), not by a checklist of individual points -- teach the learner to build a structured AO1 (accurate knowledge) + AO3 (effective discussion/evaluation), and where the question is scenario-based, AO2 (explicit application to the scenario) response, not a list of disconnected facts.

#### APP3 The cognitive approach (internal mental processes, schemas, theoretical/computer models, cognitive neuroscience)
**The cognitive approach.** In contrast to behaviourism, the cognitive approach argues internal mental (cognitive) processes -- such as perception, attention, memory, language and thinking -- can and should be studied scientifically, even though they cannot be observed directly, by making inferences about them from observable behaviour (e.g. inferring how memory works from patterns in what people can and cannot recall, as in the multi-store model, see MEM1). The approach uses **theoretical models** (simplified, often diagrammatic representations of a mental process, e.g. the multi-store model or working memory model) to represent how information might flow through a cognitive system. The **computer analogy** compares the mind to a computer, in terms of processes such as input, storage/coding, and output, and treats the human mind as an **information processor**; this influenced the development of computer models (programs designed to genuinely process information in a way that mimics human cognitive processing, allowing hypotheses about human cognition to be tested computationally) and, ultimately, artificial intelligence research. **Schemas** are packages/clusters of ideas and information developed through experience, acting as a mental framework that helps organise and interpret incoming information efficiently -- schemas become more detailed and elaborate with age/experience, and can also help explain distortion in cognitive processing, e.g. in perception (see, in AQA's broader psychology curriculum, applications such as Bartlett's reconstructive memory research) and in errors of judgement. **Cognitive neuroscience** is the field combining cognitive and biological approaches, studying the neurological/biological structures that underpin cognitive processes, made possible by modern brain-scanning technology (see BIO6); it has, for example, allowed researchers to identify specific brain regions associated with particular cognitive functions (e.g. localising areas involved in memory processing) and has enabled the objective mapping of neural correlates for cognitive processes previously studied only indirectly through behaviour. Evaluation: the cognitive approach has strong scientific credibility (using rigorous, controlled, replicable experimental methods) and has generated influential, well-evidenced theoretical models (see Paper 1's memory content); it also has valuable real-world application (e.g. the cognitive interview, CBT). However, it is criticised for relying substantially on inference rather than direct observation (mental processes are not directly observable, so models are necessarily somewhat abstract and simplified), and the computer analogy has been criticised for ignoring important influences on human cognition that a computer does not experience, such as emotion and motivation.
