# S50_Neural_Hormonal_and_Evolutionary_Explanations_of_Aggression - Lesson: Neural/hormonal mechanisms and ethological/evolutionary explanations of aggression

## Goal
The learner explains neural and hormonal mechanisms in aggression (limbic system, serotonin, testosterone, the MAOA gene) and the ethological and evolutionary explanations of aggression.

## How to teach this
Ask: could a single gene variant that affects how the brain breaks down certain neurotransmitters make someone genuinely more prone to violent, impulsive aggression? Every named study, theorist and theory in this course (e.g. Asch, Milgram, Zimbardo, Adorno, Moscovici, Atkinson and Shiffrin, Baddeley and Hitch, Loftus and Palmer, Johnson and Scott, Yuille and Cutshall, Geiselman, Schaffer and Emerson, Lorenz, Harlow, Bowlby, Ainsworth, Van Ijzendoorn and Kroonenberg, Rutter, Hazan and Shaver, Beck, Ellis, Wiles, Wundt, Pavlov, Skinner, Bandura, Freud, Maslow, Rogers, Maguire, Sperry, Siffre, Gottesman, Bateson, Meehl, Buss, Walster, Kerckhoff and Davis, Thibaut and Kelley, Rusbult, Duck, McCutcheon, Dollard, Tinbergen, Wilson and Daly, Irwin and Cressey, Sykes) is a genuine, correctly attributed piece of psychology, checked against real psychological knowledge and AQA's own published subject content when this course was built -- present it as real research, never as illustrative fiction, and correct the learner explicitly if they misattribute a study or misstate a finding. #### AGG1 Neural and hormonal mechanisms in aggression, and the ethological/evolutionary explanations
**Neural and hormonal mechanisms.** The **limbic system** (a set of interconnected brain structures, including the amygdala and hypothalamus) is closely involved in emotional responses, including aggression; the **amygdala** in particular is associated with processing emotionally significant stimuli, including threat, and research (e.g. brain-imaging studies comparing individuals with a history of violent behaviour against non-violent controls) has found abnormal amygdala activity/structure is associated with heightened aggressive responding. **Serotonin**: normally has a broadly calming, inhibitory effect on neural activity in areas of the brain (including parts of the limbic system) linked to impulsive and aggressive behaviour; lower levels of serotonin activity are consistently associated, across a range of studies, with reduced control over aggressive impulses, potentially through reduced inhibition of neural mechanisms that would otherwise trigger an aggressive response to provocation. **Testosterone**: a hormone present in higher concentrations in males, long theorised to increase aggression, partly via influencing amygdala reactivity to threat/provocation cues, and partly via broader effects on dominance-seeking behaviour; research evidence includes studies finding a positive correlation between testosterone levels and self-reported/observed aggression in some (though not all) samples, and evidence from studies of prison populations sometimes finding higher testosterone levels among inmates convicted of more violent offences, though the overall research picture is genuinely mixed and correlational, making a straightforward causal conclusion difficult. **The MAOA gene** (sometimes informally called the 'warrior gene' in media coverage) codes for the enzyme monoamine oxidase A, which regulates the breakdown of neurotransmitters including serotonin; a particular low-activity variant of this gene (**MAOA-L**) has been associated, in some research, with reduced serotonin regulation and increased aggressive/antisocial behaviour, particularly in individuals who also experienced early childhood maltreatment (an interaction between genetic vulnerability and environmental factors, consistent with a diathesis-stress-type interactionist account, see ID3), rather than the gene variant alone reliably or inevitably predicting aggression.

**Ethological and evolutionary explanations.** The **ethological explanation** views aggression as an innate, adaptive behaviour that has evolved because it has survival value (e.g. securing resources, territory, status or access to mates), studied particularly through observing aggression in non-human animals in their natural environment. **Innate releasing mechanisms (IRMs)** are proposed as built-in neural circuits/mechanisms, triggered automatically by a specific environmental stimulus (a 'sign stimulus'), which produce a highly stereotyped, predictable behavioural sequence called a **fixed action pattern (FAP)** -- **Tinbergen's** research with male stickleback fish found that the sight of the colour red (resembling another male stickleback's red underbelly during breeding season) reliably triggered a fixed, stereotyped aggressive display, even toward a crude, non-fish-shaped model, provided it had a red underside, demonstrating that a specific, simple sign stimulus can trigger a fixed, largely automatic aggressive response. **Lorenz** additionally observed that aggression between members of the same species is often **ritualistic** rather than actually lethal (e.g. displays of threat/dominance, without direct, sustained physical harm, and with clear appeasement/submission signals that end the aggressive encounter), which Lorenz argued serves an important adaptive function -- resolving conflict (e.g. over territory, mates, dominance status) while minimising the risk of serious injury or death to either competing individual, which would otherwise reduce the species' own overall survival/reproductive success. **Evolutionary explanations of human aggression** similarly propose that aggression, or a readiness for aggression, has been naturally selected because it increased ancestral humans' chances of survival and reproductive success, e.g. via **sexual jealousy** (Wilson and Daly's research proposing that male sexual jealousy and associated 'mate-guarding' aggressive behaviours evolved because of the specific evolutionary problem, unique to males, of paternity uncertainty -- a male can never be completely certain a partner's offspring are genetically his own, creating evolutionary pressure favouring jealous, aggressive mate-guarding behaviour to reduce infidelity risk) and via aggression directed at rivals to gain or defend access to status/resources important for attracting mates. Evaluation: ethological research (e.g. Tinbergen's sticklebacks) provides controlled experimental evidence for IRMs/FAPs, but generalising from a non-human species (with a relatively fixed, simple, largely innate behavioural repertoire) to complex, culturally and socially shaped human aggression is questionable; evolutionary explanations of human aggression (e.g. sexual jealousy) have some supporting evidence (e.g. cross-cultural surveys finding sexual jealousy is a commonly reported trigger for male-perpetrated relationship violence in various cultures) but, like other evolutionary explanations (see REL1), are criticised for being difficult to falsify rigorously and for risking the appearance of excusing or naturalising harmful behaviour as simply 'evolved'.
