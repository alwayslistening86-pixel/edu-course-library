# S13_Strange_Situation_Cultural_Variations_and_Disruption - Lesson: The Strange Situation, cultural variations, disruption of attachment, and later relationships

## Goal
The learner describes Ainsworth's Strange Situation and the resulting attachment types, cultural variations in attachment, Bowlby's maternal deprivation theory, the Romanian orphan studies, and the influence of early attachment on later relationships.

#### ATT7 Ainsworth's Strange Situation and types of attachment
**Ainsworth's Strange Situation.** **Ainsworth (1970)** developed a controlled, structured observational procedure to assess the quality/type of an infant's attachment to their caregiver, involving a sequence of episodes in an unfamiliar room: the caregiver and infant are introduced to the room; the infant explores while the caregiver is present; a stranger enters and interacts with the infant; the caregiver leaves (separation), leaving the infant with the stranger; the caregiver returns (reunion) and the stranger leaves; the caregiver leaves again, leaving the infant completely alone; the stranger returns; the caregiver returns for a final reunion. Behaviour is scored on **proximity-seeking**, **exploration and secure-base behaviour** (using the caregiver as a base from which to explore), **stranger anxiety**, **separation anxiety**, and **reunion behaviour**. Ainsworth identified three attachment types (a fourth, disorganised attachment, was added later by Main and Solomon). **Secure attachment (Type B, roughly 60-75% of infants in Western samples)**: moderate stranger/separation anxiety, and are easily comforted at reunion; associated with caregiver sensitivity/responsiveness. **Insecure-avoidant attachment (Type A, roughly 20-25%)**: low stranger/separation anxiety, and show little interest at reunion, tending to avoid or ignore the caregiver. **Insecure-resistant attachment (Type C, roughly 3%)**: high stranger/separation anxiety, and show conflicting reunion behaviour (seeking then resisting comfort/contact). Evaluation: the procedure has good reliability (independent observers typically agree closely on the attachment type shown, and test-retest reliability is fairly high) and has real predictive value, but is criticised for potentially lacking full ecological validity (an artificial, unfamiliar setting) and for reflecting only the child's relationship with one specific caregiver, not necessarily an overall or fixed trait of the child.

#### ATT8 Cultural variations in attachment (Van Ijzendoorn and Kroonenberg)
**Cultural variations in attachment.** **Van Ijzendoorn and Kroonenberg (1988)** conducted a meta-analysis, combining findings from 32 Strange Situation studies across 8 countries, to see whether attachment types varied by culture. They found secure attachment (Type B) was the most common classification in every country studied, suggesting some degree of universality to Bowlby's claims about the innate, biological basis of attachment; however, the *rates* of insecure types varied noticeably by culture -- insecure-avoidant attachment was relatively more common in Western European countries (e.g. Germany, where some interpreted this as reflecting a cultural value placed on independence/non-clinginess in child-rearing), while insecure-resistant attachment was relatively more common in Israel and Japan (interpreted by some as linked to less frequent, more collective/communal child-rearing practices, or to less experience of strangers). Notably, variation *within* a single country/culture was found to be greater than variation *between* different countries/cultures, suggesting attachment type is influenced considerably by factors beyond a broad national culture, such as social class, specific childcare practices, or individual differences. Evaluation: the very large combined sample gives high reliability and greater generalisability than a single small study, but the Strange Situation itself may be an example of an **imposed etic** -- a technique/set of assumptions developed in one culture (Western, US/UK) being applied to and assumed to have the same meaning in a different culture, when actually the same infant behaviour (e.g. an infant who does not appear distressed at separation) might reflect different cultural norms of child-rearing rather than a genuinely different quality of underlying attachment, so cross-cultural comparisons using this tool should be treated cautiously.

#### ATT9 Bowlby's theory of maternal deprivation and the Romanian orphan studies (Rutter)
**Bowlby's maternal deprivation theory and Romanian orphan studies.** **Bowlby (1951)** proposed that prolonged early separation/deprivation from the primary attachment figure (maternal deprivation), during a critical period in early childhood, has serious, potentially permanent, negative long-term consequences for emotional and intellectual development. His **44 Thieves Study** compared 44 juvenile delinquent adolescents (many of whom had experienced early separation from their mother) against a non-delinquent control group, and found a much higher rate of what Bowlby termed 'affectionless psychopathy' (an inability to feel guilt or genuine emotional concern for others) among the delinquent group with early separation experience -- though this study has been criticised for potential researcher bias (Bowlby himself both diagnosed the affectionless psychopathy and knew participants' separation histories) and for only being correlational, not proving separation caused later delinquency. More rigorous, later evidence for the effects of severe early deprivation came from research into children raised in severely depriving Romanian orphanages under the Ceausescu regime, adopted into UK families -- the **English and Romanian Adoptees (ERA) study, Rutter et al. (1998, 2007, 2010)**, followed over 100 such children, comparing their developmental outcomes with UK-adopted children who had not experienced this early institutional deprivation. Children adopted before six months showed, on average, close to normal levels of intellectual and emotional/attachment development by later childhood; children adopted after six months (particularly after age two) showed significantly higher rates of a distinctive pattern called **disinhibited attachment** (indiscriminately friendly, attention-seeking behaviour even toward unfamiliar adults, with no real stranger anxiety), along with lower average intellectual development that improved less fully over time -- providing strong support for the existence of a sensitive period, and dose-response evidence (the longer the deprivation, the more severe the lasting effects), though it remains debated how far these findings (severe institutional deprivation in an extreme historical setting) generalise to the ordinary, brief, everyday separations Bowlby's original theory was also sometimes taken to be warning against.

#### ATT10 The influence of early attachment on childhood and adult relationships (Hazan and Shaver)
**The influence of early attachment on later relationships.** Bowlby's internal working model (see ATT6) predicts that the quality of a child's earliest attachment relationship should be carried forward, via **continuity of relationship style**, to shape their later relationships in childhood and adulthood. Evidence broadly supports this: research into childhood friendships and romantic relationships in adolescence has found securely attached infants tend to go on to form better-quality friendships and be less likely to bully or be bullied in later childhood, while insecurely attached infants show poorer outcomes on these measures. In adult romantic relationships, **Hazan and Shaver (1987)** used a 'love quiz' published in a US newspaper, analysing responses relating current relationship experiences and beliefs about love to participants' self-reported early attachment type, and found a clear correspondence: adults classified as securely attached in infancy reported the most loving, trusting and enduring adult romantic relationships, while insecure-avoidant adults reported greater fear of intimacy/closeness, and insecure-resistant adults reported more jealous, obsessive relationship patterns -- consistent with the internal working model shaping expectations of, and behaviour within, adult romantic attachments. The internal working model has also been linked to a person's own later parenting style, with some (though not universal) evidence that adults who were insecurely attached as infants are somewhat more likely to have insecurely attached children themselves, suggesting attachment patterns can, to some degree, pass across generations. Evaluation: much of this evidence, including Hazan and Shaver's, is correlational and relies on retrospective self-report of a person's own infant attachment type (which they cannot directly and accurately recall or know), a significant methodological weakness; other factors in later life (e.g. subsequent relationship experiences, temperament) clearly also shape adult relationship quality, meaning early attachment is best seen as one influential but not fully deterministic factor.
