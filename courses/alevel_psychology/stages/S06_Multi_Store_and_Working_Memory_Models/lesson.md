# S06_Multi_Store_and_Working_Memory_Models - Lesson: The multi-store and working memory models

## Goal
The learner describes and evaluates the multi-store model of memory (sensory register, STM, LTM; coding, capacity, duration) and the working memory model (central executive, phonological loop, visuo-spatial sketchpad, episodic buffer).

## How to teach this
Ask: can you hold a ten-digit phone number in your head AND do mental arithmetic at the same time without losing the number -- and if that's hard, what does that suggest short-term memory is actually made of? Every named study, theorist and theory in this course (e.g. Asch, Milgram, Zimbardo, Adorno, Moscovici, Atkinson and Shiffrin, Baddeley and Hitch, Loftus and Palmer, Johnson and Scott, Yuille and Cutshall, Geiselman, Schaffer and Emerson, Lorenz, Harlow, Bowlby, Ainsworth, Van Ijzendoorn and Kroonenberg, Rutter, Hazan and Shaver, Beck, Ellis, Wiles, Wundt, Pavlov, Skinner, Bandura, Freud, Maslow, Rogers, Maguire, Sperry, Siffre, Gottesman, Bateson, Meehl, Buss, Walster, Kerckhoff and Davis, Thibaut and Kelley, Rusbult, Duck, McCutcheon, Dollard, Tinbergen, Wilson and Daly, Irwin and Cressey, Sykes) is a genuine, correctly attributed piece of psychology, checked against real psychological knowledge and AQA's own published subject content when this course was built -- present it as real research, never as illustrative fiction, and correct the learner explicitly if they misattribute a study or misstate a finding. #### MEM1 The multi-store model of memory (sensory register, STM, LTM; coding, capacity, duration)
**The multi-store model.** **Atkinson and Shiffrin (1968)** proposed memory consists of three separate, linear stores. The **sensory register** holds a very brief, modality-specific, high-capacity trace of sensory input (e.g. iconic memory for vision) lasting under half a second, unless attended to, in which case information passes into short-term memory. **Short-term memory (STM)** has limited capacity (Miller (1956) suggested 7 +/- 2 items, or 'chunks') and limited duration (Peterson and Peterson (1959) found recall of trigrams fell to about 10% after 18 seconds when rehearsal was prevented by counting backwards) and is coded mainly **acoustically** (by sound). **Long-term memory (LTM)** has a potentially unlimited capacity and duration (information can last a lifetime) and is coded mainly **semantically** (by meaning) -- evidence from Baddeley (1966) found participants made more errors recalling semantically similar word lists from LTM than acoustically similar lists, and the reverse pattern for STM, supporting different coding in each store. Information is transferred from STM to LTM through **rehearsal** (particularly maintenance rehearsal -- repeating information to keep it active in STM long enough to transfer it). Evaluation: supporting evidence includes case studies of amnesia (e.g. patient HM, who lost the ability to form new long-term memories after brain surgery but retained a normal STM, suggesting STM and LTM are genuinely separate systems), but the model is criticised as oversimplified (treating STM and LTM as single, unitary stores, when evidence -- e.g. from the working memory model and from case studies like KF, who had a selective STM deficit for auditory but not visual information -- suggests STM at least is not a single store), and for underplaying the role of active processing over mere rehearsal in transferring information to LTM.

#### MEM2 The working memory model (central executive, phonological loop, visuo-spatial sketchpad, episodic buffer)
**The working memory model.** **Baddeley and Hitch (1974)** proposed that STM is not a single unitary store, but an active system made up of several components working together. The **central executive** has a supervisory, attention-directing role: it allocates tasks to the other 'slave systems' and has a very limited capacity, itself unable to process much information directly. The **phonological loop** processes auditory/verbal information, and has two sub-components: the **phonological store** (holds spoken words) and the **articulatory process** (allows sub-vocal repetition/'inner speech' rehearsal of that information, with a capacity limited to roughly what can be said in about two seconds). The **visuo-spatial sketchpad** processes visual and spatial information (e.g. mentally navigating a route), and Logie further subdivided it into the visual cache (visual data) and the inner scribe (spatial/movement information). The **episodic buffer**, added by Baddeley in 2000, integrates information from the other components (and from LTM) into a single coherent episode, and provides a limited-capacity store linking working memory to long-term memory -- added partly to account for the model's original inability to explain how information from different slave systems is combined into a single memory. Evaluation: supported by dual-task studies (e.g. participants find it much harder to perform two simultaneous verbal tasks, or two simultaneous visual tasks, than one of each -- since two tasks using the same slave system compete for its limited capacity, while tasks using different slave systems can be done concurrently) and by clinical case studies (e.g. patient KF, whose brain damage impaired his phonological loop -- poor immediate recall of digits read aloud -- while his visuo-spatial sketchpad remained largely intact, supporting the separation of the two slave systems); criticised for the vague, poorly specified role of the central executive (Baddeley himself acknowledged this), and evidence is drawn heavily from brain-damaged patients, whose damage is rarely 'clean' or limited to a single proposed component.
