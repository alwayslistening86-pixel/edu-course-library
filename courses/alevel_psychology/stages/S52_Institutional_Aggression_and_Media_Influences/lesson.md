#### AGG3 Institutional aggression in prisons, and media influences on aggression
**Institutional aggression in prisons.** Two broad, competing types of explanation exist for elevated rates of aggression within institutions such as prisons. **Dispositional explanations** propose that institutional aggression results primarily from the personal characteristics/attributes that individuals bring into the institution with them. The **importation model (Irwin and Cressey)** proposes that prisoners import pre-existing dispositions, beliefs, values, and behavioural norms (e.g. a pre-existing tendency toward violence, gang affiliations/identities, or norms learned in the outside community) into the prison environment, and that these imported characteristics -- not the prison situation itself -- substantially explain patterns of aggression/violence within the institution. **Situational explanations** propose instead that institutional aggression results primarily from features of the institutional environment/situation itself, regardless of the individual dispositions of those within it. The **deprivation model (Sykes)** proposes that the specific stressful, depriving conditions of imprisonment itself (e.g. deprivation of liberty, of autonomy/personal control, of material possessions/goods, of heterosexual relationships, and of personal security/safety from other prisoners) produce significant psychological stress and frustration, which can in turn lead to aggressive behaviour as prisoners compete for scarce resources/status or attempt to reassert some control/security within a fundamentally depriving, threatening environment -- this account is broadly consistent with, and can be linked to, situational explanations of institutional behaviour more generally, such as Zimbardo's Stanford Prison Experiment (see SI3), which similarly emphasised the power of the institutional situation over individual disposition. Evaluation: research evidence for both models exists (e.g. some studies finding pre-existing violent history/gang affiliation before imprisonment predicts in-prison violence, supporting importation, while other research finds aggression rates vary significantly between different prisons with similar populations but different physical/managerial conditions, supporting deprivation), suggesting both dispositional (imported) and situational (deprivation) factors likely contribute, in an interactionist way, rather than either model alone providing a complete explanation.

**Media influences on aggression.** Research has investigated whether exposure to aggression in media, particularly violent computer games, increases real-world aggressive behaviour/cognition. Longitudinal and experimental studies have generally found a positive, though often modest, association between playing violent computer games and subsequent aggressive behaviour/cognition (e.g. increased aggressive thoughts, and, in some studies, increased aggressive behaviour in a laboratory-based measure, following a session playing a violent rather than non-violent game), though establishing clear causation from correlational, real-world research remains difficult (e.g. it is possible that individuals already more prone to aggression are simply more likely to choose to play violent games in the first place -- a selection effect). Three mechanisms are proposed to explain how media violence might increase real-world aggression. **Desensitisation**: repeated exposure to violent media content is proposed to reduce the individual's normal physiological and psychological (emotional) response to violence over time (e.g. reduced physiological arousal, such as skin conductance response, when subsequently viewing violent content, found in some studies comparing habitual violent-game players against non-players), potentially making real-world violence seem more normal/acceptable and less personally distressing, so reducing an internal inhibition against committing or condoning it. **Disinhibition**: media violence, particularly where it is shown to be rewarded, justified, or has no negative consequences for the perpetrator within the fictional narrative, may weaken the normal social/moral constraints that usually inhibit aggressive behaviour in real life, making aggression seem a more socially acceptable, normalised response option than it otherwise would. **Cognitive priming**: exposure to violent media content is proposed to activate/'prime' aggression-related thoughts, scripts, and interpretive frameworks in memory (e.g. making an individual more likely to automatically, rapidly interpret an ambiguous social situation, such as being accidentally bumped into, as a deliberate provocation), increasing the readiness with which aggressive thoughts and behavioural scripts are drawn on and acted upon in a subsequent real, ambiguous social situation, even without the person consciously deciding to be more aggressive. Evaluation: this research area has real, important social-policy relevance (e.g. informing debate over media/game content classification and regulation), but findings are genuinely mixed and effect sizes in meta-analyses are typically modest, correlational research cannot fully rule out reverse causation/selection effects (already-aggressive individuals choosing violent media), and most laboratory experimental evidence uses relatively short-term exposure and artificial aggression measures, raising real questions about how well findings generalise to genuine, real-world, longer-term aggressive behaviour.
