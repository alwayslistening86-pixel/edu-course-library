#### ATT1 Caregiver-infant interactions (reciprocity, interactional synchrony) and Schaffer's stages of attachment
**Caregiver-infant interactions.** **Reciprocity** describes an interaction pattern in which caregiver and infant respond to each other, producing a turn-taking exchange (like a conversation), each responding to the signals/behaviour of the other -- from as early as two to three weeks, infants show alert phases where they signal readiness for interaction, and sensitive caregivers pick up on and respond to these signals (Feldman and Eidelman found mothers typically respond to about two-thirds of infant alertness). **Interactional synchrony** is when caregiver and infant coordinate their behaviour so that they mirror each other's actions and emotions, in a co-ordinated, almost simultaneous way (e.g. matching facial expressions or rhythms of movement). **Meltzoff and Moore (1977)** found infants as young as 2-3 weeks old imitated an adult's specific facial expressions (e.g. tongue protrusion) and hand gestures, providing evidence that interactional synchrony is present from a very early age, and is not simply learned social behaviour. These interactions are thought to lay the foundation for later attachment, though this is difficult to establish, since research typically observes infant behaviour (e.g. via filmed interactions, often observed by more than one trained observer, giving good inter-observer reliability) without being able to establish what the infant is actually thinking or intending, and many studies are conducted in a controlled, observed lab setting that may not fully reflect everyday interactions.

**Schaffer's stages of attachment.** **Schaffer and Emerson (1964)** studied 60 Glaswegian infants over their first 18 months (via mothers' diary reports of stranger and separation anxiety) and identified four stages. **The asocial stage** (first few weeks): the infant's behaviour toward objects and people is fairly similar, though they show some preference for familiar faces. **The indiscriminate attachment stage** (2-7 months): infants show a preference for people in general over inanimate objects, and enjoy the company of familiar people, but show no preference for a particular caregiver, showing no separation or stranger anxiety yet. **The specific attachment stage** (from about 7 months): the infant forms a strong attachment to one particular caregiver (usually the biological mother, though not necessarily), shown by separation anxiety when that specific person leaves and stranger anxiety around unfamiliar people; this person becomes the infant's **primary attachment figure**. **The multiple attachments stage** (from around 9 months onward): infants extend attachment behaviour to several other regular caregivers (secondary attachment figures, e.g. father, siblings, grandparents) -- within a month of forming their first specific attachment, most infants in Schaffer and Emerson's sample had formed multiple attachments.

#### ATT2 Multiple attachments and the role of the father
**Multiple attachments and the role of the father.** While Schaffer and Emerson found most infants form their primary/first specific attachment to the mother (with fathers becoming an important attachment figure only somewhat later for most infants in their sample -- about 75% had formed an attachment with the father by 18 months), research on the specific role of fathers has produced mixed findings on the *quality*, not just timing, of that role. Some research suggests fathers, when they are the primary caregiver, can adopt a nurturing, sensitive role very similar to that traditionally associated with mothers (Field's research found primary-caregiver fathers showed similarly high levels of interactional synchrony/smiling/imitating as primary-caregiver mothers), suggesting the father's specific role depends more on their level of responsiveness than on biological sex; other research emphasises a distinct role for fathers as secondary attachment figures, associated more with **stimulating, physical play** than the mothers' more soothing/caregiving role, contributing differently but importantly to a child's development (e.g. social and emotional development, and later peer relationships). Evaluation: findings on the father's role are inconsistent, partly because studies differ on exactly what question they are asking (why do fathers generally become secondary attachment figures? or, what role do fathers who are primary attachment figures play?), and socially constructed gender-role expectations may still shape both parental behaviour and how researchers interpret/report it; economic and social change (increasing numbers of stay-at-home fathers and same-sex parent families) makes the question of a father's 'unique' biologically-determined role increasingly considered less meaningful by contemporary researchers than the broader question of what determines the quality of any attachment relationship, regardless of the caregiver's sex.
