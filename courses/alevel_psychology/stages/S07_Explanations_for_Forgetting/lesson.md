# S07_Explanations_for_Forgetting - Lesson: Explanations for forgetting: interference and retrieval failure

## Goal
The learner explains and evaluates two explanations for forgetting from long-term memory: interference and retrieval failure due to absence of cues.

## Syllabus items taught here
- MEM3 - Explanations for forgetting: interference
- MEM4 - Explanations for forgetting: retrieval failure due to absence of cues

## How to teach this
Ask: have you ever walked into a room and completely forgotten why -- but the moment you walked back into the room you'd left, the reason instantly came back? What does that suggest about how memories are 'found' again? #### MEM3 Explanations for forgetting: interference
**Interference theory.** This explains forgetting as one memory disrupting/interfering with the recall of another, particularly when the two memories are similar. **Proactive interference (PI)** is when an older, previously learned memory disrupts recall of a newer one (old interferes with new); **retroactive interference (RI)** is when a newer memory disrupts recall of an older one (new interferes with old). **McGeoch and McDonald (1931)** found that recall of a previously learned word list was progressively worse the more similar a second, subsequently learned list of words was to the original (e.g. synonyms produced the worst recall, unrelated words the least disruption), showing interference is greater when the two sets of material are more similar. Interference is considered a relatively minor real-world cause of everyday forgetting compared with retrieval failure, since it mainly affects memories that are similar in content and requires artificial laboratory conditions (e.g. paired-associate learning of word lists) to reliably demonstrate -- real-world evidence (e.g. studies of rugby players finding that forgetting the names of earlier-season opposing teams was better predicted by how many games had been played in between, i.e. by interference, than by the time that had simply elapsed) does provide some support, but interference effects are often more modest and short-lived outside the lab.

#### MEM4 Explanations for forgetting: retrieval failure due to absence of cues
**Retrieval failure due to absence of cues.** This explains forgetting not as the memory trace itself being lost, but as the memory being inaccessible because the right retrieval cues are not present at the point of recall -- 'forgetting' is really a retrieval, not a storage, failure. The **encoding specificity principle (Tulving, 1983)** states that a cue will only help retrieval if it was present at encoding and is also present at retrieval; the closer the match between encoding and retrieval contexts, the better recall will be. This produces two types of cue-dependent forgetting: **context-dependent forgetting** (external, environmental cues present at encoding are absent at retrieval, e.g. Godden and Baddeley's (1975) study of deep-sea divers, which found recall was significantly better when the environment (on land or underwater) at learning matched the environment at recall than when it did not) and **state-dependent forgetting** (internal, physiological/psychological cues -- e.g. mood, or being under the influence of a drug -- present at encoding are absent at retrieval, e.g. Carter and Cahill's (1979) finding that recall was better when a person's internal state at learning (calm or stressed/anxious) matched their internal state at recall). Evaluation: retrieval failure has good real-world application (e.g. informing the cognitive interview's use of context reinstatement, see MEM6) and reasonable ecological validity from the diving study, but many real-world forgetting situations may not straightforwardly fit context/state mismatch, and it can be difficult to establish that retrieval failure, rather than another mechanism (e.g. interference or simple decay of the trace), is the actual cause of a specific instance of forgetting.
