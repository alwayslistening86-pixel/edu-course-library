## How to teach this
Ask: instead of asking what forces or drives made someone behave a certain way, what would psychology look like if it started by asking what a person is freely striving to become? Every named study, theorist and theory in this course (e.g. Asch, Milgram, Zimbardo, Adorno, Moscovici, Atkinson and Shiffrin, Baddeley and Hitch, Loftus and Palmer, Johnson and Scott, Yuille and Cutshall, Geiselman, Schaffer and Emerson, Lorenz, Harlow, Bowlby, Ainsworth, Van Ijzendoorn and Kroonenberg, Rutter, Hazan and Shaver, Beck, Ellis, Wiles, Wundt, Pavlov, Skinner, Bandura, Freud, Maslow, Rogers, Maguire, Sperry, Siffre, Gottesman, Bateson, Meehl, Buss, Walster, Kerckhoff and Davis, Thibaut and Kelley, Rusbult, Duck, McCutcheon, Dollard, Tinbergen, Wilson and Daly, Irwin and Cressey, Sykes) is a genuine, correctly attributed piece of psychology, checked against real psychological knowledge and AQA's own published subject content when this course was built -- present it as real research, never as illustrative fiction, and correct the learner explicitly if they misattribute a study or misstate a finding. Research Methods (4.2.3, at least 25% of assessment across all three papers, plus a minimum 10% mathematical-skills requirement) is examined throughout, not only in its own stages -- sampling, experimental design and inferential statistics can appear applied to any topic area. Every numerical worked example (mean, median, mode, range, standard deviation, the sign test) was computed and verified with Python when this course was built, and the learner should always show full working for a calculation, since AQA credits correct method as well as a correct final answer. Extended-writing ('Discuss...'/'Evaluate...') questions are marked holistically against AQA's levels-of-response scheme (see `rubric.json`), not by a checklist of individual points -- teach the learner to build a structured AO1 (accurate knowledge) + AO3 (effective discussion/evaluation), and where the question is scenario-based, AO2 (explicit application to the scenario) response, not a list of disconnected facts.

#### APP6 The humanistic approach (free will, self-actualisation, Maslow, congruence) and comparison of approaches
**The humanistic approach.** In contrast to the deterministic approaches (behaviourist, biological, psychodynamic), humanistic psychology (associated with **Maslow** and **Rogers**) emphasises **free will** -- the belief that humans are active agents who can determine their own development, largely in control of and responsible for their own behaviour and life choices, rather than being wholly shaped by external/internal forces beyond their control (see ID2). Central to the approach is **self-actualisation**: the innate desire/motivation to achieve one's full personal potential, representing the highest level of psychological growth. **Maslow's hierarchy of needs**, usually depicted as a pyramid, orders human needs from most basic/fundamental to highest: physiological needs (e.g. food, water), safety needs, love/belongingness needs, esteem needs, and finally self-actualisation at the top; Maslow argued lower-level needs generally must be at least broadly satisfied before a person can strive effectively toward self-actualisation, though self-actualisation is achieved by only a minority of people. The approach is **person-centred**, focused on the whole, unique individual and their own subjective experience of the world, rather than reducing them to general laws or component processes (see ID4, holism). **Rogers** developed **client-centred (person-centred) therapy**, based on the idea that psychological problems (which Rogers saw in terms of a distorted, incongruent self-concept) often originate from a person having received **conditions of worth** in childhood -- love/approval from significant others (e.g. parents) given only conditionally, when the child meets certain expectations -- rather than **unconditional positive regard** (acceptance and support regardless of what a person says or does). This can produce **incongruence** -- a mismatch between a person's self-concept (how they see themselves) and their ideal self (how they would like to be) -- which Rogers linked to poor psychological adjustment and low self-worth; therapy aims to reduce this incongruence by providing a therapeutic relationship characterised by unconditional positive regard, empathy and genuineness, helping the client move toward **congruence** and, ultimately, self-actualisation. This approach was highly influential in the development of modern counselling psychology, emphasising the therapeutic relationship itself as central to positive change. Evaluation: the approach offers a positive, holistic view of human nature not shared by more deterministic approaches, and has had genuine practical influence on therapeutic practice (e.g. counselling); however, it is criticised as unscientific and difficult to test empirically (concepts such as self-actualisation and congruence are abstract, subjective and hard to operationalise or measure objectively), and, as a largely Western, individualistic approach, may reflect a culturally specific view of the self and personal growth that does not generalise well to more collectivist cultures.

**Comparing approaches.** The six approaches differ on several key dimensions. On the **nature-nurture debate** (see ID3): the biological and psychodynamic approaches (via innate drives) lean toward nature; behaviourism and SLT lean toward nurture/environment; the cognitive approach broadly accepts an interactionist position (innate information-processing capacities shaped by experience); humanistic psychology emphasises free will over either. On **reductionism versus holism** (see ID4): the biological approach is strongly reductionist (explaining behaviour via genes/neurochemistry); behaviourism is reductionist in a different way (stimulus-response links); humanistic psychology is explicitly holistic. On **determinism versus free will** (see ID2): behaviourism (environmental determinism), biological (biological determinism) and psychodynamic (psychic determinism) approaches are all strongly deterministic; humanistic psychology alone strongly asserts free will; the cognitive approach takes a softer, 'soft determinist' position. On **use of scientific methods**: the biological, behaviourist and (to a good extent) cognitive approaches use rigorous, objective, replicable methods; psychodynamic and humanistic approaches rely much more on subjective case-study or clinical methods, limiting their scientific credibility by comparison.
