## How to teach this
Ask: if you can train a dog to salivate at the sound of a bell, could the same basic learning principle explain how a person develops an irrational fear, or even a habit like nail-biting? Every named study, theorist and theory in this course (e.g. Asch, Milgram, Zimbardo, Adorno, Moscovici, Atkinson and Shiffrin, Baddeley and Hitch, Loftus and Palmer, Johnson and Scott, Yuille and Cutshall, Geiselman, Schaffer and Emerson, Lorenz, Harlow, Bowlby, Ainsworth, Van Ijzendoorn and Kroonenberg, Rutter, Hazan and Shaver, Beck, Ellis, Wiles, Wundt, Pavlov, Skinner, Bandura, Freud, Maslow, Rogers, Maguire, Sperry, Siffre, Gottesman, Bateson, Meehl, Buss, Walster, Kerckhoff and Davis, Thibaut and Kelley, Rusbult, Duck, McCutcheon, Dollard, Tinbergen, Wilson and Daly, Irwin and Cressey, Sykes) is a genuine, correctly attributed piece of psychology, checked against real psychological knowledge and AQA's own published subject content when this course was built -- present it as real research, never as illustrative fiction, and correct the learner explicitly if they misattribute a study or misstate a finding. Research Methods (4.2.3, at least 25% of assessment across all three papers, plus a minimum 10% mathematical-skills requirement) is examined throughout, not only in its own stages -- sampling, experimental design and inferential statistics can appear applied to any topic area. Every numerical worked example (mean, median, mode, range, standard deviation, the sign test) was computed and verified with Python when this course was built, and the learner should always show full working for a calculation, since AQA credits correct method as well as a correct final answer. Extended-writing ('Discuss...'/'Evaluate...') questions are marked holistically against AQA's levels-of-response scheme (see `rubric.json`), not by a checklist of individual points -- teach the learner to build a structured AO1 (accurate knowledge) + AO3 (effective discussion/evaluation), and where the question is scenario-based, AO2 (explicit application to the scenario) response, not a list of disconnected facts.

#### APP1 The origins of psychology (Wundt, introspection) and the behaviourist approach (classical and operant conditioning)
**The origins of psychology.** **Wilhelm Wundt** opened the first psychology laboratory in Leipzig, Germany, in 1879, marking psychology's emergence as a distinct, formal scientific discipline separate from philosophy. Wundt's method was **introspection** -- systematically and carefully analysing and recording, under standardised, controlled laboratory conditions, one's own conscious mental processes (thoughts, sensations, feelings) as they occurred, in response to a standard set of stimuli, aiming to break these processes down into their basic structural elements (an approach called structuralism). This launched psychology's aim of studying the mind scientifically, but introspection was later heavily criticised (e.g. by behaviourists) as unscientific, since it relies entirely on private, subjective, unobservable and unverifiable self-report, which cannot be objectively measured or verified by anyone else, giving rise to alternative, more strictly observable and measurable approaches, beginning with behaviourism.

**The behaviourist approach.** Behaviourism, emerging from around 1913 (Watson), rejected introspection and argued psychology should be an objective science studying only observable, measurable behaviour, using controlled laboratory experiments, and largely rejecting the study of internal, unobservable mental processes. Behaviourists argue that most behaviour is **learned** from the environment (not innate), and that the same basic learning processes apply to humans and other animals, so research (often using non-human animals) can be generalised across species. **Classical conditioning**, demonstrated by **Pavlov's** research with dogs, is learning through **association**: an unconditioned stimulus (e.g. food) naturally produces an unconditioned response (e.g. salivation); a neutral stimulus (e.g. a bell) is repeatedly paired with the unconditioned stimulus; eventually the neutral stimulus alone (now a conditioned stimulus) produces the same response (now a conditioned response, e.g. salivating at the bell alone). **Operant conditioning**, developed by **Skinner** (using 'Skinner boxes' to study rats/pigeons), is learning through the **consequences** of behaviour: **positive reinforcement** (receiving a desirable consequence, e.g. a food pellet, for a behaviour, making it more likely to recur); **negative reinforcement** (a behaviour that removes/avoids an unpleasant consequence, e.g. pressing a lever to stop an electric shock, making it more likely to recur); and **punishment** (an unpleasant consequence following a behaviour, making it less likely to recur). Evaluation: behaviourism's reliance on controlled lab experiments gives high internal validity/scientific credibility, and it has useful real-world applications (e.g. systematic desensitisation and token economies), but it is criticised for environmental determinism (largely ignoring free will/cognition, see ID2) and for the limited generalisability of animal-based research to complex human behaviour.
