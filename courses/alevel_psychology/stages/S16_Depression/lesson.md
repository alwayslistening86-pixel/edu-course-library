## How to teach this
Ask: could two people experience the exact same setback -- say, failing a test -- and one become mildly disappointed while the other becomes seriously depressed, purely because of how each of them thinks about it? #### PSY3 Depression: characteristics, Beck's negative triad, Ellis's ABC model, and CBT
**Characteristics of depression.** Depression is a mood disorder involving persistent low mood and loss of interest/pleasure in most activities, classified in DSM-5/ICD-11. **Behavioural characteristics**: reduced activity levels/lethargy; disruption to sleep (insomnia or hypersomnia) and eating behaviour (loss of appetite or comfort-eating, leading to weight change); aggression and self-harm are also possible. **Emotional characteristics**: persistent low mood/sadness, feelings of worthlessness or guilt, and reduced self-esteem, alongside anger sometimes directed at the self or others. **Cognitive characteristics**: poor concentration and difficulty making decisions, dwelling on/absolutist ('black-and-white') negative thinking (e.g. viewing a minor setback as a complete failure), and negative, self-critical or hopeless beliefs about the self, the world and the future.

**Beck's cognitive theory of depression.** **Beck (1967)** proposed that some people develop a cognitive vulnerability to depression through early experience, producing **negative self-schemas** (a tendency to interpret information about the self negatively) and **cognitive biases** (systematic errors/distortions in thinking, e.g. overgeneralising a single negative event to all situations, or selectively attending only to negative aspects of experience while ignoring positives). This cognitive vulnerability leads to Beck's **negative triad** -- persistently negative, automatic thoughts about **the self** (e.g. 'I am worthless'), **the world** (e.g. 'no one understands me') and **the future** (e.g. 'nothing will ever improve') -- and these three elements interact and maintain each other, producing and sustaining the low mood and other symptoms of depression.

**Ellis's ABC model.** **Ellis (1962)** proposed depression results from irrational thinking, via the **ABC model**: an **Activating event** (something happens, e.g. failing an exam) is followed by a **Belief** (a rational or irrational interpretation of that event, e.g. rationally, 'I need to work harder next time', or irrationally, 'I always fail, I'm useless'), which produces a **Consequence** (emotional and behavioural, e.g. rational beliefs produce healthy negative emotions like disappointment, while irrational beliefs produce unhealthy, disproportionate emotions like severe depression). Ellis identified particular irrational beliefs prone to causing problems, including 'musturbatory thinking' (rigidly believing one *must* achieve a certain standard) and 'utopianism' (believing life must always be fair).

**Cognitive behaviour therapy (CBT).** CBT is the main psychological treatment for depression based on these cognitive theories, aiming to identify and challenge/change the negative, irrational thoughts (negative triad, or irrational beliefs) that are causing and maintaining depressive symptoms, replacing them with more rational, balanced or realistic alternatives. Beck's approach involves the client and therapist working together as 'scientists', treating negative thoughts as hypotheses to be tested against real-world evidence, gradually building a more balanced, realistic set of beliefs. Ellis's approach, extended into **Rational Emotive Behaviour Therapy (REBT)**, adds a **D**ispute stage to the ABC model, in which the therapist actively and directly challenges/disputes the client's irrational beliefs (e.g. through logical argument, empirical questioning of the evidence, or pragmatic challenges about how useful the belief actually is), aiming to produce more rational beliefs and healthier emotional consequences; behavioural activation (encouraging engagement in rewarding activities) is often also used alongside the cognitive elements. Evaluation: CBT has strong research support for effectiveness with mild-to-moderate depression (e.g. **Wiles et al. (2016)**, in a large UK randomised controlled trial, found CBT added to usual GP care produced significantly better outcomes at 12 months than usual care alone for patients whose depression had not responded to antidepressants) and is relatively brief/cost-effective compared with some alternatives, but is less consistently effective for very severe depression (where concentration/motivation for the demanding cognitive work of therapy may itself be too impaired), requires active client engagement/effort, and, because it focuses on cognitions, may not adequately address cases where depression has a substantial biological/situational (rather than purely cognitive) origin.
