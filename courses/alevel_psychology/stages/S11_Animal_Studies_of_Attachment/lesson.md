# S11_Animal_Studies_of_Attachment - Lesson: Animal studies of attachment (Lorenz, Harlow)

## Goal
The learner describes and evaluates Lorenz's and Harlow's animal studies of attachment, and their implications for human attachment.

## Syllabus items taught here

- ATT4 - Animal studies of attachment: Harlow (contact comfort)

## How to teach this
Ask: why would a family of baby goslings follow around the first moving object they saw after hatching -- even if that object was a scientist in wellington boots, not their mother? Every named study, theorist and theory in this course (e.g. Asch, Milgram, Zimbardo, Adorno, Moscovici, Atkinson and Shiffrin, Baddeley and Hitch, Loftus and Palmer, Johnson and Scott, Yuille and Cutshall, Geiselman, Schaffer and Emerson, Lorenz, Harlow, Bowlby, Ainsworth, Van Ijzendoorn and Kroonenberg, Rutter, Hazan and Shaver, Beck, Ellis, Wiles, Wundt, Pavlov, Skinner, Bandura, Freud, Maslow, Rogers, Maguire, Sperry, Siffre, Gottesman, Bateson, Meehl, Buss, Walster, Kerckhoff and Davis, Thibaut and Kelley, Rusbult, Duck, McCutcheon, Dollard, Tinbergen, Wilson and Daly, Irwin and Cressey, Sykes) is a genuine, correctly attributed piece of psychology, checked against real psychological knowledge and AQA's own published subject content when this course was built -- present it as real research, never as illustrative fiction, and correct the learner explicitly if they misattribute a study or misstate a finding. #### ATT3 Animal studies of attachment: Lorenz (imprinting)
**Lorenz's research: imprinting.** **Lorenz (1935)** randomly divided a clutch of goose eggs: half hatched with their natural mother (a control group), the other half hatched in an incubator, with Lorenz himself as the first moving object the goslings saw. The incubator group followed Lorenz everywhere, while the control group followed their mother, and this difference was permanent -- even when the two groups were mixed together, the incubator-hatched goslings continued to follow Lorenz, not the mother goose. Lorenz called this process **imprinting** -- a rapid, innate form of attachment occurring within a strict, biologically-determined **critical period** (for geese, the first few hours after hatching), after which imprinting becomes increasingly difficult or impossible; a gosling that failed to imprint within this window did not attach to a mother figure at all. Lorenz also investigated **sexual imprinting**, finding that birds imprinted on a species/object during this critical period would later direct courtship/mating behaviour toward that same species/object as adults (e.g. a peacock raised with tortoises in a zoo reptile house later performed courtship displays toward tortoises). Evaluation: Lorenz's conclusions are based on bird species, whose attachment systems are more rigid and innate than mammals' (**generalisability to humans is limited**, since human attachment is more complex, flexible and less purely innate); some of Lorenz's observations (e.g. of the peacock) have also been questioned methodologically, since it is hard to establish with certainty what such incidental observations actually demonstrate.

#### ATT4 Animal studies of attachment: Harlow (contact comfort)
**Harlow's research: contact comfort.** **Harlow (1958)** tested whether infant rhesus monkeys attach to a caregiver primarily because of feeding (as behaviourist 'cupboard love' theory, see ATT5, would predict) or because of contact comfort. He reared infant monkeys with two surrogate 'mothers': one made of bare wire that dispensed milk, and one covered in soft cloth that did not dispense milk. Regardless of which surrogate fed them, the infant monkeys spent far more time clinging to and seeking comfort from the cloth-covered mother, especially when frightened, and used her as a safe base from which to explore a novel environment -- strongly suggesting attachment is based on **contact comfort**, not simply on which figure provides food. Harlow also identified a critical period (about 90 days) for healthy attachment formation in monkeys: monkeys reared with only a wire mother, or reared in total isolation beyond this critical period, showed severe and often irreversible long-term consequences in adulthood, including social/emotional abnormalities (e.g. difficulty forming relationships, unusually aggressive or fearful behaviour), poor/abnormal (even absent or neglectful/abusive) parenting of their own later offspring, and abnormal social/sexual behaviour, illustrating the lasting developmental importance of adequate early attachment. Evaluation: like Lorenz's research, generalisability to humans from a non-human primate is limited, though rhesus monkeys are more closely related to humans than geese, strengthening this particular evidence's relevance to theories like Bowlby's (see ATT5); the study also raises serious **ethical concerns** given the severe, lasting psychological harm caused to the monkeys, which would not be considered acceptable in psychological research today, though Harlow's findings had real-world value in changing practices around childcare and orphanages/institutional care.
