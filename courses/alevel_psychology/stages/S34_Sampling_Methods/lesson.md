# S34_Sampling_Methods - Lesson: Sampling methods

## Goal
The learner describes and evaluates random, systematic, stratified, opportunity and volunteer sampling, and explains the implications of sampling bias for generalisation.

## How to teach this

#### RM5 Sampling methods: random, systematic, stratified, opportunity, volunteer
**Population and sample.** The **target population** is the entire group of people a researcher is interested in and wishes to generalise their findings to; since it is rarely possible to test an entire population, researchers select a smaller **sample** intended to be representative of that population. **Sampling bias** occurs when a sample is not representative of the target population (e.g. systematically over- or under-representing certain groups), limiting how far findings can be confidently generalised beyond the sample itself. AQA specifies five sampling techniques. **Random sampling**: every member of the target population has an equal chance of being selected (e.g. using a random number generator applied to a complete numbered list of the population); strength: has the best chance, of these methods, of producing a genuinely representative, unbiased sample, since selection is not influenced by the researcher; limitation: can still, by chance, produce an unrepresentative sample, and can be time-consuming/impractical if a full list of the population is not available. **Systematic sampling**: selecting every nth member of the target population from a list (e.g. every 10th name); strength: relatively objective, avoiding direct researcher bias in who is selected; limitation: still not guaranteed to be representative, and can accidentally produce a biased sample if the list has an underlying pattern that coincides with the sampling interval. **Stratified sampling**: the population is divided into subgroups (strata) based on relevant characteristics (e.g. age group, sex), and participants are then randomly selected from each stratum in proportion to its size within the population; strength: designed to produce a sample that accurately, proportionally reflects the composition of the target population on the chosen characteristics, generally giving good generalisability; limitation: the identified strata may not reflect every characteristic that actually matters for the research question, and it is more time-consuming to organise than simpler methods. **Opportunity sampling**: selecting whichever members of the target population are conveniently available at the time (e.g. approaching people in a particular location); strength: quick, easy and practical, requiring relatively little time/planning; limitation: is often highly unrepresentative (biased toward whoever happens to be available in one particular place/time), significantly limiting generalisability. **Volunteer (self-selected) sampling**: participants self-select by responding to a researcher's request for participants (e.g. an advertisement); strength: quick, and involves minimal researcher effort to actively recruit; limitation: often produces a biased sample, since people who volunteer may systematically differ in relevant ways (e.g. being more motivated, curious or, in some research, an unusual personality profile) from those who do not volunteer -- a bias sometimes called volunteer bias.
