# S43_Idiographic_Nomothetic_and_Ethical_Implications - Lesson: Idiographic/nomothetic approaches and ethical implications of research

## Goal
The learner distinguishes idiographic and nomothetic approaches to psychological investigation, and explains the ethical implications of research studies and theory, including socially sensitive research.

## Syllabus items taught here
- ID5 - Idiographic and nomothetic approaches; ethical implications of research studies and theory, including socially sensitive research

## How to teach this
Ask: is psychology's job to discover general laws that apply to everyone, or to deeply understand what makes one particular individual tick -- and can it really do both? Every named study, theorist and theory in this course (e.g. Asch, Milgram, Zimbardo, Adorno, Moscovici, Atkinson and Shiffrin, Baddeley and Hitch, Loftus and Palmer, Johnson and Scott, Yuille and Cutshall, Geiselman, Schaffer and Emerson, Lorenz, Harlow, Bowlby, Ainsworth, Van Ijzendoorn and Kroonenberg, Rutter, Hazan and Shaver, Beck, Ellis, Wiles, Wundt, Pavlov, Skinner, Bandura, Freud, Maslow, Rogers, Maguire, Sperry, Siffre, Gottesman, Bateson, Meehl, Buss, Walster, Kerckhoff and Davis, Thibaut and Kelley, Rusbult, Duck, McCutcheon, Dollard, Tinbergen, Wilson and Daly, Irwin and Cressey, Sykes) is a genuine, correctly attributed piece of psychology, checked against real psychological knowledge and AQA's own published subject content when this course was built -- present it as real research, never as illustrative fiction, and correct the learner explicitly if they misattribute a study or misstate a finding. Research Methods (4.2.3, at least 25% of assessment across all three papers, plus a minimum 10% mathematical-skills requirement) is examined throughout, not only in its own stages -- sampling, experimental design and inferential statistics can appear applied to any topic area. Every numerical worked example (mean, median, mode, range, standard deviation, the sign test) was computed and verified with Python when this course was built, and the learner should always show full working for a calculation, since AQA credits correct method as well as a correct final answer. Extended-writing ('Discuss...'/'Evaluate...') questions are marked holistically against AQA's levels-of-response scheme (see `rubric.json`), not by a checklist of individual points -- teach the learner to build a structured AO1 (accurate knowledge) + AO3 (effective discussion/evaluation), and where the question is scenario-based, AO2 (explicit application to the scenario) response, not a list of disconnected facts.

#### ID5 Idiographic and nomothetic approaches; ethical implications of research studies and theory, including socially sensitive research
**Idiographic and nomothetic approaches.** The **idiographic approach** focuses on the individual, aiming to understand and describe the unique, subjective experience of a particular person, often using qualitative methods that produce rich, detailed, in-depth data (e.g. case studies -- see MEM1's patient HM, or unstructured interviews), without necessarily seeking to generalise findings to a wider population; associated particularly with the humanistic approach (see APP6) and aspects of the psychodynamic approach (see APP5, which was substantially built on detailed individual case studies). The **nomothetic approach** instead aims to establish general laws/principles of behaviour that apply universally, or at least broadly, across people, typically using more quantitative methods and larger samples that allow statistical analysis and generalisation (e.g. the biological and behaviourist approaches, see APP1/APP4, and most of the experimental research covered throughout this course, e.g. Asch's or Milgram's studies, see SI1-SI4). Evaluation: the idiographic approach provides a complete, detailed, holistic understanding of a specific individual that a nomothetic study cannot match, but findings from a single case cannot be confidently generalised, and idiographic methods are more vulnerable to subjective researcher interpretation; the nomothetic approach allows scientific, statistically-testable, generalisable conclusions (in keeping with a more objective, empirical science, see RM9), but can lose sight of the unique individual, sometimes reducing rich human experience to a set of scores or broad statistical trends that may not accurately describe any single person. Many areas of psychology in practice combine both approaches (e.g. establishing a general, nomothetic diagnostic category for a disorder like depression, then using more idiographic methods to understand and treat how that disorder specifically manifests in one particular client).

**Ethical implications of research studies and theory.** Beyond the specific ethical guidelines governing the treatment of individual participants during a study (see RM7), psychologists must also consider the broader, longer-term ethical implications of conducting particular research and of the theories/findings it produces, for participants, for the specific groups studied, and for society more widely -- including implications the original researchers may not have fully anticipated when the study was designed. **Socially sensitive research** (a term proposed by **Sieber and Stanley, 1988**) refers to research in which the findings, or even simply the research question itself, may have direct, real-world social consequences for the participants or the wider social group they represent (e.g. research comparing intelligence or offending rates across different ethnic or social groups, or aspects of the research into cultural variations in attachment, see ATT8, or gender bias in diagnosis, see ID1/SCZ1) -- such research can, even if scientifically sound and well-intentioned, be misused to justify prejudice or discriminatory social policy, or can cause genuine distress/stigma to the group being studied, whether or not the original findings are subsequently supported or overturned. Sieber and Stanley identified several points at which ethical implications should be considered: the research question itself (how it is phrased, and what its implicit assumptions are); the conduct of the research and treatment of participants; the institutional context (who funds/uses the research); and how the findings are interpreted and applied once published (research can be used, including by others without the original researchers' consent or intention, in ways that cause real-world harm). Evaluation: psychologists cannot ethically simply avoid all socially sensitive topics, since doing so would mean failing to study important, real, consequential social issues (e.g. mental health, discrimination, offending) that badly need rigorous, valid psychological evidence rather than being left entirely to speculation or prejudice; the more defensible response is for researchers to consider these wider implications carefully and responsibly at every stage of the research process (rather than only the immediate treatment of participants during the study itself), and for the profession (e.g. via BPS guidance) to provide some structure for this wider ethical reflection.
