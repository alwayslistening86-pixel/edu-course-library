## How to teach this
Ask: if you were placed in a cave with no clocks, no daylight and no other cues to time at all, would your sleep-wake cycle stay locked to a neat 24 hours, or would it start to drift? #### BIO7 Biological rhythms: circadian, infradian, ultradian; endogenous pacemakers and exogenous zeitgebers
**Biological rhythms** are regular, cyclical changes in the body's biological activity/behaviour over time, controlled by both internal (**endogenous**) biological mechanisms and external (**exogenous**) environmental cues. **Circadian rhythms** are biological rhythms that follow roughly a 24-hour cycle, the best-known example being the sleep-wake cycle. **Siffre's cave studies** (Michel Siffre spent extended periods underground with no natural light or external time cues) found his sleep-wake cycle, freed from normal external cues, drifted to a naturally-occurring cycle of closer to 25 hours, suggesting the body has an innate, endogenous circadian rhythm slightly longer than 24 hours that is normally adjusted/entrained by external cues to match the actual 24-hour day. **Infradian rhythms** last longer than 24 hours (e.g. weekly, monthly or annual cycles), the clearest example being the female **menstrual cycle** (typically around 28 days, regulated by hormonal changes, e.g. rising and falling oestrogen and progesterone); another example is **seasonal affective disorder (SAD)**, a form of depression linked to a roughly annual (seasonal) cycle, more common in winter months, thought to be influenced by reduced light exposure affecting the hormone melatonin. **Ultradian rhythms** last less than 24 hours, recurring more than once in a day, the clearest example being the cycle of **sleep stages** during a single night's sleep: sleep progresses through repeating 90-minute cycles from light sleep through deeper stages to **REM (rapid eye movement) sleep** (associated with vivid dreaming and, despite outward physical stillness, high brain activity resembling wakefulness), before beginning the cycle again, typically repeating around four to six times per night.

**Endogenous pacemakers and exogenous zeitgebers.** **Endogenous pacemakers** are the body's own, innate internal biological 'clocks' that regulate many biological rhythms; the main endogenous pacemaker for the circadian sleep-wake cycle is the **suprachiasmatic nucleus (SCN)**, a small structure located in the hypothalamus, which receives direct information about light levels from the eye (even when the eyes are closed) via a specialised pathway, and uses this to regulate/entrain the sleep-wake cycle, partly by controlling the release of **melatonin** (a hormone, released by the pineal gland, that induces sleepiness -- melatonin release is suppressed by light and increases in darkness). **Exogenous zeitgebers** (German for 'time-givers') are external environmental cues that entrain/reset biological rhythms to match the actual external environment; the most important exogenous zeitgeber for circadian rhythms is **light**, which resets the SCN daily to keep the internal (naturally slightly longer than 24-hour) clock synchronised with the actual 24-hour day-night cycle -- this is why jet lag occurs (the internal clock is briefly out of step with a new light-dark cycle after rapid travel across time zones) and why shift work, which disrupts normal light exposure and sleep timing, is associated with a range of negative health effects. Other exogenous zeitgebers include social cues (e.g. meal times, social schedules), which can also help entrain rhythms, particularly in the absence of strong light cues. Evaluation: research (e.g. Siffre's studies, and SCN research) provides strong evidence that biological rhythms have a genuine endogenous, biological basis while also being importantly influenced/entrained by the environment (an interactionist position), but much foundational research (e.g. isolation studies like Siffre's) involves very small samples (often a single participant), and individual differences in natural circadian rhythm length ('chronotype' -- some people being naturally more 'morning' or 'evening' types) mean findings from a small number of case studies may not generalise well to the wider population.
