# S44_Evolutionary_Explanations_and_Attraction - Lesson: Evolutionary explanations and factors affecting attraction

## Goal
The learner explains evolutionary explanations of partner preferences and the factors affecting attraction: self-disclosure, physical attractiveness (matching hypothesis) and filter theory.

## Syllabus items taught here
- REL1 - Evolutionary explanations of partner preferences (sexual selection, anisogamy, inter-/intra-sexual selection)
- REL2 - Factors affecting attraction: self-disclosure, physical attractiveness (matching hypothesis), filter theory

## How to teach this
Ask: across cultures, research consistently finds men and women report somewhat different priorities when choosing a long-term partner -- could evolution, rather than culture alone, help explain why? #### REL1 Evolutionary explanations of partner preferences (sexual selection, anisogamy, inter-/intra-sexual selection)
**Evolutionary explanations of partner preferences.** Evolutionary psychology explains human romantic/reproductive behaviour in terms of **sexual selection** -- any genetically influenced characteristic/preference that increases an individual's chances of successfully reproducing, and so passing on their genes, will be favoured by natural selection over evolutionary time. **Anisogamy** refers to the fundamental difference in size and number of male versus female gametes (sperm are small, numerous and 'cheap' to produce; eggs are larger, far fewer, and metabolically 'costly'), which, combined with women's greater minimum biological investment in any resulting offspring (pregnancy, childbirth, historically breastfeeding), is argued to have produced different, evolutionarily-shaped mating strategies for men and women. **Inter-sexual selection**: the strategies used by one sex to attract mates of the other sex; since women historically bear the greater cost/risk of reproduction, evolutionary theory predicts women should be the more selective/'choosier' sex, favouring partners who display signs of good genes and resource/status (able to invest in and protect offspring) -- consistent with cross-cultural survey research (e.g. **Buss, 1989**, across 37 cultures) finding women consistently rate resources/status highly as a partner preference. **Intra-sexual selection**: competition within one sex (typically studied as competition between males) for access to mates of the other sex, favouring traits that help an individual outcompete same-sex rivals (e.g. physical size/strength, or, in modern research, other displays of status); evolutionary theory predicts men, whose minimum reproductive investment is lower, should show a stronger preference for indicators of youth and fertility (linked, in evolutionary terms, to reproductive value) -- again broadly consistent with Buss's finding that men, cross-culturally, tend to rate physical attractiveness and relative youth more highly than women do. Evaluation: cross-cultural consistency in these broad preference patterns (e.g. Buss's large, cross-cultural survey) is often cited as support for at least some genuine evolutionary basis to human partner preferences, but evolutionary explanations are criticised for being difficult to test rigorously/falsifiably (since a plausible-sounding evolutionary story can often be constructed after the fact for almost any observed pattern of behaviour -- a criticism sometimes called the problem of 'just-so stories'), for underplaying the role of social and cultural learning and rapidly changing modern social/economic conditions (e.g. increasing female financial independence) in shaping partner preferences, and for beta bias/oversimplification of the real diversity of preferences and relationship types that exist both across and within genders and cultures.

#### REL2 Factors affecting attraction: self-disclosure, physical attractiveness (matching hypothesis), filter theory
**Factors affecting attraction.** **Self-disclosure**: revealing personal, private information about oneself to another person; **Social penetration theory (Altman and Taylor, 1973)** proposes that as a romantic relationship develops, partners reveal increasingly broad (across more topics) and deep (more intimate, personal) self-disclosure to each other, in a reciprocal process -- appropriately-timed, reciprocal self-disclosure increases feelings of intimacy and attraction, while disclosing too much too soon (or too little) can be off-putting and damage a developing relationship. **Physical attractiveness**: generally found to be an important initial predictor of attraction, though its role in longer-term relationship formation is refined by the **matching hypothesis (Walster et al., 1966)**, which proposes that rather than everyone simply seeking the most physically attractive partner possible, people tend to select romantic partners who are a reasonably close match to their own level of physical attractiveness, since choosing a highly desirable partner risks rejection, while a person will generally seek the most attractive partner realistically available to them given their own perceived attractiveness/'value' -- Walster et al.'s original 'computer dance' study found only limited direct support for the hypothesis, but later research using actual, established couples (rather than randomly paired strangers at a single event) has found stronger evidence that partners' attractiveness levels do tend to correlate. **Filter theory (Kerckhoff and Davis, 1962)** proposes that people use a series of filters to narrow the 'field of availables' (all potential partners) down to the person they eventually form a serious relationship with, at different stages of relationship development: **social demography** (factors such as proximity, social class, religion and education level restrict the realistic range of people someone is likely to even meet and consider, an early-stage filter); **similarity in attitudes** (in relationships of relatively short duration, shared/similar attitudes and values become an important filter, since discovering major disagreement can end a developing relationship, consistent with the general finding that similarity, rather than difference, predicts attraction); and **complementarity of needs** (in longer-term relationships, partners increasingly value the way each meets the other's needs, even where this involves genuine differences, e.g. one partner's need to nurture being complemented by the other's need to be nurtured, becoming a more important factor for maintaining a relationship as it matures). Evaluation: filter theory usefully identifies that different factors matter at different relationship stages, but later research (particularly given modern online dating, which can bypass the traditional social-demography 'field of availables' filter almost entirely) has questioned how well it fits contemporary patterns of relationship formation, and its original supporting evidence has proven difficult to consistently replicate.
