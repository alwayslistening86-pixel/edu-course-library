## How to teach this
Ask: if you wanted to know which brain areas are active while someone solves a maths problem, versus exactly how fast their brain responds to a flashed image, would you use the same technique for both? Every named study, theorist and theory in this course (e.g. Asch, Milgram, Zimbardo, Adorno, Moscovici, Atkinson and Shiffrin, Baddeley and Hitch, Loftus and Palmer, Johnson and Scott, Yuille and Cutshall, Geiselman, Schaffer and Emerson, Lorenz, Harlow, Bowlby, Ainsworth, Van Ijzendoorn and Kroonenberg, Rutter, Hazan and Shaver, Beck, Ellis, Wiles, Wundt, Pavlov, Skinner, Bandura, Freud, Maslow, Rogers, Maguire, Sperry, Siffre, Gottesman, Bateson, Meehl, Buss, Walster, Kerckhoff and Davis, Thibaut and Kelley, Rusbult, Duck, McCutcheon, Dollard, Tinbergen, Wilson and Daly, Irwin and Cressey, Sykes) is a genuine, correctly attributed piece of psychology, checked against real psychological knowledge and AQA's own published subject content when this course was built -- present it as real research, never as illustrative fiction, and correct the learner explicitly if they misattribute a study or misstate a finding. Research Methods (4.2.3, at least 25% of assessment across all three papers, plus a minimum 10% mathematical-skills requirement) is examined throughout, not only in its own stages -- sampling, experimental design and inferential statistics can appear applied to any topic area. Every numerical worked example (mean, median, mode, range, standard deviation, the sign test) was computed and verified with Python when this course was built, and the learner should always show full working for a calculation, since AQA credits correct method as well as a correct final answer. Extended-writing ('Discuss...'/'Evaluate...') questions are marked holistically against AQA's levels-of-response scheme (see `rubric.json`), not by a checklist of individual points -- teach the learner to build a structured AO1 (accurate knowledge) + AO3 (effective discussion/evaluation), and where the question is scenario-based, AO2 (explicit application to the scenario) response, not a list of disconnected facts.

#### BIO6 Ways of studying the brain: fMRI, EEG, ERPs, post-mortem examinations
**fMRI (functional magnetic resonance imaging).** Measures brain activity indirectly, by detecting changes in blood oxygenation and flow that occur in response to neural activity (more active neurons require more oxygenated blood, the **BOLD -- blood oxygen-level-dependent -- response**), producing detailed 3D images showing which brain areas are active during a particular task. Strengths: does not involve radiation (unlike some other scanning methods), and has good spatial resolution (can pinpoint activity to a specific brain area fairly precisely). Limitations: poor temporal resolution (there is a delay of several seconds between neural activity and the resulting change in blood flow it detects, so fMRI cannot capture activity that happens on a very fast, millisecond timescale) and it is expensive, requiring the person to remain very still inside a large scanner.

**EEG (electroencephalogram).** Measures general electrical activity within the brain via electrodes attached to the scalp, recording the combined electrical signals (brainwave patterns) produced by neurons firing beneath. Widely used to study, e.g., sleep stages (see BIO7) and to diagnose conditions such as epilepsy (via distinctive abnormal brainwave patterns). Strengths: excellent temporal resolution (can detect changes in electrical activity in real time, to within milliseconds). Limitations: poor spatial resolution (cannot precisely identify the exact source/location within the brain of a particular signal), and it only detects general electrical activity, making it difficult to distinguish between the activity produced by different, nearby neural sources.

**ERPs (event-related potentials).** A refinement of the basic EEG: rather than looking at general electrical activity, the EEG recording made in response to a specific stimulus/event of interest (e.g. a flashed image) is isolated, using a statistical averaging technique across many repeated presentations of the stimulus to filter out extraneous background brain activity ('noise'), leaving a clearer signal (the ERP) specifically linked to that stimulus. Strengths: like EEG, has excellent temporal resolution, and (unlike a raw, general EEG reading) allows researchers to isolate a specific type of neural response, giving it more precise application than a standard EEG. Limitations: still lacks the fine spatial resolution of fMRI, and requires a large number of trials/repetitions to produce a reliable signal, and background noise/other extraneous activity can be difficult to eliminate entirely.

**Post-mortem examinations.** Involves examining a deceased person's brain physically, typically correlating structural/anatomical abnormalities found in that brain with cognitive or behavioural deficits/abnormalities the person had been observed to show during their lifetime (e.g. Broca's original patient, or research linking specific brain abnormalities to disorders such as schizophrenia, see SCZ2). Strengths: allows for much more detailed, fine-grained examination of brain structure at the cellular level than is possible with any live-brain scanning technique, and was historically foundational in establishing localisation of function (before scanning technology existed). Limitations: causation is difficult to establish (an observed abnormality may not actually be the cause of the person's condition/behaviour during life, or the deceased may also have taken medication, e.g. antipsychotics, that itself altered brain structure, confounding the comparison), and there are ethical issues around consent for the use of the brain tissue after death.
