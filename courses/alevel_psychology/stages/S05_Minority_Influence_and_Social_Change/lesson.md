#### SI8 Minority influence: consistency, commitment, flexibility (Moscovici)
**Minority influence.** Unlike majority influence (which tends to produce only public compliance via normative social influence), a consistent minority can produce genuine, lasting **internalisation** of their view in the majority, through informational social influence -- the minority's persistence makes onlookers question their own position and consider the minority's viewpoint as a genuinely plausible source of new information. Three factors make minority influence more effective. **Consistency**: the minority repeating the same message over time (diachronic consistency) and different members of the minority agreeing with one another (synchronic consistency) makes the minority appear confident and increases the message's credibility, drawing attention to the minority's position. **Commitment**: the minority demonstrating dedication to their cause, sometimes through personal sacrifice or risk (the 'augmentation principle' -- commitment despite the cost of holding a minority view suggests the minority must genuinely believe strongly in their position), makes the majority take the view more seriously and look at it more closely. **Flexibility**: a minority that is willing to adapt and negotiate their position (rather than being rigid and dogmatic) is more persuasive than one seen as inflexible, since flexibility is seen as more reasonable and less threatening. **Moscovici's blue-green slides study (1969)** demonstrated minority influence experimentally: groups of participants, including two confederates, judged the colour of 36 clearly blue slides; when the two confederates consistently answered 'green' throughout, about 8.42% of genuine participants also said 'green' on at least one trial (and 32% conformed at least once), compared with a control group with no confederates, showing a small, consistent minority can shift some genuine participants' judgements.

#### SI9 The role of social influence processes in social change
**Social influence and social change.** Social change is the process by which societies shift their attitudes/behaviour to create new social norms (e.g. changing attitudes to smoking, civil rights, marriage equality). Psychologists explain how the processes already covered contribute to this. **Minority influence's role**: a committed, consistent minority (e.g. civil rights or suffrage campaigners) draws attention to an issue and, through **consistency**, **commitment** and gradual, growing exposure, can produce a **snowball effect** -- as more and more people are converted (through the same internalisation process seen in Moscovici's study), the minority eventually grows into a new majority, at which point the (now-majority) view can spread further via **conformity** through normative social influence, as most people go along with what has become the new social norm. Researchers describe a process called **social cryptomnesia**, where society changes its collective attitudes/memory of an issue but forgets that the change actually originated with the actions of a committed minority. **Obedience's role**: research such as Milgram's variation studies also has implications for social change -- disobedient models (e.g. one confederate refusing to give shocks) can encourage others to disobey too, showing how minority dissent, including from within an obedient hierarchy, can begin to erode a previously accepted social norm. Evaluation: real-world social changes (e.g. the gradual, decades-long shift in attitudes to smoking following consistent public-health campaigning, or LGBT+ rights campaigning) are often cited as broadly supporting this account, but social change is typically slow (the 'snowball effect' can take many years or decades), and these processes are drawn from artificial laboratory studies (e.g. Moscovici's slide judgements), which may not generalise well to complex, real-world social and political change involving much larger, more emotionally significant issues.
