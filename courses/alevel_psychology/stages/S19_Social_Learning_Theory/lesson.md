#### APP2 Social learning theory (imitation, identification, modelling, vicarious reinforcement, mediational processes; Bandura)
**Social learning theory (SLT).** **Bandura** argued that learning can also occur indirectly, through observation and imitation of others (**role models**), not only through direct reinforcement (as behaviourism proposes). **Imitation** is copying the observed behaviour of a role model. **Identification** is the process by which an observer associates themselves with a role model and wants to be like them -- identification is more likely when the model is similar to the observer (e.g. same sex, similar age), attractive, or has high status. **Modelling**, from the observer's perspective, is imitating the behaviour of a role model; from the role model's perspective, it is demonstrating a particular behaviour that may be learned through observation. **Vicarious reinforcement**: rather than needing to be reinforced directly, an observer is more likely to imitate a model's behaviour if they observe that model being reinforced (rewarded) for it, and less likely to imitate if the model is observed being punished -- learning therefore occurs through observing the consequences experienced by someone else. SLT proposes four **mediational (cognitive) processes** that must occur for observational learning to result in performed behaviour: **attention** (the observer must notice the behaviour); **retention** (the observer must be able to remember the behaviour observed); **motor reproduction** (the observer must be physically/practically capable of reproducing the behaviour); and **motivation** (the observer must have a reason/incentive to reproduce the behaviour, e.g. having seen it lead to reinforcement).

**Bandura's Bobo doll research.** **Bandura, Ross and Ross (1961)** had children individually observe an adult model behaving either aggressively or non-aggressively toward an inflatable Bobo doll, then later observed the children's own behaviour, alone, in a room with the doll and other toys. Children who had observed the aggressive model imitated significantly more physically and verbally aggressive acts toward the doll (including novel aggressive acts not directly demonstrated) than children who observed the non-aggressive model, and boys were generally more aggressive overall, with same-sex modelling effects also observed (children imitated a same-sex model somewhat more). A later variation found children who saw the aggressive model being rewarded imitated more aggression than those who saw the model punished, supporting vicarious reinforcement. Evaluation: SLT usefully bridges behaviourist and cognitive approaches by incorporating internal mediational processes, and explains behaviours (e.g. culturally or socially specific behaviour) that pure behaviourism struggles to account for; however, Bandura's research is criticised for low ecological validity (an inflatable doll designed to be hit is not equivalent to real-world aggression toward another person, and children may simply have displayed demand characteristics, doing 'what they thought was expected'), and SLT still relies substantially on direct/vicarious reinforcement to explain why a behaviour, once learned, continues to be performed, which some argue keeps it closer to behaviourism than a fully independent, purely cognitive theory of learning.
