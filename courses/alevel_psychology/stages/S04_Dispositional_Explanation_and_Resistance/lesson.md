#### SI6 The dispositional explanation for obedience: the Authoritarian Personality
**The dispositional explanation: the Authoritarian Personality.** Unlike Milgram's situational account, **Adorno et al. (1950)** proposed that some people are simply more predisposed to obey, because of their personality. Adorno developed this after studying the personality traits of people who showed high levels of prejudice, and argued that an **authoritarian personality** -- characterised by extreme respect for and submissiveness to authority, contempt for people seen as socially inferior, a highly conventional/rigid outlook, and cognitive inflexibility (an intolerance of ambiguity, seeing the world in black-and-white terms) -- disposes a person to obey authority figures unquestioningly. Adorno argued this personality type originates in a harsh, strict childhood involving conditional love (affection dependent on the child's strict obedience) and severe discipline, producing resentment the child cannot express directly toward their parents; this resentment is displaced onto safer, weaker targets (a defence mechanism called scapegoating), and the person identifies strongly with strong/powerful figures. Adorno measured this using the F-scale (a questionnaire measuring fascistic tendencies); people scoring higher on the F-scale showed greater obedience in obedience research and greater prejudice toward minority groups. Evaluation: the theory is criticised for being correlational (a link between authoritarianism and obedience does not prove the personality causes obedience -- both could stem from a third factor, e.g. lower education); the F-scale has been criticised for methodological flaws (e.g. response bias -- acquiescence/agreeing with items regardless of content, since all items are worded in the same 'direction'); and the theory struggles to explain why obedience to authority was so widespread across an entire population (e.g. in Nazi Germany) rather than confined to a particular personality type, which situational explanations (e.g. legitimacy of authority) account for more easily.

#### SI7 Resistance to social influence: social support and locus of control
**Resistance to social influence.** Not everyone conforms or obeys; two factors help explain resistance. **Social support**: the presence of others who are not conforming/obeying gives an individual the confidence to resist social pressure too, since it shows the majority view/authority instruction is not unanimous and breaks the appearance that everyone agrees -- consistent with Asch's finding that conformity dropped sharply once unanimity was broken by even one dissenter, and with Milgram's variation in which obedience fell to 10% when the participant was joined by two confederates who refused to continue giving shocks. The dissenting/disobedient other acts as a model, showing resistance is possible; the individual does not need to adopt the model's specific view, just their example of independence. **Locus of control (LOC)**: Rotter's concept of how much a person believes they, versus external forces, control events in their life. People with a strong **internal locus of control** believe they are largely responsible for/in control of what happens to them, tend to take more personal responsibility for their decisions, and have been found in research to be more resistant to social influence -- they are more self-confident, less concerned with social approval, and more likely to base decisions on their own judgement. People with a strong **external locus of control** believe outcomes are due to luck, fate or powerful others, tend to take a more passive, less responsible orientation to their own behaviour, and have been found to be more likely to conform/obey. Evaluation: research support exists for both factors (e.g. Allen and Levine's replication of an Asch-type task found a dissenter's presence reduced conformity even when the dissenter wore thick glasses and clearly had poor eyesight, since the value of social support lies in breaking unanimity rather than in the dissenter's expertise), but locus of control's link to resistance is not always strong or consistent across studies, and both explanations account only for resistance, not for why some people initially conform/obey more than others in the first place.
