#### AGG2 Social psychological explanations of aggression: frustration-aggression hypothesis, social learning theory, deindividuation
**The frustration-aggression hypothesis (Dollard et al., 1939)** proposes that frustration (the blocking of a goal a person is attempting to reach) always produces some readiness/drive to act aggressively, and, conversely, aggression is always caused by some prior frustration -- i.e. frustration and aggression were originally proposed as inevitably, directly linked. This original, strong version was later revised (e.g. by **Berkowitz**) to acknowledge that frustration produces a readiness for aggression, but whether this is actually expressed as aggression depends on situational factors, including the presence of **aggressive cues** (environmental stimuli associated with aggression, e.g. the mere sight of a weapon) which can trigger the translation of frustration-induced readiness into an actual aggressive act (a phenomenon sometimes called the 'weapons effect', distinct from the weapon focus effect on eyewitness memory, see MEM6). The theory also proposes **displacement**: since the true source of frustration is often too powerful, intangible, or otherwise unavailable to attack directly (e.g. one's employer, or a wider social/economic situation), aggression may instead be redirected toward a safer, more available substitute target (echoing Freud's concept of displacement as a defence mechanism, see APP5) -- a mechanism sometimes offered as a partial explanation for scapegoating of minority groups during periods of widespread economic/social frustration.

**Social learning theory (SLT)**, applied specifically to aggression (see APP2 for the general theory), proposes that aggressive behaviour is learned through observing and imitating aggressive role models, particularly when the model's aggression is seen to be rewarded (**vicarious reinforcement**) rather than punished, and that the same four mediational processes (attention, retention, motor reproduction, motivation) determine whether observed aggression is actually performed; **self-efficacy** -- a person's own belief in their capability of successfully carrying out a particular aggressive act -- also grows with each successfully performed aggressive act, making further aggression more likely. Bandura's Bobo doll research (see APP2) is frequently cited as direct supporting evidence specifically for the application of SLT to aggression.

**Deindividuation** proposes that being part of a large group/crowd can produce a psychological state in which a person loses their individual sense of identity and personal responsibility/accountability for their own actions, making uncharacteristic, uninhibited, often antisocial or aggressive behaviour considerably more likely than it would be as an individually identifiable person acting alone. This is thought to occur through several mechanisms: **reduced private self-awareness** (less internal reflection on one's own personal values/standards while immersed in a group, reducing normal self-regulation of behaviour); **reduced public self-awareness** (feeling anonymous and unidentifiable to others within a large group, especially if wearing a uniform/disguise or in low lighting/darkness, reducing concern about others' judgement of one's individual behaviour, and reducing the perceived likelihood of being personally identified/held responsible afterward); and a resulting shift in behaviour toward following the group's (sometimes more extreme or 'contagious') immediate social/situational norms, rather than one's own usual, individual moral standards. Research evidence includes studies finding people wearing masks/disguise, or in situations of low individual identifiability, tend to behave more aggressively than clearly identifiable individuals in an otherwise comparable situation (e.g. some field research finding Halloween trick-or-treaters in costume/disguise, especially in groups, were more likely to take extra sweets when told to take 'only one' than identifiable, non-disguised individual children). Evaluation: SLT usefully accounts for individual differences in aggression (based on differing role-model exposure) that purely biological explanations struggle to fully explain, and deindividuation has genuine real-world application (e.g. informing understanding of crowd violence, and, relevant to Zimbardo's own later theorising, institutional aggression, see AGG4); however, the frustration-aggression hypothesis has been criticised for being too broad/simplistic in its original strong form (not all frustration leads to aggression, and aggression clearly can occur without obvious prior frustration, e.g. some forms of instrumental, planned aggression), and both SLT and deindividuation research often rely on somewhat artificial laboratory or lab-adjacent settings (e.g. the Bobo doll), raising ecological validity concerns about how far findings generalise to real-world aggressive/violent behaviour.
