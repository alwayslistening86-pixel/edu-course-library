## How to teach this
Ask: if a drug can reduce a person's hallucinations and delusions, does that alone tell a therapist how to help that same person feel able to hold down a job or maintain a friendship again? Every named study, theorist and theory in this course (e.g. Asch, Milgram, Zimbardo, Adorno, Moscovici, Atkinson and Shiffrin, Baddeley and Hitch, Loftus and Palmer, Johnson and Scott, Yuille and Cutshall, Geiselman, Schaffer and Emerson, Lorenz, Harlow, Bowlby, Ainsworth, Van Ijzendoorn and Kroonenberg, Rutter, Hazan and Shaver, Beck, Ellis, Wiles, Wundt, Pavlov, Skinner, Bandura, Freud, Maslow, Rogers, Maguire, Sperry, Siffre, Gottesman, Bateson, Meehl, Buss, Walster, Kerckhoff and Davis, Thibaut and Kelley, Rusbult, Duck, McCutcheon, Dollard, Tinbergen, Wilson and Daly, Irwin and Cressey, Sykes) is a genuine, correctly attributed piece of psychology, checked against real psychological knowledge and AQA's own published subject content when this course was built -- present it as real research, never as illustrative fiction, and correct the learner explicitly if they misattribute a study or misstate a finding. Every numerical worked example (mean, median, mode, range, standard deviation, the sign test) was computed and verified with Python when this course was built, and the learner should always show full working for a calculation, since AQA credits correct method as well as a correct final answer. Extended-writing ('Discuss...'/'Evaluate...') questions are marked holistically against AQA's levels-of-response scheme (see `rubric.json`), not by a checklist of individual points -- teach the learner to build a structured AO1 (accurate knowledge) + AO3 (effective discussion/evaluation), and where the question is scenario-based, AO2 (explicit application to the scenario) response, not a list of disconnected facts.

#### SCZ4 Drug therapy, psychological treatments (CBT, family therapy, token economies) and the interactionist approach
**Drug therapy.** **Typical antipsychotics** (e.g. chlorpromazine), developed from the 1950s onward, work primarily as **dopamine antagonists**, binding to but not stimulating dopamine receptors (particularly D2 receptors) in the brain, blocking their normal activity and so reducing dopamine transmission, thereby reducing positive symptoms (hallucinations, delusions), consistent with the (original) dopamine hypothesis (see SCZ2); they are effective mainly against positive symptoms and have limited effect on negative symptoms. Side effects can be significant, including **extrapyramidal symptoms** (movement-related side effects, e.g. tremors, muscle stiffness, resembling Parkinson's disease, arising because typical antipsychotics also block dopamine activity in brain areas that control movement, not only those linked to psychosis) and, with long-term use, **tardive dyskinesia** (involuntary, repetitive facial/body movements which can persist even after the drug is stopped). **Atypical antipsychotics** (e.g. clozapine, risperidone), developed from around the 1970s onward, act on a somewhat broader range of receptors (including dopamine and serotonin receptors), and were developed partly to reduce the severity of the movement-related side effects associated with typical antipsychotics, while also being somewhat more effective against negative symptoms for some patients; clozapine, in particular, is often used specifically for patients who have not responded well to other antipsychotics, though it carries its own risk of a rare but serious side effect, agranulocytosis (a dangerous drop in white blood cells), requiring regular blood monitoring. Evaluation: drug therapy is relatively low-cost and non-disruptive to daily life for most patients (in contrast with intensive psychological therapy), and meta-analyses generally confirm antipsychotic drugs are significantly more effective than a placebo at reducing symptoms; however, they treat symptoms rather than necessarily any underlying cause, a meaningful proportion of patients do not respond adequately even to an adequate trial, side effects (both from typical and, to a lesser but real extent, atypical antipsychotics) can be severe and are a major cause of patients discontinuing medication, and relapse is common once medication is stopped without other support.

**Psychological treatments.** **Cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT) for psychosis** aims to help the patient identify, understand and challenge the irrational thoughts/beliefs underlying their symptoms (e.g. examining the evidence for a delusional belief, or developing alternative, less distressing explanations for a hallucinatory experience), and to develop practical coping strategies for managing residual symptoms and reducing the distress they cause, even where it does not eliminate the symptoms entirely; typically used alongside, not instead of, drug therapy. **Family therapy** works with the whole family (not just the individual patient), typically aiming to improve family communication, reduce the level of expressed emotion (see SCZ2/SCZ3) in the household, and provide the family with education and practical strategies for supporting the patient's recovery and reducing the risk of relapse; research has found family therapy is associated with reduced relapse and re-hospitalisation rates. **Token economies**: a behavioural management technique, based on operant conditioning (see APP1), typically used in an institutional setting, in which desirable behaviours (e.g. good hygiene, social engagement, taking medication as prescribed) are immediately reinforced with tokens (secondary reinforcers, having no inherent value themselves) that can later be exchanged for tangible rewards/privileges (primary reinforcers); this aims to increase desirable behaviour and quality of everyday functioning, particularly for managing negative symptoms such as avolition in an institutional setting, rather than treating the underlying disorder itself. Evaluation: CBT and family therapy both have reasonable research support for improving outcomes (e.g. reduced relapse, better everyday functioning) when used alongside drug therapy, but are more resource-intensive/time-consuming to deliver than medication alone, limiting availability; token economies raise ethical concerns (e.g. about the dignity/autonomy of institutionalised patients, and the risk of relatively powerless, vulnerable individuals in effect being coerced through denial of privileges) and their real-world use has declined considerably as institutional, long-term hospital-based care of this kind has become less common.

**The interactionist approach.** The **diathesis-stress model** (see ID3), most influentially formulated by **Meehl (1962)**, proposes schizophrenia develops from the combination of a genetic/biological vulnerability (**diathesis**) and exposure to sufficient environmental **stress** (e.g. a significant, stressful life event, or a high-expressed-emotion family environment, see SCZ2); Meehl's original version proposed a single specific genetic vulnerability ('schizotaxia') interacting with chronic stress, while more modern versions of the model recognise multiple possible diatheses (genetic, but also, e.g., prenatal complications or early psychological trauma) that can combine, in different proportions and combinations for different individuals, with different kinds of environmental stressor to trigger the disorder. The interactionist approach's practical implication for treatment is that biological (drug) and psychological (CBT, family therapy) treatments are increasingly used together, on the reasoning that treating both the underlying biological vulnerability and the environmental/psychological stress factors together is likely to be more effective than either alone -- this combined approach is now broadly reflected in official clinical treatment guidance (e.g. UK NICE guidelines recommending both antipsychotic medication and psychological therapy such as CBT and family intervention for schizophrenia, rather than either in isolation).
