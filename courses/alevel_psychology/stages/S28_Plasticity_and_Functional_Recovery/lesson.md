#### BIO5 Plasticity and functional recovery of the brain after trauma
**Plasticity.** The brain's ability to change and adapt (functionally and physically) as a result of experience and learning, throughout the whole lifespan (not, as once thought, only in early childhood development). New experiences and learning cause new synaptic connections to form and strengthen, while connections that are rarely used are weakened and eventually eliminated -- a process called **synaptic pruning** -- refining the brain's neural structure to reflect the demands actually placed on it. **Maguire et al. (2000)** found, using MRI scans, that London taxi drivers -- who must learn and navigate the city's extremely complex street layout ('The Knowledge') -- had significantly larger posterior hippocampi (a brain region associated with spatial navigation and memory) than a matched control group, and hippocampal volume correlated positively with the length of time spent as a taxi driver, providing strong evidence that extensive, sustained learning produces structural changes in the brain.

**Functional recovery after trauma.** Following physical injury (e.g. a stroke) or trauma, the brain can, to a degree, recover lost functioning by reorganising and compensating for damaged areas, in a specific form of plasticity. Mechanisms include: **axonal sprouting** (undamaged axons grow new nerve endings to reconnect with neurons whose original connection was severed by the damage, forming new neural pathways); **denervation supersensitivity** (axons that do a similar job to those damaged become more sensitive/aroused, to compensate for the loss, though this can also produce unwanted side effects, e.g. hypersensitivity to pain); and **recruitment of homologous (equivalent) areas** on the opposite hemisphere to take over specific functions originally performed by the damaged area, at least temporarily. Functional recovery is not automatic or guaranteed, and its extent is influenced by a range of individual factors, including age (recovery is often, though not universally, found to be more complete in younger individuals, whose brains tend to show somewhat greater plasticity), the person's cognitive reserve (more years of education have been associated with a greater likelihood of a good recovery, potentially because a larger existing network of connections offers more alternative routes for compensation), and factors such as an individual's overall physical health and level of stimulation/rehabilitation received during recovery. Evaluation: research into plasticity and functional recovery (e.g. Maguire's study, and clinical case evidence of recovery from stroke/brain injury) has real-world, practical value, informing rehabilitation approaches (e.g. encouraging targeted, repeated practice of lost skills to promote adaptive reorganisation); however, plasticity/recovery is not unlimited, and negative plasticity can also occur (e.g. prolonged drug use has been linked to poorer cognitive functioning through maladaptive synaptic pruning, and some functional recovery, particularly involving recruitment of homologous areas, can itself have costs, e.g. reduced capacity for other functions the newly recruited area would otherwise have performed).
