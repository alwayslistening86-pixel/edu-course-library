# S12_Explanations_of_Attachment - Lesson: Learning theory and Bowlby's monotropic theory

## Goal
The learner explains and evaluates two explanations of attachment: learning theory (cupboard love) and Bowlby's monotropic theory, including the concepts of a critical period and the internal working model.

- ATT6 - Bowlby's monotropic theory of attachment

#### ATT5 Learning theory (cupboard love) as an explanation of attachment
**Learning theory ('cupboard love').** This explanation applies behaviourist principles (see APP1) to attachment, proposing attachment is a learned behaviour, not an innate process. **Classical conditioning**: food (an unconditioned stimulus, producing pleasure, an unconditioned response) is repeatedly paired with the caregiver (initially a neutral stimulus); through this association, the caregiver becomes a conditioned stimulus who alone now produces the pleasure response (now a conditioned response), i.e. attachment. **Operant conditioning**: when an infant is hungry, this creates an uncomfortable drive state; food reduces this discomfort, acting as a **primary reinforcer**; because the caregiver is the one who supplies the food, the caregiver becomes associated with this reinforcement and becomes a **secondary reinforcer**, and the infant seeks proximity to the caregiver because this proximity itself becomes rewarding (negative reinforcement, since it removes/avoids the discomfort of the drive state). Evaluation: learning theory is strongly contradicted by evidence from animal studies (Harlow's monkeys preferred a non-feeding but comforting cloth surrogate over a feeding wire one -- see ATT4 -- directly undermining the 'cupboard love' feeding-based account) and by human research (Schaffer and Emerson found many infants' primary attachment was not to whoever fed them most, but to whoever was most responsive/interactive with them), suggesting other factors (e.g. responsiveness, contact comfort) matter more than feeding itself in forming attachment.

#### ATT6 Bowlby's monotropic theory of attachment
**Bowlby's monotropic theory.** **Bowlby (1969)** proposed attachment is an innate, evolved system with survival value, providing an evolutionary explanation. **Monotropy**: infants have an innate predisposition to form one particularly important attachment, usually to the mother (though not necessarily the biological mother), which is qualitatively different from and more important than other attachments -- Bowlby's 'law of continuity' suggested the more constant/predictable an infant's care, the better the quality of this primary attachment, and his 'law of accumulated separation' suggested the negative effects of separations from this figure add up. **Social releasers**: innate infant behaviours (e.g. crying, smiling, gripping) that elicit caregiving/attention from adults, triggering an innate adult social response that promotes proximity and interaction, and so the formation of attachment -- attachment is thus an interactive process, not something the infant does alone. **The critical (or 'sensitive') period**: Bowlby proposed a period, up to around age two (extending to age five, with the window becoming progressively more sensitive rather than a strict, all-or-nothing cutoff), during which attachment must form or it becomes much more difficult afterward. **The internal working model**: the infant's primary attachment relationship forms a mental representation/template of what relationships are like -- a blueprint the child carries forward and uses to understand and form future relationships (including their own later parenting behaviour and romantic relationships, see ATT9), giving early attachment quality a lasting, formative influence via **continuity of relationship style**. Evaluation: supported by research on social releasers (e.g. Brazelton et al. found that when adults were instructed to ignore infants' social releasers, some infants initially showed distress and eventually became motionless, illustrating their importance in eliciting a caregiving response) and by longitudinal research linking early attachment quality to later relationship outcomes (see ATT9); criticised for the concept of monotropy being difficult to test/falsify precisely, and for being culturally and historically influenced (Bowlby's original ideas emerged in a mid-20th-century context that some later argued reflected then-prevailing assumptions about mothers as primary caregivers more than purely objective science).
