# S02_Conformity_Variables_and_Social_Roles - Lesson: Variables affecting conformity, and conformity to social roles

## Goal
The learner explains the situational variables affecting conformity shown by Asch's research (group size, unanimity, task difficulty) and describes and evaluates Zimbardo's research into conformity to social roles.

- SI3 - Zimbardo's Stanford Prison Experiment

## How to teach this
Ask: if you were the only person in a room of seven strangers who all confidently gave an obviously wrong answer to an easy question, how many times out of twelve do you think you'd go along with them? Every named study, theorist and theory in this course (e.g. Asch, Milgram, Zimbardo, Adorno, Moscovici, Atkinson and Shiffrin, Baddeley and Hitch, Loftus and Palmer, Johnson and Scott, Yuille and Cutshall, Geiselman, Schaffer and Emerson, Lorenz, Harlow, Bowlby, Ainsworth, Van Ijzendoorn and Kroonenberg, Rutter, Hazan and Shaver, Beck, Ellis, Wiles, Wundt, Pavlov, Skinner, Bandura, Freud, Maslow, Rogers, Maguire, Sperry, Siffre, Gottesman, Bateson, Meehl, Buss, Walster, Kerckhoff and Davis, Thibaut and Kelley, Rusbult, Duck, McCutcheon, Dollard, Tinbergen, Wilson and Daly, Irwin and Cressey, Sykes) is a genuine, correctly attributed piece of psychology, checked against real psychological knowledge and AQA's own published subject content when this course was built -- present it as real research, never as illustrative fiction, and correct the learner explicitly if they misattribute a study or misstate a finding. Research Methods (4.2.3, at least 25% of assessment across all three papers, plus a minimum 10% mathematical-skills requirement) is examined throughout, not only in its own stages -- sampling, experimental design and inferential statistics can appear applied to any topic area. Every numerical worked example (mean, median, mode, range, standard deviation, the sign test) was computed and verified with Python when this course was built, and the learner should always show full working for a calculation, since AQA credits correct method as well as a correct final answer. Extended-writing ('Discuss...'/'Evaluate...') questions are marked holistically against AQA's levels-of-response scheme (see `rubric.json`), not by a checklist of individual points -- teach the learner to build a structured AO1 (accurate knowledge) + AO3 (effective discussion/evaluation), and where the question is scenario-based, AO2 (explicit application to the scenario) response, not a list of disconnected facts.

#### SI2 Variables affecting conformity (Asch) and conformity to social roles (Zimbardo)
**Asch's research and variables affecting conformity.** **Asch (1951)** had naive participants join a group of confederates and judge, aloud, which of three comparison lines matched a standard line -- an unambiguous, easy task. Confederates unanimously gave the same wrong answer on 12 of 18 'critical trials'. On average, participants conformed to the wrong answer on about 33% of critical trials, and 75% conformed at least once; a control group with no confederates made errors on fewer than 1% of trials, showing the wrong answers were due to group pressure, not simple misjudgement. Asch's variations identified three key situational variables: **group size** -- conformity rose with group size up to about three confederates, but adding further confederates beyond this made little additional difference (suggesting it is unanimity, not sheer numbers, that matters most); **unanimity** -- conformity dropped sharply when just one confederate gave the correct answer (or even a different wrong answer), because this broke the group's unanimous agreement and gave the participant social support; **task difficulty** -- conformity increased when the line-judgement task was made harder (lines more similar in length), consistent with informational social influence, since a more ambiguous task makes the participant less sure of their own judgement and more likely to rely on others for information. Asch's study is limited by being an artificial task with low ecological validity, being conducted with US male participants in the 1950s (a culturally and historically specific 'conformist' era), and raising ethical concerns (deception of participants about the study's true purpose).

#### SI3 Zimbardo's Stanford Prison Experiment
**Conformity to social roles: Zimbardo's Stanford Prison Experiment.** **Zimbardo et al. (1973)** set up a mock prison in the basement of Stanford University and randomly assigned emotionally stable male volunteers to the role of 'guard' or 'prisoner'. Prisoners were 'arrested' at home, given a uniform and identification number (removing individual identity), and guards were given uniforms, mirrored sunglasses and told to keep order, though not given explicit instructions to be aggressive. The planned two-week study was stopped after six days because guards became increasingly brutal and authoritarian (some devising cruel and degrading punishments), while prisoners became increasingly passive, distressed, and some had to be released early with severe psychological reactions. Zimbardo concluded that people readily conform to the social roles they are given, even when this conflicts with their personal morals, and that the situation itself (not individual dispositions -- volunteers had been screened as psychologically stable) was powerful enough to produce this extreme behaviour, supporting a situational rather than dispositional explanation of behaviour in institutions (relevant also to institutional aggression, see AGG4). Evaluation: the study has real-world application (e.g. informing understanding of the 2004 Abu Ghraib prisoner-abuse scandal) but faces criticism for lacking full experimental control (Zimbardo himself acted as prison superintendent, potentially biasing how he briefed and managed guards -- reducing internal validity), for demand characteristics (some guards later said they were 'performing' based on stereotypes of brutal guards from films), and for serious ethical issues (psychological harm to prisoners, limited informed consent about what the experience would actually involve).
