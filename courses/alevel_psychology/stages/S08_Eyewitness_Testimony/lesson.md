# S08_Eyewitness_Testimony - Lesson: Eyewitness testimony: misleading information and anxiety

## Goal
The learner explains, with research evidence, how misleading information and anxiety affect the accuracy of eyewitness testimony.

- MEM6 - Eyewitness testimony: the effect of anxiety

## How to teach this
Ask: if a friend describes a car accident to you using the word 'smashed' rather than 'hit', could that single word change what you later 'remember' seeing yourself? Every named study, theorist and theory in this course (e.g. Asch, Milgram, Zimbardo, Adorno, Moscovici, Atkinson and Shiffrin, Baddeley and Hitch, Loftus and Palmer, Johnson and Scott, Yuille and Cutshall, Geiselman, Schaffer and Emerson, Lorenz, Harlow, Bowlby, Ainsworth, Van Ijzendoorn and Kroonenberg, Rutter, Hazan and Shaver, Beck, Ellis, Wiles, Wundt, Pavlov, Skinner, Bandura, Freud, Maslow, Rogers, Maguire, Sperry, Siffre, Gottesman, Bateson, Meehl, Buss, Walster, Kerckhoff and Davis, Thibaut and Kelley, Rusbult, Duck, McCutcheon, Dollard, Tinbergen, Wilson and Daly, Irwin and Cressey, Sykes) is a genuine, correctly attributed piece of psychology, checked against real psychological knowledge and AQA's own published subject content when this course was built -- present it as real research, never as illustrative fiction, and correct the learner explicitly if they misattribute a study or misstate a finding. Research Methods (4.2.3, at least 25% of assessment across all three papers, plus a minimum 10% mathematical-skills requirement) is examined throughout, not only in its own stages -- sampling, experimental design and inferential statistics can appear applied to any topic area. Every numerical worked example (mean, median, mode, range, standard deviation, the sign test) was computed and verified with Python when this course was built, and the learner should always show full working for a calculation, since AQA credits correct method as well as a correct final answer. #### MEM5 Eyewitness testimony: misleading information (leading questions, post-event discussion)
**Misleading information and eyewitness testimony (EWT).** EWT is the evidence given in court, or to police, by a witness to a crime or accident, based on their memory; research shows this memory can be significantly distorted. **Leading questions**: a question that, by its phrasing, suggests a particular (often incorrect) answer. **Loftus and Palmer (1974)** showed participants film clips of car accidents, then asked how fast the cars were going when they 'hit' each other, with the verb varied across conditions ('contacted', 'hit', 'bumped', 'collided', 'smashed'). Estimated speed increased with the intensity of the verb (from an average of 31.8mph for 'contacted' to 40.5mph for 'smashed'); a week later, participants asked with 'smashed' were also more than twice as likely to falsely recall having seen broken glass at the scene (there was none), showing leading questions can distort both the original memory report and, over time, the memory itself. Loftus explained this with the **response-bias explanation** (the wording simply influences how participants choose to answer, without changing the underlying memory) versus the **substitution explanation** (the memory itself is altered/substituted to become consistent with the suggested information) -- the false 'broken glass' recall supports substitution, since it involved a genuinely new false memory, not just a biased response choice. **Post-event discussion**: witnesses who discuss an event with each other (or are exposed to media coverage) after it happens can experience **memory conformity** (Gabbert et al., 2003, found witnesses who discussed a witnessed event with another 'witness' who had actually seen a different version went on to incorporate details from the other person's account into their own recollection, even including things they had not personally seen) or **memory contamination** (their own memory becomes altered/combined with information from others), both reducing the accuracy of individual EWT.

#### MEM6 Eyewitness testimony: the effect of anxiety
**Anxiety and eyewitness testimony.** Research on whether high anxiety improves or impairs EWT accuracy has produced conflicting findings. Evidence for anxiety *impairing* accuracy: **Johnson and Scott (1976)** used a staged scenario in which participants overheard an argument, then saw a man leave holding either a pen (low-anxiety condition) or a blood-covered knife (high-anxiety condition, the 'weapon focus effect'); participants in the weapon condition were significantly less accurate at identifying the man from a set of photos, suggesting anxiety narrows attention onto the source of threat (the weapon) at the cost of encoding other details (such as the person's face), consistent with the wider **tunnel theory of memory**. Evidence for anxiety *improving* accuracy: **Yuille and Cutshall (1986)** interviewed real witnesses to an actual armed robbery in Canada some months after the event, and found witnesses who reported the highest stress at the time were the most accurate in their recall, even several months later, contradicting the laboratory weapon-focus findings and raising questions about how far a staged laboratory scenario captures genuine, real-world stress. These apparently conflicting findings are reconciled by the **Yerkes-Dodson law**, an inverted-U relationship between arousal and performance: a moderate level of anxiety/arousal produces optimal memory performance, but performance declines at both very low and (particularly relevant here) very high levels of anxiety -- so anxiety's effect on EWT accuracy likely depends on how far the actual anxiety level of a witness in a particular situation falls on this curve, which is difficult to establish precisely and helps explain why real crime witnesses (who may often be at only moderately high, not extreme, anxiety) can sometimes show good recall.
