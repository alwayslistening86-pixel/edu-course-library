# S14_Definitions_of_Abnormality - Lesson: Definitions of abnormality

## Goal
The learner explains and evaluates four definitions of abnormality: deviation from social norms, failure to function adequately, statistical infrequency, and deviation from ideal mental health.

## Syllabus items taught here
- PSY1 - Definitions of abnormality: statistical infrequency, deviation from social norms, failure to function adequately, deviation from ideal mental health

## How to teach this
Ask: if 'abnormal' just meant 'rare', would being a genius, or having an exceptionally happy marriage, count as a psychological abnormality? Every named study, theorist and theory in this course (e.g. Asch, Milgram, Zimbardo, Adorno, Moscovici, Atkinson and Shiffrin, Baddeley and Hitch, Loftus and Palmer, Johnson and Scott, Yuille and Cutshall, Geiselman, Schaffer and Emerson, Lorenz, Harlow, Bowlby, Ainsworth, Van Ijzendoorn and Kroonenberg, Rutter, Hazan and Shaver, Beck, Ellis, Wiles, Wundt, Pavlov, Skinner, Bandura, Freud, Maslow, Rogers, Maguire, Sperry, Siffre, Gottesman, Bateson, Meehl, Buss, Walster, Kerckhoff and Davis, Thibaut and Kelley, Rusbult, Duck, McCutcheon, Dollard, Tinbergen, Wilson and Daly, Irwin and Cressey, Sykes) is a genuine, correctly attributed piece of psychology, checked against real psychological knowledge and AQA's own published subject content when this course was built -- present it as real research, never as illustrative fiction, and correct the learner explicitly if they misattribute a study or misstate a finding. Research Methods (4.2.3, at least 25% of assessment across all three papers, plus a minimum 10% mathematical-skills requirement) is examined throughout, not only in its own stages -- sampling, experimental design and inferential statistics can appear applied to any topic area. Every numerical worked example (mean, median, mode, range, standard deviation, the sign test) was computed and verified with Python when this course was built, and the learner should always show full working for a calculation, since AQA credits correct method as well as a correct final answer. Extended-writing ('Discuss...'/'Evaluate...') questions are marked holistically against AQA's levels-of-response scheme (see `rubric.json`), not by a checklist of individual points -- teach the learner to build a structured AO1 (accurate knowledge) + AO3 (effective discussion/evaluation), and where the question is scenario-based, AO2 (explicit application to the scenario) response, not a list of disconnected facts.

#### PSY1 Definitions of abnormality: statistical infrequency, deviation from social norms, failure to function adequately, deviation from ideal mental health
AQA specifies four definitions used to decide whether behaviour/thinking counts as psychologically abnormal. **Statistical infrequency**: behaviour is abnormal if it is statistically rare/uncommon in the population, e.g. relative to a normal distribution, behaviour more than two standard deviations from the mean might be classed as abnormal (e.g. very low IQ). Limitation: rarity alone is not a good marker of abnormality -- some statistically rare traits are not undesirable or a 'disorder' (e.g. exceptionally high IQ), and some genuinely serious conditions (e.g. mild depression) are statistically common, not rare. **Deviation from social norms**: behaviour is abnormal if it violates the unwritten rules/expected standards of behaviour within a particular society or culture. Limitation: social norms vary hugely across cultures and over historical time (e.g. homosexuality was historically classified as a mental disorder in some diagnostic systems, reflecting the prejudice of the era rather than genuine psychological abnormality), so this definition risks being used to pathologise behaviour that simply differs from the majority, and can lead to culturally-biased over-diagnosis (see ID1). **Failure to function adequately**: behaviour is abnormal if a person can no longer cope with the ordinary demands of day-to-day living, e.g. Rosenhan and Seligman's criteria include personal distress, observer discomfort (causing distress to others), and irrationality/unpredictability. Limitation: who decides what counts as 'coping', and some unusual behaviour that appears not to be 'functioning' by normal standards can actually be adaptive or a rational response to unusual circumstances (e.g. extreme grief). **Deviation from ideal mental health**: **Jahoda (1958)** proposed abnormality is the absence of a set of positive criteria for good mental health, including having a positive/accurate view of the self, resistance to stress, personal growth/self-actualisation, autonomy, an accurate perception of reality, and mastery of the environment; the more of these criteria a person lacks, the more 'abnormal' they are considered. Limitation: this is an extremely demanding standard (very few people would meet all criteria all of the time), and criteria such as self-actualisation are vague, subjective and reflect a particular Western, individualistic cultural ideal rather than a universal standard of health.
