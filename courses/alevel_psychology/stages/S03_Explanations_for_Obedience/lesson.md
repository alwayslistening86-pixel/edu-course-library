#### SI4 Milgram's research and the situational variables affecting obedience (proximity, location, uniform)
**Milgram's research into obedience.** **Milgram (1963)** recruited male volunteers, told them the study was about the effects of punishment on learning, and used a rigged draw so the volunteer always became the 'teacher', with a confederate as the 'learner' in another room. The teacher was instructed by an experimenter (in a grey lab coat) to give the learner increasingly severe electric shocks (labelled 15V up to 450V, in fact fake) each time the learner made an error in a word-pair test, and to continue when the learner protested, screamed, fell silent, or when the teacher hesitated, using a standardised sequence of verbal prods ('Please continue', 'The experiment requires that you continue', 'It is absolutely essential that you continue', 'You have no other choice, you must go on'). 65% of participants continued to the maximum 450V, and all participants gave shocks of at least 300V, showing surprisingly high levels of obedience to a legitimate authority figure even when it conflicted with personal conscience; many participants showed visible signs of extreme stress (sweating, trembling, nervous laughter) while continuing to obey.

**Situational variables Milgram varied.** **Proximity**: obedience fell (to 40%) when the teacher had to be in the same room as the learner, and fell further (to 30%) when the teacher had to physically force the learner's hand onto a shock plate, since closeness makes the consequences of the action harder to ignore/distance oneself from. **Location**: obedience fell (to 47.5%) when the study was moved from prestigious Yale University to a run-down office building, since a less prestigious setting reduced the perceived legitimacy of the authority. **Uniform**: obedience fell (to 20%) when the experimenter giving instructions wore ordinary clothes rather than a lab coat (and the instructions were given by an 'ordinary member of the public' instead), since a uniform is a visible symbol of legitimate authority.

#### SI5 Explanations for obedience: legitimacy of authority and the agentic state
**Agentic state and legitimacy of authority.** Milgram's own explanation of obedience has two related parts. **Legitimacy of authority**: some people in a society are given the power to punish/give instructions to others, through a hierarchical structure most people accept as legitimate from childhood (e.g. parents, teachers, employers, the state); people are more likely to obey a figure they perceive as legitimate (having a genuine right to expect obedience within a particular context), which explains why obedience fell so sharply when Milgram's authority figure's legitimacy was undermined (a run-down office rather than Yale; no lab coat). **The agentic state**: Milgram proposed that obedience occurs because a person shifts from an 'autonomous state' (feeling personal responsibility for their own actions, guided by their own conscience) into an 'agentic state' -- seeing themselves as the agent of another person's (the authority figure's) wishes, and so no longer personally responsible for the consequences of their actions; this shift is called **agentic shift**. Remaining in the agentic state despite the personal/moral strain this produces is explained by **binding factors** -- aspects of the situation (e.g. the desire not to disrupt the smooth running of the procedure, or politeness) that keep a person locked in the agentic state even while experiencing distress. Together, legitimacy of authority (why we are prone to obey certain figures in the first place) and the agentic state (the psychological mechanism by which we then stop feeling personally responsible) form Milgram's core explanation of destructive obedience.
