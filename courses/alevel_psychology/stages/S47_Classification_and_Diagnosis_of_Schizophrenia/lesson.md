# S47_Classification_and_Diagnosis_of_Schizophrenia - Lesson: Classification and diagnosis of schizophrenia

## Goal
The learner describes the classification of schizophrenia (positive and negative symptoms) and evaluates the reliability and validity of its diagnosis.

## How to teach this
Ask: if two different psychiatrists, using two different diagnostic manuals, could reasonably disagree about whether the same patient has schizophrenia, what does that tell us about how precise this diagnosis really is? Every named study, theorist and theory in this course (e.g. Asch, Milgram, Zimbardo, Adorno, Moscovici, Atkinson and Shiffrin, Baddeley and Hitch, Loftus and Palmer, Johnson and Scott, Yuille and Cutshall, Geiselman, Schaffer and Emerson, Lorenz, Harlow, Bowlby, Ainsworth, Van Ijzendoorn and Kroonenberg, Rutter, Hazan and Shaver, Beck, Ellis, Wiles, Wundt, Pavlov, Skinner, Bandura, Freud, Maslow, Rogers, Maguire, Sperry, Siffre, Gottesman, Bateson, Meehl, Buss, Walster, Kerckhoff and Davis, Thibaut and Kelley, Rusbult, Duck, McCutcheon, Dollard, Tinbergen, Wilson and Daly, Irwin and Cressey, Sykes) is a genuine, correctly attributed piece of psychology, checked against real psychological knowledge and AQA's own published subject content when this course was built -- present it as real research, never as illustrative fiction, and correct the learner explicitly if they misattribute a study or misstate a finding. #### SCZ1 Classification of schizophrenia; reliability and validity of diagnosis
**Classification of schizophrenia.** Schizophrenia is a severe mental disorder, classified using either the **DSM-5** (Diagnostic and Statistical Manual, used mainly in the US) or the **ICD-11** (International Classification of Diseases, used mainly in the UK/Europe), which differ somewhat in their precise diagnostic criteria (e.g. DSM-5 requires at least one positive symptom to be present for diagnosis; ICD-11 does not strictly require this, allowing diagnosis based on negative symptoms alone in principle). Symptoms are grouped into two broad categories. **Positive symptoms**: atypical symptoms experienced *in addition to* normal experience (an excess/distortion of normal functioning). **Hallucinations**: sensory experiences (most commonly auditory, e.g. hearing voices, but can affect any sense) that have no genuine basis in reality, and are experienced as vivid and real by the person. **Delusions**: strong, irrational beliefs that are firmly held despite clear evidence against them (e.g. delusions of persecution -- believing oneself to be the target of a conspiracy or being watched/plotted against; delusions of grandeur -- believing oneself to have special, exaggerated powers/importance). **Negative symptoms**: atypical symptoms reflecting the *loss/reduction* of normal functioning. **Speech poverty (alogia)**: reduced quantity and/or complexity of speech. **Avolition**: a severe loss of motivation/interest in carrying out goal-directed activities, sometimes broken down into reduced motivation across the domains of work/education, close relationships, and personal hygiene/basic self-care.

**Reliability and validity of diagnosis.** **Reliability** in this context refers to the consistency of diagnosis, e.g. whether different clinicians independently arrive at the same diagnosis for the same patient (inter-rater reliability), or whether the same clinician would diagnose the same patient consistently on separate occasions (test-retest reliability); research has found reliability of schizophrenia diagnosis has historically been relatively poor compared with many physical health diagnoses, though has improved with more detailed, standardised criteria in more recent editions of DSM/ICD. **Validity** refers to whether the diagnosis genuinely identifies a distinct, real, meaningful underlying condition. Several factors undermine both reliability and validity. **Co-morbidity**: schizophrenia is frequently diagnosed alongside other conditions (e.g. depression, substance-use disorders), which complicates diagnosis and raises the possibility that what is classified as a single disorder, 'schizophrenia', might sometimes actually represent a different underlying condition, or two co-occurring but genuinely separate conditions. **Symptom overlap**: schizophrenia shares symptoms with other disorders (e.g. bipolar disorder can also involve some psychotic symptoms), making it genuinely difficult, in some cases, to reliably distinguish between them using symptom checklists alone, again reducing both reliability (different clinicians may reasonably disagree) and validity (raising doubt about whether the diagnostic categories accurately carve up genuinely distinct underlying conditions). **Culture bias**: rates of schizophrenia diagnosis have been found to vary substantially by ethnicity within the same country (e.g. UK research has found notably higher diagnosis rates among some Black ethnic groups than the White British population), which may reflect genuine cultural differences in symptom expression being misinterpreted by clinicians from a different cultural background as more severe/psychotic than intended (e.g. culturally normal reports of hearing ancestral spirits' voices being interpreted, by a clinician unfamiliar with that cultural context, as a hallucination), rather than a genuine underlying difference in rates of the disorder itself -- a form of cultural/diagnostic bias (see ID1). **Gender bias**: some research has suggested men are, on average, diagnosed with schizophrenia somewhat more often and, in some studies, at an earlier age than women, and it has been suggested women's symptoms may sometimes be under-recognised or attributed to other causes, though findings and their interpretation in this area are genuinely mixed and debated.
