#### PSY4 OCD: characteristics, genetic and neural explanations, and drug therapy
**Characteristics of OCD.** Obsessive-compulsive disorder (OCD) is classified in DSM-5/ICD-11 as involving obsessions and/or compulsions. **Behavioural characteristics**: compulsions -- repetitive behaviours or mental acts (e.g. excessive hand-washing, checking, counting) that a person feels driven to perform, often according to rigid rules, usually aimed at reducing anxiety caused by an obsession; compulsions are typically time-consuming (e.g. taking up more than an hour a day) and the person usually recognises them as excessive or irrational, yet feels unable to stop (**avoidance** of anxiety-provoking situations is also common). **Emotional characteristics**: obsessions cause significant anxiety and distress; compulsions may bring brief, temporary relief from this anxiety; the person may also feel guilt, disgust or embarrassment about their symptoms, and depression sometimes accompanies OCD due to the distressing, persistent nature of the symptoms. **Cognitive characteristics**: obsessions -- recurrent, intrusive thoughts, images or urges that the person recognises as their own but experiences as unwanted, distressing and difficult to control or ignore (e.g. an intrusive fear of contamination); cognitive strategies (e.g. avoidance, distraction) are often used, not always successfully, to try to manage these obsessive thoughts; insight into the excessive/irrational nature of the obsessions and compulsions is usually retained (distinguishing OCD from some psychotic-spectrum conditions).

**Biological explanations.** **Genetic explanation**: OCD is believed to be polygenic (influenced by the combined, small effect of many genes, not a single 'OCD gene'), with candidate genes including those affecting the neurotransmitter systems implicated in OCD, e.g. variations in the **COMT gene** (affecting dopamine regulation) and the **SERT gene** (affecting serotonin transport) have both been associated with increased OCD risk in some studies. Twin studies provide supporting evidence -- concordance rates for OCD are consistently found to be higher in monozygotic (identical) than dizygotic (non-identical) twins, though concordance is well below 100% even in identical twins, showing environmental factors also play a substantial role (OCD is also considered **aetiologically heterogeneous** -- different combinations of genes/factors may cause OCD in different people). **Neural explanation**: research points to abnormal functioning in specific brain circuits and neurotransmitter systems: lower levels of **serotonin** are associated with OCD (consistent with the effectiveness of drugs that increase serotonin activity, see below); abnormalities in the **basal ganglia** (a brain structure involved in movement/action selection, potentially linked to compulsive behaviours) and the **orbitofrontal cortex (OFC)** (involved in decision-making, potentially linked to intrusive thoughts converting into anxiety/action, sometimes described via the concept of a 'worry circuit') have both been implicated by brain-imaging research comparing OCD patients with controls.

**Drug therapy.** The main biological treatment for OCD is drug therapy, most commonly **SSRIs (selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors)**, which work by increasing the level of serotonin available in the synapse -- by blocking its reabsorption (reuptake) into the presynaptic neuron after release, more serotonin remains available to bind to receptors on the postsynaptic neuron, which is thought to help normalise the abnormal functioning (e.g. in the OFC-basal ganglia circuit) associated with OCD symptoms; SSRIs typically take several weeks to produce a noticeable improvement in symptoms. Where SSRIs alone are insufficient, they may be combined with other drugs (e.g. a low-dose antipsychotic) or with CBT. Evaluation: drug therapy is supported by evidence of effectiveness (e.g. meta-analyses of randomised, placebo-controlled trials generally find SSRIs significantly reduce OCD symptoms compared with placebo, and are relatively cost-effective and non-disruptive compared with intensive psychological therapy, requiring little effort from the patient beyond taking the medication), but is criticised for side effects (e.g. nausea, headaches, and in a minority increased anxiety, particularly when treatment starts), for a meaningful proportion of patients not responding adequately even with an adequate trial of medication, and for treating symptoms rather than necessarily any underlying psychological cause -- highlighted by high relapse rates for many patients once medication is stopped.
