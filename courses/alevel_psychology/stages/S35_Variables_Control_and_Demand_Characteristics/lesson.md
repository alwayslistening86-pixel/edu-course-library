# S35_Variables_Control_and_Demand_Characteristics - Lesson: Variables, control, demand characteristics and pilot studies

## Goal
The learner defines and operationalises variables (independent, dependent, extraneous, confounding), explains methods of controlling extraneous variables (randomisation, standardisation, counterbalancing), and explains demand characteristics, investigator effects and the role of pilot studies.

## Syllabus items taught here

## How to teach this

#### RM6 Variables (IV/DV/EV/confounding, operationalisation), control (randomisation, standardisation, counterbalancing), demand characteristics/investigator effects, and pilot studies
**Variables.** The **independent variable (IV)** is the variable the researcher manipulates (or, in a natural/quasi-experiment, that varies naturally/pre-exists) between conditions; the **dependent variable (DV)** is the variable that is measured, and which the researcher predicts will be affected by the IV. Both should be **operationalised** -- defined in precise, clearly measurable terms (e.g. 'number of words correctly recalled from a 20-item list in 2 minutes', not vaguely 'memory'). An **extraneous variable (EV)** is any variable, other than the IV, that could potentially affect the DV, but which does not necessarily vary systematically between conditions (e.g. room temperature, if kept constant) -- if not controlled, it can add unwanted 'noise' but does not necessarily bias the comparison between conditions. A **confounding variable** is a specific, more serious type of extraneous variable that varies systematically alongside the IV (i.e. differs consistently between the conditions being compared), so that it becomes impossible to know whether any observed difference in the DV is genuinely due to the IV or is actually due to this confound instead -- confounding variables specifically threaten a study's internal validity and ability to draw causal conclusions.

**Controlling extraneous variables.** **Standardisation**: ensuring all participants experience exactly the same procedures, instructions and environment (as far as possible) in every condition, so that only the IV genuinely differs between conditions. **Randomisation**: using chance methods (e.g. random allocation of participants to conditions, or randomising the order in which stimulus materials are presented) to control for the effects of an extraneous variable, by ensuring it is not systematically biased toward any particular condition. **Counterbalancing**: used specifically to control order effects in a repeated measures design (see RM2), ensuring different orders of the conditions are used equally often across the sample.

**Demand characteristics and investigator effects.** **Demand characteristics** are cues in an experimental situation that may unconsciously reveal the study's aim/hypothesis to participants, who may then (consciously or unconsciously) alter their natural behaviour as a result -- e.g. the 'please-U' effect (trying to be a 'good participant' and help confirm what they believe the hypothesis to be) or, conversely, 'screw-U' effect (deliberately behaving in the opposite way, to sabotage the study). **Investigator effects** are any unintended influence of a researcher's own behaviour or characteristics (e.g. body language, tone of voice, appearance, or unconscious bias in how they record/interpret ambiguous data) on a participant's behaviour or on the outcome of the study; these can be reduced using a **double-blind design** (in which neither the participant nor the researcher directly interacting with them knows which condition the participant is in, reducing the chance the researcher unintentionally influences behaviour or biases recording).

**Pilot studies.** A **pilot study** is a small-scale trial run of an investigation, conducted before the full, main study, allowing the researcher to test and, if necessary, refine and improve aspects of the procedure (e.g. clarity of instructions, suitability of materials, timing) before committing the time and resources needed for the full study, helping identify and fix potential problems (including possible confounding variables, or unclear/ambiguous questionnaire items) in advance, saving time and resources and improving the quality of the eventual main study.
