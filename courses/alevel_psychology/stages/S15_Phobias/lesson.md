#### PSY2 Phobias: behavioural/emotional/cognitive characteristics, the two-process model, and behavioural treatments (systematic desensitisation, flooding)
**Characteristics of phobias.** A phobia is an excessive, irrational fear of an object, place or situation, classified in DSM-5/ICD-11 as an anxiety disorder. **Behavioural characteristics**: panic (crying, screaming, or attempts to run away when confronted with the phobic stimulus), avoidance (going to great lengths to avoid contact with the feared stimulus, which can interfere significantly with daily life), and endurance (remaining in the presence of the feared stimulus but experiencing high levels of anxiety). **Emotional characteristics**: persistent, excessive anxiety/fear that is out of proportion to the actual danger posed by the stimulus, and an emotional response that is unreasonable given the objective level of threat. **Cognitive characteristics**: selective attention to the phobic stimulus (difficulty focusing on anything else once it is encountered), irrational beliefs about the stimulus (exaggerated perceptions of danger, resistant to rational argument/reasoning), and cognitive distortions (an inaccurate perception of the feared stimulus, e.g. perceiving a spider as more threatening/larger than it objectively is).

**The two-process model.** **Mowrer (1960)** proposed phobias are both acquired and maintained through learning. Phobias are **acquired** through **classical conditioning**: a neutral stimulus (e.g. a dog) is paired with a frightening/traumatic unconditioned stimulus (e.g. being bitten, producing an unconditioned fear response), so the previously neutral stimulus becomes a conditioned stimulus that alone now produces a conditioned fear response (illustrated by Watson and Rayner's 'Little Albert' study, in which a baby was conditioned to fear a white rat by pairing it with a loud, frightening noise). Phobias are then **maintained** through **operant conditioning**: avoiding the feared stimulus reduces the anxiety it would otherwise cause, which is negatively reinforcing (the removal of an unpleasant feeling strengthens the avoidance behaviour), so the avoidance behaviour -- and therefore the phobia itself -- persists over time, since the person never has the opportunity to learn the stimulus is not actually as dangerous as feared.

**Treating phobias.** **Systematic desensitisation (SD)**, developed by Wolpe, is based on **classical conditioning** and **counterconditioning** -- the principle that a new response incompatible with fear (relaxation) can replace the old fear response to the same stimulus, since the two responses cannot coexist (**reciprocal inhibition**). It involves three stages: the client is taught relaxation techniques (e.g. breathing exercises); an **anxiety hierarchy** is constructed together with the therapist, ranking feared situations related to the phobic stimulus from least to most frightening; the client is then gradually exposed to each step of the hierarchy while remaining relaxed, only progressing to the next step once fully relaxed at the current one, until the phobic stimulus itself no longer produces anxiety. **Flooding** instead involves immediate, prolonged exposure to the most frightening version of the phobic stimulus (with the client's informed consent), preventing avoidance; since a fear response cannot be maintained indefinitely, physiological exhaustion eventually occurs and the client learns, through **extinction**, that the phobic stimulus does not actually lead to harm, rapidly breaking the learned stimulus-fear association. Evaluation: SD has good research support for effectiveness with specific phobias and is well-tolerated by clients (it is not distressing to undergo), but may be less effective for phobias without a clear behavioural/avoidance component (e.g. some social phobias, which may involve more cognitive elements); flooding is highly effective and often faster than SD, but is highly unpleasant for the client (raising ethical/informed-consent considerations) and has a higher dropout rate, and symptom substitution is sometimes reported (the underlying anxiety re-emerging in a different form) if the phobia's underlying cause is not addressed.
