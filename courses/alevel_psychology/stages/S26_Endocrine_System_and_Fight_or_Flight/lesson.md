#### BIO3 The endocrine system and the fight-or-flight response
**The endocrine system.** The endocrine system works alongside the nervous system to regulate bodily functions, but communicates more slowly (over seconds to hours, rather than milliseconds) and more diffusely, using **hormones** -- chemical messengers released directly into the bloodstream by **glands**, which then travel throughout the body but only affect **target cells/organs** that have the appropriate receptors for that particular hormone, producing widespread, longer-lasting effects. The **pituitary gland**, located in the brain, is often called the 'master gland' because it controls the release of hormones from most other endocrine glands in the body (via its own hormone output, in turn regulated by the hypothalamus). Other key glands include the **adrenal glands** (located above the kidneys, releasing adrenaline and cortisol, important in the stress response, see below) and the **thyroid gland** (releasing thyroxine, which regulates metabolism, heart rate and growth).

**The fight-or-flight response.** When an organism perceives a threat/stressor, the body needs to respond rapidly to prepare for confronting (fight) or escaping (flight) the danger. On detecting a threat, the **hypothalamus** activates the **sympathetic nervous system** (see BIO1), triggering what is together called the **sympathomedullary pathway**: the sympathetic nervous system stimulates the **adrenal medulla** (the inner part of the adrenal gland) to release **adrenaline** into the bloodstream. Adrenaline produces widespread physiological changes that prepare the body for rapid action: increased heart rate and blood pressure (pumping more oxygenated blood to the muscles), increased breathing rate (more oxygen intake), diversion of blood flow away from non-essential functions (e.g. digestion) toward the muscles, pupil dilation (improving vision), and release of glucose from energy stores for immediate use by the muscles. Once the threat has passed, the parasympathetic nervous system (see BIO1) acts to reverse these changes, returning the body to its resting state. Evaluation: the fight-or-flight response has clear evolutionary/adaptive value (rapidly mobilising the body's resources to maximise the chance of survival when facing a genuine physical threat), but is criticised as an incomplete account of the modern human stress response, since it does not fit well with a broader range of possible responses to threat identified in more recent research, including a 'freeze' response (a brief period of hyper-vigilant immobility before fight or flight, which may itself have adaptive value, e.g. avoiding detection by a predator) and, in some research (particularly on female stress responses), a 'tend-and-befriend' response (seeking social support and protecting offspring, proposed as an alternative evolved strategy) -- suggesting the fight-or-flight model, based substantially on early research using male animals/participants, may not fully capture the range of human physiological stress responses.
