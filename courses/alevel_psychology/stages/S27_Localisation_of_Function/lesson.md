#### BIO4 Localisation of brain function and hemispheric lateralisation (split-brain research)
**Localisation of function.** This is the theory that different, specific areas of the brain are responsible for particular physical and psychological functions, rather than the brain acting as a single, undifferentiated mass (a competing view called equipotentiality/holistic theory). The brain's outer layer, the **cerebral cortex**, is divided into four **lobes** in each hemisphere: the **frontal lobe** (includes the **motor cortex**, at the rear of the frontal lobe, controlling voluntary movement on the opposite side of the body, with different body parts represented in different amounts of cortical space roughly proportional to the precision of movement needed -- more space for hands/face than for the torso); the **parietal lobe** (includes the **somatosensory cortex**, processing sensory information from the skin, e.g. touch, temperature, pressure, for the opposite side of the body); the **occipital lobe** (contains the **visual cortex**, processing visual information, with each eye sending information to both hemispheres' visual cortex via partially crossing pathways); and the **temporal lobe** (contains the **auditory cortex**, processing sound/auditory information). Language is also localised: **Broca's area**, in the frontal lobe (typically the left hemisphere in most people), is associated with speech production -- damage produces **Broca's aphasia**, characterised by slow, non-fluent, laboured speech, though comprehension is largely intact; **Wernicke's area**, in the temporal lobe (also typically left hemisphere), is associated with language comprehension/understanding -- damage produces **Wernicke's aphasia**, characterised by fluent but often meaningless or nonsensical speech, and poor comprehension of others' speech.

**Hemispheric lateralisation and split-brain research.** The brain's two hemispheres are somewhat specialised for different functions (lateralisation), e.g. language is typically more left-hemisphere dominant, while some visuo-spatial/facial-recognition tasks show more right-hemisphere dominance. The **corpus callosum** is the bundle of nerve fibres connecting the two hemispheres, normally allowing them to communicate and share information. **Sperry's (1968)** split-brain research studied patients who had undergone a **commissurotomy** (surgical severing of the corpus callosum, historically used as a last-resort treatment for severe epilepsy), effectively separating the two hemispheres from directly communicating. By presenting visual information to only one visual field at a time (each visual field projects, due to the crossing visual pathways, information initially only to the opposite hemisphere in a split-brain patient), Sperry showed patients could verbally name an object shown to the right visual field (processed by the left, typically language-dominant hemisphere) but could not verbally name (though could often still select/point to with the left hand) an object shown only to the left visual field (processed by the right hemisphere, which in most people lacks the capacity for speech), providing strong evidence for hemispheric specialisation of function, particularly for language. Evaluation: localisation is supported by clinical case evidence (e.g. Broca's and Wernicke's original patients, and modern brain-imaging studies confirming these functional areas), but is challenged by evidence of brain plasticity (see BIO5) showing other brain areas can sometimes take over functions after damage, suggesting localisation is not always fixed or absolute, and some researchers argue higher-order functions in particular may rely on more widely distributed brain networks rather than single, strictly localised areas.
