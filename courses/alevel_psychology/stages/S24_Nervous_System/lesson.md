#### BIO1 The nervous system: central and peripheral (somatic and autonomic) divisions
**The nervous system.** The nervous system is a specialised network of cells (neurons) that allows communication throughout the body, enabling coordinated action and the processing of information from inside and outside the body. It has two main divisions. The **central nervous system (CNS)** consists of the brain and spinal cord: the brain is the centre of conscious awareness, controls most bodily functions, and includes the cerebral cortex (higher-order thinking, see BIO4); the spinal cord connects the brain to the peripheral nervous system and controls spinal reflexes (rapid, automatic responses to a stimulus, e.g. withdrawing a hand from a hot surface, which do not require conscious brain processing). The **peripheral nervous system (PNS)** connects the CNS to the rest of the body (limbs and organs), transmitting sensory information to the CNS and motor instructions from the CNS to muscles and glands, and is itself divided into two sub-systems. The **somatic nervous system (SNS)** governs voluntary, conscious muscle movement (e.g. moving an arm), and also carries sensory information from receptors (e.g. sight, sound, touch) to the CNS. The **autonomic nervous system (ANS)** governs involuntary, automatic functioning of internal organs and glands (e.g. heart rate, digestion, breathing rate) and is itself divided into two further branches with broadly opposing effects: the **sympathetic nervous system**, which is dominant in stressful, arousing 'fight-or-flight' situations (see BIO3), increasing physiological arousal (e.g. increasing heart rate, diverting blood to muscles); and the **parasympathetic nervous system**, which is dominant in calm, restful conditions, generally reversing the sympathetic changes and returning the body toward its resting state ('rest and digest').
