# S32_Other_Research_Methods - Lesson: Other research methods: observation, self-report, case studies, content analysis

## Goal
The learner describes and evaluates observational methods, self-report methods (questionnaires, interviews), case studies and content analysis.

## Syllabus items taught here
- RM3 - Other research methods: observation, self-report, case studies, content analysis

## How to teach this
Ask: if you wanted to genuinely understand how children play together, would you learn more from secretly watching them in a real playground, or from asking them afterwards to describe what they did? Every named study, theorist and theory in this course (e.g. Asch, Milgram, Zimbardo, Adorno, Moscovici, Atkinson and Shiffrin, Baddeley and Hitch, Loftus and Palmer, Johnson and Scott, Yuille and Cutshall, Geiselman, Schaffer and Emerson, Lorenz, Harlow, Bowlby, Ainsworth, Van Ijzendoorn and Kroonenberg, Rutter, Hazan and Shaver, Beck, Ellis, Wiles, Wundt, Pavlov, Skinner, Bandura, Freud, Maslow, Rogers, Maguire, Sperry, Siffre, Gottesman, Bateson, Meehl, Buss, Walster, Kerckhoff and Davis, Thibaut and Kelley, Rusbult, Duck, McCutcheon, Dollard, Tinbergen, Wilson and Daly, Irwin and Cressey, Sykes) is a genuine, correctly attributed piece of psychology, checked against real psychological knowledge and AQA's own published subject content when this course was built -- present it as real research, never as illustrative fiction, and correct the learner explicitly if they misattribute a study or misstate a finding. Research Methods (4.2.3, at least 25% of assessment across all three papers, plus a minimum 10% mathematical-skills requirement) is examined throughout, not only in its own stages -- sampling, experimental design and inferential statistics can appear applied to any topic area. Every numerical worked example (mean, median, mode, range, standard deviation, the sign test) was computed and verified with Python when this course was built, and the learner should always show full working for a calculation, since AQA credits correct method as well as a correct final answer. Extended-writing ('Discuss...'/'Evaluate...') questions are marked holistically against AQA's levels-of-response scheme (see `rubric.json`), not by a checklist of individual points -- teach the learner to build a structured AO1 (accurate knowledge) + AO3 (effective discussion/evaluation), and where the question is scenario-based, AO2 (explicit application to the scenario) response, not a list of disconnected facts.

#### RM3 Other research methods: observation, self-report, case studies, content analysis
**Observational methods.** Involves watching and recording behaviour as it naturally occurs, without manipulating variables. **Naturalistic observation** takes place in the setting where the behaviour would normally occur (high ecological validity, but less control, and behaviour cannot easily be replicated in a matched setting); **controlled observation** takes place in a structured/artificial setting, with the researcher controlling some variables (more control and easier replication, but lower ecological validity). Observations can also be **covert** (participants are unaware they are being observed, reducing demand characteristics but raising ethical/consent concerns) or **overt** (participants know they are being observed, addressing ethical concerns but risking demand characteristics/altered natural behaviour); and **participant** (the observer is also part of the group/situation being observed, potentially gaining unique insight but risking a loss of objectivity) or **non-participant** (the observer remains separate from those being observed, more objective but potentially missing detail only accessible from within the group). To record behaviour systematically, researchers develop **behavioural categories** -- clearly operationalised, observable and measurable categories of behaviour (e.g. 'hitting', 'sharing a toy') that a target behaviour is coded into, avoiding vague, overlapping or ambiguous categories, and use **event sampling** (recording every instance of a specific pre-defined behaviour/event, whenever it occurs) or **time sampling** (recording behaviour only at set, regular time intervals, e.g. every 30 seconds) to manage what is often continuous behaviour into a systematic, analysable record.

**Self-report methods.** Involves asking participants directly to report on their own thoughts, feelings or behaviour. **Questionnaires** use written questions, which can be **open** (allowing free, unrestricted responses, generating rich qualitative data but harder to analyse/quantify systematically) or **closed** (offering a fixed set of response options, e.g. yes/no or a rating scale, producing quantitative data that is easier to analyse but may not capture a respondent's true view fully); careful question design is needed to avoid ambiguity, leading/loaded questions and double-barrelled questions (asking about two things at once). **Interviews** involve a researcher asking questions directly (in person, by phone, etc.), and can be **structured** (a fixed, pre-determined set of questions asked in the same order to every participant, giving good replicability but limited flexibility to follow up interesting responses) or **unstructured** (a free-flowing conversation guided by a general aim rather than fixed questions, allowing rich, detailed and flexible data but making comparison between participants and replication far more difficult; **semi-structured** interviews combine some fixed questions with the flexibility to follow up). Self-report methods generally risk **social desirability bias** (participants answering in a way they believe is more socially acceptable, rather than fully honestly).

**Case studies.** A detailed, in-depth investigation of a single individual, small group, institution or event, typically drawing on multiple sources/methods of data (e.g. observation, interview, testing) over an extended period, often used to study rare phenomena (e.g. unusual brain damage, such as patient HM, see MEM1) that could not otherwise be studied experimentally. Strength: provides rich, detailed, holistic insight that broader methods cannot capture, and allows study of otherwise inaccessible/rare cases. Limitation: findings from a single case cannot be confidently generalised to the wider population, and case studies often rely substantially on the researcher's own subjective interpretation, retrospective/incomplete information, and the case's own possibly unreliable recall, all reducing objectivity.

**Content analysis.** A method for systematically analysing the content of qualitative material (e.g. media output, transcripts, diaries) by converting it into quantitative data, typically through developing coding units/categories (similar in principle to behavioural categories in observation) and counting instances of these within the material, sometimes further developed into **thematic analysis**, which instead identifies and analyses qualitative themes/patterns of meaning within the material rather than purely quantifying it. Strength: allows systematic, replicable, objective analysis of naturally occurring, often very rich qualitative material, with high ecological validity since the material was not created for the research; limitation: coding categories can still involve subjective researcher judgement (affecting reliability if not clearly and consistently applied), and analysis is limited to the content that already exists, rather than material specifically designed to answer the research question.
