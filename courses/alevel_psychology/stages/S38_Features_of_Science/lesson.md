# S38_Features_of_Science - Lesson: Features of science, report writing, peer review and economic implications

## Goal
The learner explains features of science (objectivity, empirical method, replicability, falsifiability, theory construction, hypothesis testing, paradigms and paradigm shifts), the structure of a scientific report, the role of peer review, and the economic implications of psychological research.

## Syllabus items taught here
- RM9 - Features of science, scientific report writing, peer review, and economic implications of research

## How to teach this
Ask: what actually makes a claim 'scientific' -- is it that it's true, or is it something else about how the claim could, in principle, be shown to be wrong? Every named study, theorist and theory in this course (e.g. Asch, Milgram, Zimbardo, Adorno, Moscovici, Atkinson and Shiffrin, Baddeley and Hitch, Loftus and Palmer, Johnson and Scott, Yuille and Cutshall, Geiselman, Schaffer and Emerson, Lorenz, Harlow, Bowlby, Ainsworth, Van Ijzendoorn and Kroonenberg, Rutter, Hazan and Shaver, Beck, Ellis, Wiles, Wundt, Pavlov, Skinner, Bandura, Freud, Maslow, Rogers, Maguire, Sperry, Siffre, Gottesman, Bateson, Meehl, Buss, Walster, Kerckhoff and Davis, Thibaut and Kelley, Rusbult, Duck, McCutcheon, Dollard, Tinbergen, Wilson and Daly, Irwin and Cressey, Sykes) is a genuine, correctly attributed piece of psychology, checked against real psychological knowledge and AQA's own published subject content when this course was built -- present it as real research, never as illustrative fiction, and correct the learner explicitly if they misattribute a study or misstate a finding. Research Methods (4.2.3, at least 25% of assessment across all three papers, plus a minimum 10% mathematical-skills requirement) is examined throughout, not only in its own stages -- sampling, experimental design and inferential statistics can appear applied to any topic area. Every numerical worked example (mean, median, mode, range, standard deviation, the sign test) was computed and verified with Python when this course was built, and the learner should always show full working for a calculation, since AQA credits correct method as well as a correct final answer. Extended-writing ('Discuss...'/'Evaluate...') questions are marked holistically against AQA's levels-of-response scheme (see `rubric.json`), not by a checklist of individual points -- teach the learner to build a structured AO1 (accurate knowledge) + AO3 (effective discussion/evaluation), and where the question is scenario-based, AO2 (explicit application to the scenario) response, not a list of disconnected facts.

#### RM9 Features of science, scientific report writing, peer review, and economic implications of research
**Features of science.** **Objectivity**: research should be conducted without the researcher's own personal opinions, expectations or biases influencing data collection/interpretation, so far as possible, achieved through controlled methods and standardised procedures. The **empirical method**: gathering evidence through direct observation and experiment, rather than through reasoning, intuition or belief alone, so that theories are tested against real-world data. **Replicability**: the extent to which the exact procedures of a study can be repeated (by the same or different researchers) to see whether similar results are obtained; requires clear, precise, standardised, fully-reported procedures, and consistent replication is important for confirming a finding's validity and generalisability. **Falsifiability**: **Popper** argued that a genuinely scientific theory must be capable, in principle, of being proven false/refuted by evidence -- a theory that cannot be falsified by any conceivable observation (e.g. some psychodynamic concepts, see APP5) is not a properly scientific theory, however plausible it seems; a good scientific theory survives repeated, genuine attempts to falsify it, rather than merely accumulating confirming evidence. **Theory construction and hypothesis testing**: a scientific theory is a set of general principles/laws that can explain and predict a range of phenomena; theories generate testable hypotheses, which are then tested empirically, with evidence supporting or challenging the theory, allowing it to be refined, developed or (if repeatedly falsified) rejected in favour of a better one -- an ongoing, self-correcting cyclical process. **Paradigms and paradigm shifts**: **Kuhn** argued a mature science is characterised by broad agreement, within its scientific community, on a shared set of basic assumptions and ways of working -- a **paradigm**; when accumulating evidence that cannot be explained within the current paradigm reaches a critical point (a 'crisis'), a **paradigm shift** occurs -- a scientific revolution in which the old paradigm is replaced by a new one that better accounts for the evidence. Kuhn controversially argued psychology, given its range of competing/co-existing approaches (see APP1-APP6), may not yet have achieved a single, unified paradigm in the way physical sciences like physics have, raising debate over how far psychology fully qualifies as a mature science.

**Scientific report writing.** Psychological research is conventionally reported in a standardised structure: the **abstract** (a brief summary of the whole study); the **introduction** (reviews relevant background theory/research and states the study's aim(s) and hypothesis/hypotheses); the **method** (describes, in enough precise detail to allow replication, the design, participants, apparatus/materials, procedure and ethical considerations); the **results** (presents the findings, typically including descriptive statistics and the outcome of any inferential test, see RM10); the **discussion** (interprets the findings, relates them back to existing theory/research, considers limitations, and suggests implications/future research); and **referencing** (a full, standardised list of all sources cited, allowing readers to locate and verify the original material).

**Peer review.** Before publication in an academic journal, a submitted study is typically sent by the journal's editor to other independent experts in the same field (peers), who scrutinise its quality, methodology, validity of conclusions and originality, and recommend whether it should be published, revised, or rejected. This process aims to maintain the quality, credibility and objectivity of published research, filtering out seriously flawed studies before they reach the wider scientific community and public; however, peer review is criticised for potential reviewer bias (e.g. favouring research that confirms established/mainstream theoretical positions over more innovative or unconventional findings, or 'file drawer'/publication bias -- a tendency to publish only positive, statistically significant results, distorting the overall published evidence base), and it cannot detect deliberate fraud or fabricated data with certainty.

**Economic implications of psychological research.** Psychological research and its findings can have real economic consequences and value for society, e.g.: effective treatments for mental health conditions (see PSY2-PSY4, SCZ4-SCZ5) can reduce the substantial economic costs of lost working days/productivity and healthcare/welfare spending associated with untreated mental illness; research into attachment and childcare (see ATT1-ATT10) has informed workplace policies such as parental leave; and research into effective study/memory techniques (e.g. the cognitive interview, see MEM7, informing more efficient, accurate police investigations) can have knock-on economic benefits by improving the efficiency and fairness of important social institutions.
