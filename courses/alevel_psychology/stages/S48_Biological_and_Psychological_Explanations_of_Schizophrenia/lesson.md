## How to teach this
Ask: if schizophrenia partly runs in families, does that necessarily mean it's 'in the genes' -- or could a shared, difficult family environment explain the same pattern? Every named study, theorist and theory in this course (e.g. Asch, Milgram, Zimbardo, Adorno, Moscovici, Atkinson and Shiffrin, Baddeley and Hitch, Loftus and Palmer, Johnson and Scott, Yuille and Cutshall, Geiselman, Schaffer and Emerson, Lorenz, Harlow, Bowlby, Ainsworth, Van Ijzendoorn and Kroonenberg, Rutter, Hazan and Shaver, Beck, Ellis, Wiles, Wundt, Pavlov, Skinner, Bandura, Freud, Maslow, Rogers, Maguire, Sperry, Siffre, Gottesman, Bateson, Meehl, Buss, Walster, Kerckhoff and Davis, Thibaut and Kelley, Rusbult, Duck, McCutcheon, Dollard, Tinbergen, Wilson and Daly, Irwin and Cressey, Sykes) is a genuine, correctly attributed piece of psychology, checked against real psychological knowledge and AQA's own published subject content when this course was built -- present it as real research, never as illustrative fiction, and correct the learner explicitly if they misattribute a study or misstate a finding. Research Methods (4.2.3, at least 25% of assessment across all three papers, plus a minimum 10% mathematical-skills requirement) is examined throughout, not only in its own stages -- sampling, experimental design and inferential statistics can appear applied to any topic area. Every numerical worked example (mean, median, mode, range, standard deviation, the sign test) was computed and verified with Python when this course was built, and the learner should always show full working for a calculation, since AQA credits correct method as well as a correct final answer. Extended-writing ('Discuss...'/'Evaluate...') questions are marked holistically against AQA's levels-of-response scheme (see `rubric.json`), not by a checklist of individual points -- teach the learner to build a structured AO1 (accurate knowledge) + AO3 (effective discussion/evaluation), and where the question is scenario-based, AO2 (explicit application to the scenario) response, not a list of disconnected facts.

#### SCZ2 Biological explanations of schizophrenia: genetics and neural correlates (dopamine hypothesis)
**Biological explanations.** **Genetic explanation**: schizophrenia is understood as **polygenic** (influenced by the combined small effect of many genes, no single 'schizophrenia gene') and **aetiologically heterogeneous** (different combinations of genetic and other factors may lead to schizophrenia in different individuals). Twin studies (**Gottesman's** large-scale family study) found progressively higher concordance rates for schizophrenia with closer genetic relatedness (e.g. concordance around 48% for identical/MZ twins, compared with around 17% for non-identical/DZ twins, and lower again for more distant relatives), strongly suggesting a substantial genetic contribution, though the well below 100% MZ concordance also shows genetic factors alone cannot fully account for schizophrenia -- environmental factors clearly also play a significant role (consistent with the diathesis-stress model, see ID3 and SCZ5). **Neural explanation -- the dopamine hypothesis**: the original ('classic') dopamine hypothesis proposed schizophrenia (particularly its positive symptoms) results from **hyperdopaminergia** -- abnormally high levels of dopamine activity, especially in the subcortical brain regions associated with the transmission of speech and thought to the frontal lobes; this was substantially supported by the finding that drugs which increase dopamine activity (e.g. amphetamines) can induce psychotic, schizophrenia-like symptoms in people without the disorder, and drugs which reduce dopamine activity (antipsychotics, see SCZ4) can reduce psychotic symptoms in people with schizophrenia. The **revised (updated) dopamine hypothesis** proposes a more nuanced picture, combining hyperdopaminergia in subcortical brain regions (associated with positive symptoms) with, at the same time, **hypodopaminergia** -- abnormally low levels of dopamine activity -- in the prefrontal cortex (associated instead with negative symptoms and cognitive deficits), better accounting for the full range of positive and negative symptoms than the original hypothesis alone.

**Psychological explanations.** **Family dysfunction**: several theories propose that abnormal, dysfunctional patterns of family communication/relating contribute to the development (or relapse) of schizophrenia. **Bateson's double bind theory** proposes that a child who repeatedly receives contradictory messages from a parent (e.g. verbal affection expressed alongside non-verbal rejection/hostility), such that any response risks being seen as wrong, may develop a confused, internally contradictory understanding of reality and their own relationships, reflected later in disorganised thinking/behaviour resembling schizophrenic symptoms. **Expressed emotion (EE)** refers to the family communication style, particularly toward a family member who already has schizophrenia, characterised by high levels of criticism, hostility and emotional over-involvement (e.g. excessive, intrusive concern); a high-EE family environment is a well-established risk factor specifically for **relapse** in people who already have a schizophrenia diagnosis, acting as a source of chronic stress that can trigger a return of symptoms (relevant to the diathesis-stress model, since EE may act as the environmental stressor triggering the disorder or a relapse in someone with an existing biological vulnerability). **Cognitive explanations**: propose that schizophrenia involves genuinely dysfunctional patterns of thinking/information processing -- for example, difficulty with **metarepresentation** (the cognitive ability to reflect on and monitor one's own thoughts and behaviour, and interpret the actions of others), impairment of which could explain both hallucinations (e.g. misattributing one's own internally generated thoughts to an external source, experienced as a 'voice') and delusions (difficulty distinguishing internally generated ideas/intentions from reality); this cognitive dysfunction is generally treated as a proximate (immediate, mechanistic) rather than ultimate/original cause, since the cognitive approach itself does not fully explain why this dysfunctional processing arises in the first place, and researchers increasingly view it as a downstream consequence of the underlying neural/biological abnormalities described above rather than a wholly separate, competing cause. Evaluation: biological explanations have strong empirical support (from twin studies, and from the effectiveness of dopamine-based drug treatments, see SCZ4) but genetic and purely neural accounts alone do not fully explain why concordance is well below 100% even in identical twins, or why onset is often triggered by specific stressful life events, both pointing toward a combined, interactionist diathesis-stress account (see SCZ5) rather than any single explanation in isolation.

#### SCZ3 Psychological explanations of schizophrenia: family dysfunction and cognitive explanations
**Further evaluation of the psychological explanations.** Research support for family dysfunction explanations is mixed and has been criticised on methodological grounds: much early supporting evidence (e.g. for Bateson's double bind theory) came from retrospective, potentially biased accounts (parents' or patients' own recollections of past family interactions, gathered after a schizophrenia diagnosis had already been made, which may be distorted or selectively remembered), rather than direct, prospective observation of family interaction before onset, making it hard to establish that dysfunctional family communication genuinely causes schizophrenia rather than simply being a consequence of, or a reaction to, living with a family member who already has the disorder (i.e. a problem of establishing the direction of causality). Family-based explanations have also been criticised, particularly historically, for placing undue blame on parents (especially mothers, in some earlier versions of these theories, sometimes referred to critically as the 'schizophrenogenic mother' concept) for a condition increasingly understood as having a substantial, independent biological basis, which caused significant additional distress to families at the time. Expressed emotion, by contrast, has considerably stronger, more direct research support specifically for its role in relapse (rather than in original onset), with studies consistently finding patients returning to a high-EE family environment after a schizophrenic episode are significantly more likely to relapse than those returning to a low-EE environment, and this research has had genuine practical value, informing family-based psychoeducation interventions aimed at reducing expressed emotion and so reducing relapse risk. Cognitive explanations have reasonable indirect research support (e.g. studies using specific cognitive tasks have found people with schizophrenia perform differently from controls in ways consistent with metarepresentation deficits) and have informed cognitive behavioural treatment approaches (see SCZ5), but are criticised for being a relatively incomplete, proximate-level explanation on their own -- they describe the cognitive mechanism by which certain symptoms might arise, without fully explaining the deeper, ultimate biological or environmental origin of that cognitive dysfunction in the first place, which is why cognitive and biological explanations are increasingly seen as complementary, operating at different levels of explanation (see ID4), rather than as directly competing accounts.
