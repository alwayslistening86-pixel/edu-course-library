# S31_Experimental_Methods_and_Design - Lesson: Experimental methods and experimental design

## Goal
The learner distinguishes laboratory, field, natural and quasi-experiments, and explains the three experimental designs (repeated measures, independent groups, matched pairs) with their strengths and limitations.

## Syllabus items taught here
- RM1 - Types of experiment: laboratory, field, natural, quasi
- RM2 - Experimental design: independent groups, repeated measures, matched pairs

## How to teach this
Ask: if you wanted to test whether noise affects concentration, would testing people in a soundproofed lab tell you the same thing as testing people in their normal, noisy workplace -- and would either tell you what you'd learn from comparing people who already work in quiet versus noisy jobs? Every named study, theorist and theory in this course (e.g. Asch, Milgram, Zimbardo, Adorno, Moscovici, Atkinson and Shiffrin, Baddeley and Hitch, Loftus and Palmer, Johnson and Scott, Yuille and Cutshall, Geiselman, Schaffer and Emerson, Lorenz, Harlow, Bowlby, Ainsworth, Van Ijzendoorn and Kroonenberg, Rutter, Hazan and Shaver, Beck, Ellis, Wiles, Wundt, Pavlov, Skinner, Bandura, Freud, Maslow, Rogers, Maguire, Sperry, Siffre, Gottesman, Bateson, Meehl, Buss, Walster, Kerckhoff and Davis, Thibaut and Kelley, Rusbult, Duck, McCutcheon, Dollard, Tinbergen, Wilson and Daly, Irwin and Cressey, Sykes) is a genuine, correctly attributed piece of psychology, checked against real psychological knowledge and AQA's own published subject content when this course was built -- present it as real research, never as illustrative fiction, and correct the learner explicitly if they misattribute a study or misstate a finding. Research Methods (4.2.3, at least 25% of assessment across all three papers, plus a minimum 10% mathematical-skills requirement) is examined throughout, not only in its own stages -- sampling, experimental design and inferential statistics can appear applied to any topic area. Every numerical worked example (mean, median, mode, range, standard deviation, the sign test) was computed and verified with Python when this course was built, and the learner should always show full working for a calculation, since AQA credits correct method as well as a correct final answer. Extended-writing ('Discuss...'/'Evaluate...') questions are marked holistically against AQA's levels-of-response scheme (see `rubric.json`), not by a checklist of individual points -- teach the learner to build a structured AO1 (accurate knowledge) + AO3 (effective discussion/evaluation), and where the question is scenario-based, AO2 (explicit application to the scenario) response, not a list of disconnected facts.

#### RM1 Types of experiment: laboratory, field, natural, quasi
**Types of experiment.** An experiment tests a causal hypothesis by manipulating an **independent variable (IV)** and measuring its effect on a **dependent variable (DV)**, while trying to control extraneous variables. A **laboratory experiment** is conducted in a controlled, artificial environment, with the researcher able to directly manipulate the IV and tightly control extraneous variables; strength: high control allows confident causal conclusions and is easily replicated; limitation: the artificial setting can reduce ecological validity, and participants may be more likely to show demand characteristics, knowing they are being studied. A **field experiment** is conducted in a more natural, everyday setting, with the researcher still directly manipulating the IV; strength: higher ecological validity than a lab experiment, and participants often unaware they are being studied (reducing demand characteristics); limitation: less control over extraneous variables, and ethical issues can arise around informed consent if participants do not know they are in a study. A **natural experiment** studies the effect of an IV that occurs naturally, which the researcher does not manipulate (e.g. comparing outcomes before and after a real policy change, or between naturally occurring groups); strength: allows research into variables that could not ethically or practically be manipulated directly; limitation: cannot randomly allocate participants to conditions, so cause and effect is harder to establish confidently (a naturally occurring IV may be confounded with other differences between groups), and such naturally occurring events are rare, limiting when this method can be used. A **quasi-experiment** has an IV based on an existing difference between people (e.g. age, sex, or a diagnosed condition) rather than one the researcher manipulates or that occurs from a natural event; strength: allows comparison of groups that could not ethically be created by manipulation; limitation: participants cannot be randomly allocated to conditions (since the 'IV' is a pre-existing characteristic), so cause and effect cannot be established with full confidence, and extraneous participant variables may differ systematically between the groups being compared.

#### RM2 Experimental design: independent groups, repeated measures, matched pairs
**Experimental design.** This refers to how participants are allocated to the conditions of an experiment. **Independent groups design**: different participants are used in each condition of the experiment; strength: no order effects (e.g. practice or fatigue from doing multiple conditions) and reduces the chance participants guess the study's aim from experiencing multiple conditions; limitation: participant variables (individual differences between the different people in each group) are not controlled, potentially confounding the results, and it requires more participants overall than repeated measures for the same amount of data. **Repeated measures design**: the same participants take part in every condition of the experiment; strength: participant variables are controlled, since the same people are compared with themselves, and fewer participants are needed; limitation: order effects (e.g. practice effects improving performance in a later condition, or fatigue/boredom effects worsening it) can confound results, and participants may be more likely to guess the aim of the study (having experienced all conditions), leading to demand characteristics. Order effects in a repeated measures design can be controlled using **counterbalancing** (e.g. using an 'ABBA' design, where half the participants do condition A then B, and the other half do B then A, so order effects are balanced out across the whole sample rather than eliminated within any one participant). **Matched pairs design**: different participants are used in each condition (as in independent groups), but they are matched, individually, on key relevant characteristics likely to affect the DV (e.g. age, IQ), with one of each matched pair placed in each condition; strength: reduces (though does not fully eliminate) the impact of participant variables compared with an unmatched independent groups design, while still avoiding order effects; limitation: it is time-consuming and often difficult/impossible to match participants closely on every relevant variable, and it still requires as many participants as independent groups design.
