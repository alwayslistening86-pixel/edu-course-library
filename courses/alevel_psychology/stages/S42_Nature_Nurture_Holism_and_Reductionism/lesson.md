#### ID3 The nature-nurture debate and the interactionist approach (diathesis-stress, epigenetics)
**The nature-nurture debate.** This concerns the relative importance of innate, inherited ('nature') factors versus environmental, experiential ('nurture') factors in determining behaviour. Nature-focused explanations emphasise heredity (genes) as the primary influence on behaviour (see APP4's genetic basis of behaviour); nurture-focused explanations emphasise the role of learning and experience (e.g. behaviourism, see APP1). Historically the debate was often framed as an either/or question, but the contemporary, broadly accepted position is **interactionism**: nature and nurture interact and cannot be meaningfully or fully separated, since even a strongly genetically influenced characteristic is expressed through, and shaped by, the environment the individual develops in (recall the genotype/phenotype distinction, see APP4). The **diathesis-stress model** is an influential example of interactionism, proposing that a person may have a genetic/biological vulnerability (a diathesis) to a disorder (e.g. schizophrenia, see SCZ2), but this vulnerability alone is not usually sufficient -- the disorder typically develops only if the person also experiences a sufficient environmental trigger/stressor. Related to this, **epigenetics** is the study of how environmental factors/life experiences can change the way genes are expressed (switched 'on' or 'off') without changing the underlying genetic code (DNA sequence) itself, and how these changes can, in some cases, even be passed on to future generations -- providing a further, more precise biological mechanism for how nature and nurture genuinely interact, rather than acting as wholly separate influences. Evaluation: interactionist positions like the diathesis-stress model are generally considered more scientifically defensible and better supported by evidence than a pure nature-only or pure nurture-only account, but interactionism can also make research and theory harder to test cleanly (since isolating the precise, separable contribution of nature versus nurture to a specific outcome becomes much more difficult once their interaction, rather than their separate effects, is the focus).

#### ID4 Holism versus reductionism, including levels of explanation
**Holism and reductionism.** This debate concerns whether behaviour is best understood as a whole, unified, unique phenomenon, or by breaking it down into smaller, simpler component parts. **Holism** argues that behaviour can only be properly understood by studying the whole system/person/situation, since a complex phenomenon is 'more than the sum of its parts' -- reducing it to smaller components risks losing meaning that exists only at the level of the whole system (e.g. humanistic psychology's person-centred, holistic approach, see APP6, or social psychological explanations that consider the whole social situation, e.g. Zimbardo's situational account, see SI3). Strength: can produce a fuller, more complete, meaningful and ecologically valid account of complex behaviour; limitation: can become vague, imprecise and difficult to scientifically test compared with a more reductionist approach. **Reductionism** argues complex behaviour is best explained by breaking it down into smaller, simpler constituent parts/component processes. **Levels of explanation** in psychology can be arranged from the highest, most holistic to the lowest, most reductionist: socio-cultural level (explanations in terms of the wider social/cultural context, e.g. the influence of culture on attachment, see ATT8) -> psychological level (explanations in terms of cognitive/psychological processes, e.g. Beck's negative triad, see PSY3) -> physiological level (explanations in terms of the nervous system/brain structures, e.g. localisation, see BIO4) -> neurochemical level (explanations in terms of neurotransmitters/hormones, e.g. serotonin and depression). **Biological reductionism**: explaining complex psychological phenomena in terms of relatively simple biological factors (e.g. genes, neurochemistry) -- strength: allows precise, testable, scientifically credible explanations and has led to effective biological treatments; limitation: risks oversimplifying complex behaviour, and ignoring important psychological/social/contextual influences. **Environmental (stimulus-response) reductionism**: explaining complex behaviour purely in terms of simple stimulus-response associations/learned links (central to behaviourism, see APP1) -- strength: allows objective, scientifically testable explanations using controlled experimental methods; limitation: risks oversimplifying behaviour by ignoring internal mental/cognitive processes (e.g. thought, emotion) that mediate between a stimulus and a response. Evaluation: reductionist explanations are generally easier to test scientifically (a strength for the empirical, falsifiable science AQA's issues-and-debates content values, see RM9), but a fully reductionist account risks 'losing' something important about complex, real human behaviour and experience that only emerges at a more holistic level of explanation -- many psychologists now favour combining levels of explanation (an interactionist, rather than a purely reductionist or purely holistic, position) to give the fullest possible account.
