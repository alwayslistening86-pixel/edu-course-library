## Goal

## How to teach this
Ask: if a relationship gives you plenty of rewards but you know a much better option is realistically out there for you, does that alone make you more likely to leave -- or does something else matter more? Every named study, theorist and theory in this course (e.g. Asch, Milgram, Zimbardo, Adorno, Moscovici, Atkinson and Shiffrin, Baddeley and Hitch, Loftus and Palmer, Johnson and Scott, Yuille and Cutshall, Geiselman, Schaffer and Emerson, Lorenz, Harlow, Bowlby, Ainsworth, Van Ijzendoorn and Kroonenberg, Rutter, Hazan and Shaver, Beck, Ellis, Wiles, Wundt, Pavlov, Skinner, Bandura, Freud, Maslow, Rogers, Maguire, Sperry, Siffre, Gottesman, Bateson, Meehl, Buss, Walster, Kerckhoff and Davis, Thibaut and Kelley, Rusbult, Duck, McCutcheon, Dollard, Tinbergen, Wilson and Daly, Irwin and Cressey, Sykes) is a genuine, correctly attributed piece of psychology, checked against real psychological knowledge and AQA's own published subject content when this course was built -- present it as real research, never as illustrative fiction, and correct the learner explicitly if they misattribute a study or misstate a finding. Research Methods (4.2.3, at least 25% of assessment across all three papers, plus a minimum 10% mathematical-skills requirement) is examined throughout, not only in its own stages -- sampling, experimental design and inferential statistics can appear applied to any topic area. Every numerical worked example (mean, median, mode, range, standard deviation, the sign test) was computed and verified with Python when this course was built, and the learner should always show full working for a calculation, since AQA credits correct method as well as a correct final answer. Extended-writing ('Discuss...'/'Evaluate...') questions are marked holistically against AQA's levels-of-response scheme (see `rubric.json`), not by a checklist of individual points -- teach the learner to build a structured AO1 (accurate knowledge) + AO3 (effective discussion/evaluation), and where the question is scenario-based, AO2 (explicit application to the scenario) response, not a list of disconnected facts.

#### REL3 Theories of romantic relationships: social exchange theory, equity theory, Rusbult's investment model
**Social exchange theory (Thibaut and Kelley, 1959)** proposes that romantic relationships are governed by an economic-style cost-benefit analysis: partners seek to maximise rewards (e.g. companionship, sex, support) and minimise costs (e.g. effort, compromise, opportunities given up), and relationship satisfaction/commitment depends on the resulting profit (rewards minus costs). Judgements of a relationship's worth are made relative to two comparison standards: the **comparison level (CL)** -- what a person believes they reasonably deserve/expect from a relationship, based on their previous relationship experiences and general expectations, with a relationship judged satisfactory if it meets or exceeds this level; and the **comparison level for alternatives (CLalt)** -- whether a person believes they could get a better outcome (more profit) in an alternative relationship (or by being single) than in their current one, with a person more likely to leave a relationship, even one that is itself profitable, if a sufficiently more attractive alternative is perceived to be realistically available. Limitation: this theory has been criticised for being difficult to test precisely (rewards, costs and comparison levels are highly subjective, and hard to operationalise/measure objectively and consistently), and for arguably being self-centred/individualistic in its assumptions (real relationships, especially in some collectivist cultures, may be experienced and understood less in individually self-interested terms than social exchange theory assumes).

**Equity theory (Walster et al., 1978)** extends social exchange theory, arguing that what matters most for relationship satisfaction is not simply maximising one's own individual profit, but the perceived **fairness/equity** of the ratio of rewards to costs (or, more precisely, of rewards relative to each partner's inputs/contributions) between both partners -- a relationship where one partner consistently receives disproportionately more (**over-benefiting**) or less (**under-benefiting**) than the other, relative to what each puts in, is predicted to cause dissatisfaction for both partners (even, counter-intuitively, for the over-benefiting partner, who research suggests may experience guilt or discomfort), not only for the partner who is under-benefiting. Equity theory further predicts that partners who perceive significant inequity will be motivated to try to restore equity, either by actually changing the real balance of rewards/inputs in the relationship, or by psychologically re-evaluating/distorting their perception of the relationship's inputs and outcomes to make it feel more equitable than it strictly is. Research support: studies of long-term couples have generally found that perceived equity (rather than simple, absolute reward maximisation) correlates more strongly with relationship satisfaction, supporting equity theory's refinement over a pure social exchange account.

**Rusbult's investment model (1980)** further develops social exchange theory by adding a third key factor to satisfaction and comparison with alternatives: **investment** -- the resources a partner has put into the relationship that would be lost, or significantly diminished in value, if the relationship ended, distinguishing intrinsic investments (resources put directly into the relationship itself, e.g. time, emotional energy, self-disclosure shared) from extrinsic investments (resources originally external to the relationship that have become bound up with it, e.g. shared possessions, a joint mortgage, mutual friends, children). The model proposes that **commitment** to a relationship (the best overall predictor, in this model, of whether a relationship will actually persist, more so than satisfaction alone) is jointly determined by **satisfaction** (the level of rewards versus costs, similar to social exchange theory), **comparison with alternatives** (as in social exchange theory's CLalt), and **investment size** -- and, crucially, the model predicts that a person can remain highly committed to, and stay in, a relationship that is not, on its own, especially satisfying, if their investment in it is sufficiently large (since leaving would mean losing that accumulated investment), helping explain why people sometimes stay in relationships that outwardly appear unsatisfying. Evaluation: Rusbult's model has good research support (e.g. studies finding investment size is indeed a significant independent predictor of relationship commitment/persistence, beyond satisfaction and alternatives alone) and usefully helps explain relationship persistence that pure social exchange or equity theory alone struggles to fully account for; like the other economic theories of relationships, it is criticised for a largely individualistic, self-interested assumption about human relationships that may not fully reflect more collectivist cultural contexts, and for relying substantially on retrospective, correlational self-report data, from which causal conclusions about what actually drives commitment cannot be fully confirmed.
