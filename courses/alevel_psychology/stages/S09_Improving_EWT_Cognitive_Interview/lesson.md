## How to teach this
Ask: if a police interview simply asked a witness to describe an event backwards, from a different person's point of view, could that really help them remember more accurately -- or would it just confuse them? Every named study, theorist and theory in this course (e.g. Asch, Milgram, Zimbardo, Adorno, Moscovici, Atkinson and Shiffrin, Baddeley and Hitch, Loftus and Palmer, Johnson and Scott, Yuille and Cutshall, Geiselman, Schaffer and Emerson, Lorenz, Harlow, Bowlby, Ainsworth, Van Ijzendoorn and Kroonenberg, Rutter, Hazan and Shaver, Beck, Ellis, Wiles, Wundt, Pavlov, Skinner, Bandura, Freud, Maslow, Rogers, Maguire, Sperry, Siffre, Gottesman, Bateson, Meehl, Buss, Walster, Kerckhoff and Davis, Thibaut and Kelley, Rusbult, Duck, McCutcheon, Dollard, Tinbergen, Wilson and Daly, Irwin and Cressey, Sykes) is a genuine, correctly attributed piece of psychology, checked against real psychological knowledge and AQA's own published subject content when this course was built -- present it as real research, never as illustrative fiction, and correct the learner explicitly if they misattribute a study or misstate a finding. Research Methods (4.2.3, at least 25% of assessment across all three papers, plus a minimum 10% mathematical-skills requirement) is examined throughout, not only in its own stages -- sampling, experimental design and inferential statistics can appear applied to any topic area. Every numerical worked example (mean, median, mode, range, standard deviation, the sign test) was computed and verified with Python when this course was built, and the learner should always show full working for a calculation, since AQA credits correct method as well as a correct final answer. Extended-writing ('Discuss...'/'Evaluate...') questions are marked holistically against AQA's levels-of-response scheme (see `rubric.json`), not by a checklist of individual points -- teach the learner to build a structured AO1 (accurate knowledge) + AO3 (effective discussion/evaluation), and where the question is scenario-based, AO2 (explicit application to the scenario) response, not a list of disconnected facts.

#### MEM7 Improving the accuracy of eyewitness testimony: the cognitive interview
**The cognitive interview.** Developed by **Fisher and Geiselman (1992)**, the cognitive interview (CI) is a set of techniques for interviewing witnesses, based on psychological research into memory (e.g. the encoding specificity principle, see MEM4), designed to maximise the amount of accurate information recalled without using leading questions. It has four main techniques. **Report everything**: the witness is encouraged to report every single detail of the event, even those that seem trivial, irrelevant or that the witness is not confident about, since a seemingly unimportant detail might act as a trigger/cue for other important information, and small details can later be cross-checked against other evidence. **Reinstate the context**: the witness mentally recreates the context of the original incident (both the environment -- sights, sounds, smells -- and their own emotional state at the time), based on the encoding specificity principle that recall is better when retrieval cues match those present at encoding (context-dependent and state-dependent effects, see MEM4). **Reverse the order**: the witness recalls the events in a different chronological order from the original sequence (e.g. from the end backwards to the start), to disrupt reliance on expectations/schemas about how the event 'should have' unfolded, and to prevent dishonest accounts (which are harder to reverse convincingly, since a fabricated account typically follows a script). **Change perspective**: the witness recalls the incident from other people's perspectives (e.g. imagining how another witness or the perpetrator experienced it), again to disrupt schema-driven expectations about a stereotypical sequence of events and to encourage recall of additional detail. A later development, the **enhanced cognitive interview**, added attention to the social dynamics of the interview itself (e.g. reducing witness anxiety, minimising distractions, and careful management of the interviewer's own language/pace to avoid unintentionally leading the witness).
