#### REL4 Duck's phase model of relationship breakdown
**Duck's phase model of relationship breakdown (1988, 2007)** proposes relationship dissolution unfolds through four distinct phases, each triggered by reaching a particular threshold of dissatisfaction. The **intra-psychic phase** begins when a partner privately reaches a threshold of 'I can't stand this any more', reflecting silently and privately on their dissatisfaction with the relationship and the partner's behaviour, without yet voicing these concerns to the partner directly, weighing up the pros and cons of staying versus the possibility of leaving. The **dyadic phase** begins once the threshold 'I would be justified in withdrawing' is reached, and the dissatisfied partner now confronts their partner directly, discussing their feelings and the state of the relationship together -- this phase can lead either to attempts at repair/reconciliation, or to a mutual recognition that the relationship cannot be saved. The **social phase** begins once the threshold 'I mean it' is reached, and the breakdown is made public, involving wider social networks (friends, family), who may be asked to provide support, take sides, act as mediators, or help facilitate the breakup, and gossip/rumour about the relationship's end may begin to spread. The **grave-dressing phase** begins once the threshold 'It's now inevitable' is reached, and involves the ex-partners constructing and communicating their own public account/narrative of why the relationship ended, in a way that protects their own self-image/social reputation (e.g. each partner tending to present a version of events that assigns relatively little blame to themselves), and preparing to move on. Duck later added a fifth, **resurrection phase**, describing how ex-partners use the lessons/experience of the ended relationship to inform and shape their approach to future relationships. Evaluation: the model usefully describes breakdown as a gradual, staged process with distinct psychological turning points, rather than a single event, and has real-world application (e.g. informing relationship counselling about which stage a couple may be at, and what kind of intervention -- e.g. focused on the dyadic phase -- might still help); it is criticised, however, for being based substantially on retrospective accounts of relationship breakdown (relying on participants' memory and self-serving reconstruction of events, which may not accurately reflect what happened in real time), and for not fully explaining why some relationships that reach a given threshold do sometimes recover, while others at an apparently similar point do not.

#### REL5 Virtual relationships and parasocial relationships
**Virtual relationships.** Relationships conducted substantially or entirely through electronic/computer-mediated communication (CMC) raise the question of how self-disclosure and attraction work when reduced non-verbal cues (e.g. facial expression, tone of voice) are available. The **reduced cues theory** argues that CMC relationships lack many of the non-verbal cues present in face-to-face interaction, which reduces people's sense of individual identity and can lead to more blunt, deindividuated, disinhibited communication, making virtual relationships generally less personal and more difficult to develop deep intimacy within, compared with face-to-face relationships. In contrast, the **hyperpersonal model** argues that virtual relationships can actually become more intense and more intimate, more quickly, than equivalent face-to-face relationships, partly because CMC allows people more time to carefully construct and selectively self-present a more attractive, idealised version of themselves (**selective self-presentation**), and because the receiver, lacking full information, may fill in the gaps with an idealised, positive impression of the sender -- this can create a rapid, intense, reciprocal cycle of self-disclosure. Related to this is **absence of gating** in virtual relationships: 'gates' are obstacles/barriers to relationship formation that exist prominently in face-to-face interaction (e.g. physical unattractiveness, physical distance, social anxiety/shyness) but that can be largely absent, hidden, or far less relevant online, at least in an initial stage of virtual interaction, potentially allowing relationships to form and develop based more purely on similarity of interests/attitudes and effective self-disclosure than they might face-to-face, before physical/social 'gates' become relevant later, if and when the relationship moves toward face-to-face contact.

**Parasocial relationships.** A one-sided relationship in which one person (a 'fan') invests significant emotional energy, interest and time into another person who is typically a celebrity/media figure entirely unaware of the fan's existence (there is no reciprocal, two-way relationship at all). Research (using **McCutcheon's Celebrity Attitude Scale**) has identified levels of parasocial involvement, described by the **absorption-addiction model**: a mild, common **entertainment-social** level (following a celebrity's activities as a source of enjoyable social conversation with others); a more intense **intense-personal** level (more personal, obsessive involvement, e.g. seeing the celebrity as a 'soulmate' or intimate friend, absorbing much more time and emotional investment); and a **borderline-pathological** level (extreme, potentially uncontrollable behaviours, e.g. willingness to engage in illegal or highly inappropriate acts on the celebrity's behalf), thought to progressively increase in the degree of psychological dysfunction involved. Parasocial relationships have also been explained through **attachment theory (Maltby et al.)**, with some research finding people with an insecure attachment type (particularly insecure-resistant) are more likely to form intense parasocial relationships, potentially as a way of substituting for, or compensating for, difficulties in real-life relationships. Evaluation: parasocial relationships have real-world relevance given the modern prominence of celebrity and social media culture (relevant to understanding, e.g., excessive social-media following behaviour), but much of the underlying research is correlational, and self-report measures of celebrity attitudes may be affected by social desirability bias (participants under-reporting the true intensity of their own parasocial involvement).
