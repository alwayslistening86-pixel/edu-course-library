## How to teach this
Start with: 'How can a phone signal, sunlight and an X-ray all be the same kind of thing?' Build the spectrum from the answers.

#### 6.6.1.1 Transverse and longitudinal waves
Waves transfer energy and information without transferring matter. In transverse waves the oscillations are perpendicular to the direction of energy transfer (ripples on water, light and all electromagnetic waves, S-waves). In longitudinal waves the oscillations are parallel to the direction of energy transfer, forming compressions and rarefactions (sound, ultrasound, P-waves).

#### 6.6.1.2 Properties of waves
Amplitude is the maximum displacement from the rest position; wavelength is the distance from one point to the same point on the next wave; frequency is the number of waves passing a point per second (Hz); period T = 1/f. Wave speed = frequency x wavelength (v = f lambda). A wave of frequency 50 Hz and wavelength 6.0 m travels at 300 m/s. Wave speed is set by the medium.

#### 6.6.2.1 Types of electromagnetic waves
Electromagnetic (EM) waves are transverse waves that transfer energy from a source to an absorber, and all travel at the same speed in a vacuum or air, 3 x 10 to the power 8 m/s. The spectrum in order of increasing frequency (decreasing wavelength): radio waves, microwaves, infrared, visible light, ultraviolet, X-rays, gamma rays. The visible spectrum runs from red (longest wavelength) to violet (shortest).

#### 6.6.2.2 Properties of electromagnetic waves 1 (Higher tier)
Different substances may absorb, transmit, refract or reflect EM waves in ways that depend on wavelength. Radio waves can be produced by oscillations in electrical circuits, and when absorbed by a conductor they create an alternating current with the same frequency as the wave, which is how radio receivers work. Refraction of EM waves at boundaries between materials follows from a change in speed.

#### 6.6.2.3 Properties of electromagnetic waves 2 (Higher tier)
Changes in atoms and nuclei can generate EM waves over a range of frequencies: electrons changing energy levels emit UV or visible light, and changes in the nucleus emit gamma rays. Ultraviolet, X-rays and gamma rays are ionising radiation that can cause mutations and cancer; UV also causes sunburn and skin cancer. Radiation dose in sieverts measures the risk. Higher frequency means more harmful.

#### 6.6.2.4 Uses and applications of electromagnetic waves (Higher tier)
Radio waves: broadcasting and communications; microwaves: satellite communications and cooking; infrared: heating, thermal imaging and remote controls; visible light: fibre-optic communication and photography; ultraviolet: security marking and fluorescent lamps; X-rays: medical imaging of bones; gamma rays: sterilising equipment and treating cancer. Each use depends on how the wave is absorbed, transmitted or reflected.

#### P-RP7 Required practical: wave properties
Make observations to identify suitable apparatus to measure the frequency, wavelength and speed of waves in a ripple tank and in a solid (for example a string or rod). In a ripple tank, use a strobe light or a video to measure frequency, and a ruler on a projected image to find wavelength; speed = f x wavelength. Use a long line of waves to reduce the percentage error in wavelength.

#### P-RP8 Required practical: infrared radiation and surfaces
Investigate how the amount of infrared radiation absorbed or radiated by a surface depends on its nature. Use a Leslie cube (each face a different surface) filled with hot water and an infrared detector or thermometer at a fixed distance from each face, or two cans of different colours. Control the distance, water temperature and detector. The matt black surface emits most.
