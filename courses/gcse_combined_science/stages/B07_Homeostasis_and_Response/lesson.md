#### 4.5.1 Homeostasis
Homeostasis is the regulation of internal conditions to maintain optimum conditions for enzymes and cell function, for example temperature, blood glucose and water content. Control systems have receptors (detect stimuli), coordination centres (brain, spinal cord, pancreas) that process information, and effectors (muscles or glands) that produce responses. Nervous and hormonal systems both take part.

#### 4.5.2 Structure and function
The central nervous system is the brain and spinal cord. Sensory neurones carry impulses from receptors to the CNS, relay neurones connect inside it, and motor neurones carry impulses to effectors. A synapse is a gap where a chemical transmitter diffuses across to trigger the next impulse. In a reflex arc: stimulus, receptor, sensory neurone, relay neurone, motor neurone, effector, response. Reflexes are rapid and automatic, protecting the body.

#### 4.5.3.1 Human endocrine system
The endocrine system uses glands that secrete hormones directly into the blood, which carries them to target organs. Effects are slower but longer lasting than nerve impulses. The pituitary is the master gland (releases FSH, LH, ADH and other hormones), the thyroid makes thyroxine, the pancreas makes insulin and glucagon, adrenal glands make adrenaline, ovaries make oestrogen and progesterone, and testes make testosterone.

#### 4.5.3.2 Control of blood glucose concentration
The pancreas monitors blood glucose. If too high, it releases insulin, which makes liver and muscle cells convert glucose to glycogen. If too low, it releases glucagon, which converts glycogen back to glucose. In type 1 diabetes the pancreas does not make enough insulin: managed by diet and insulin injections. In type 2 diabetes the body cells no longer respond to insulin: linked to obesity, managed by diet, exercise and sometimes drugs.

#### 4.5.3.3 Hormones in human reproduction
At puberty oestrogen in females and testosterone in males cause secondary sexual characteristics. The menstrual cycle lasts about 28 days: FSH from the pituitary causes an egg to mature and stimulates oestrogen release; oestrogen repairs the uterus lining and stimulates LH; LH triggers ovulation; progesterone maintains the lining and inhibits FSH and LH.

#### 4.5.3.4 Contraception
Hormonal methods include oral contraceptives (oestrogen and progesterone inhibit FSH so no eggs mature), injections, implants and skin patches, which release progesterone for months. Non-hormonal methods include condoms and diaphragms (barrier methods), spermicidal agents, intrauterine devices, abstaining from intercourse at fertile times, and surgical sterilisation. Compare effectiveness, side effects, and protection from sexually transmitted infections.

#### 4.5.3.5 The use of hormones to treat infertility (Higher tier)
FSH and LH can be given to a woman to stimulate ovulation. In IVF, hormones stimulate eggs to mature; eggs are collected, fertilised with sperm in a lab, and developing embryos are placed into the uterus. IVF has a low success rate, can be emotionally and physically stressful, and can produce multiple births.

#### 4.5.3.6 Negative feedback (Higher tier)
Negative feedback keeps hormone levels within limits. Thyroxine from the thyroid stimulates the basal metabolic rate, important in growth and development; when blood thyroxine is too high, its release is reduced. Adrenaline from the adrenal glands prepares the body for fight or flight, raising heart rate and blood flow to muscles and releasing glucose.

#### B-RP6 Required practical: human reaction time
Plan and carry out an investigation into the effect of a factor on human reaction time, for example caffeine, practice, or distraction. Use the ruler-drop test: a partner drops a ruler without warning and the learner catches it; record the distance and convert to time. Control variables: same hand, same person, same ruler. Repeat several times to calculate a mean, identify anomalies, and note that human error in timing is why the ruler method is better than a stopwatch.
