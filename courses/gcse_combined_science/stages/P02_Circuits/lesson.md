## How to teach this
Start with: 'Why do all the lights in a house go out if one bulb in old fairy lights fails, but not in your kitchen?' Draw series and parallel.

#### 6.2.1.1 Standard circuit diagram symbols
Know the symbols for a cell, battery, switch (open and closed), lamp, fixed resistor, variable resistor, diode, LED, thermistor, LDR, ammeter (circle A), voltmeter (circle V) and fuse. An ammeter goes in series; a voltmeter goes in parallel across a component. Circuit diagrams use straight lines and no gaps.

#### 6.2.1.2 Electrical charge and current
Current is the rate of flow of charge: charge flow = current x time (Q = I t), with charge in coulombs, current in amperes (A) and time in seconds. A current of 3 A for 20 s carries 60 C. In a single closed loop, current has the same value at every point. Energy transferred = charge x potential difference (E = Q V).

#### 6.2.1.3 Current, resistance and potential difference
Potential difference (V) is the energy transferred per coulomb; resistance (ohms) is how hard it is for charge to flow. V = I R: a 12 V supply across a 4 ohm resistor gives 3 A. For a fixed resistor at constant temperature, current is directly proportional to potential difference (an ohmic conductor). Resistance of a wire increases with length and falls as it gets thicker.

#### 6.2.1.4 Resistors
Ohmic conductor: I-V graph is a straight line through the origin. Filament lamp: the line curves because resistance rises as the filament heats up. Diode: current flows in one direction only, with very high resistance in the reverse direction. Thermistor: resistance decreases as temperature increases (used in thermostats). LDR: resistance decreases as light intensity increases (used in automatic lights). In an LDR or thermistor circuit, changing conditions changes the current.

#### 6.2.2 Series and parallel circuits
Series: the same current flows through each component; the total potential difference is shared, so V total = V1 + V2; total resistance = R1 + R2. Parallel: the potential difference across each branch is the same as the supply; the total current is the sum of the branch currents; total resistance is less than the smallest branch resistance because there are more paths. Adding a resistor in series increases the total resistance; adding one in parallel decreases it.

#### P-RP2 Required practical: factors affecting resistance
Use circuit diagrams to set up and check circuits to investigate the resistance of a wire of different lengths (using a voltmeter and ammeter, and R = V / I), and of resistors in series and parallel. Keep current low to avoid heating the wire, take readings, plot resistance against length, and find that it is a straight line through the origin (resistance proportional to length).

#### P-RP3 Required practical: I-V characteristics
Investigate a fixed resistor, filament lamp and diode. Connect an ammeter in series and voltmeter across the component, with a variable resistor or variable power supply to change the current. Record both directions for a diode. Plot I against V with I on the y axis. Record repeat readings and use suitable ranges.
