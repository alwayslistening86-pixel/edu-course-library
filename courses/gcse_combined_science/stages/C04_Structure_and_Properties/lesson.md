# C04_Structure_and_Properties - Lesson: Structure, bonding and properties of substances

## Goal
The learner links the bonding and structure of ionic compounds, small molecules, polymers, giant covalent structures, metals and alloys, and the forms of carbon, to their properties and uses.

## How to teach this
Start with: 'Diamond and the graphite in a pencil are both pure carbon. Why are they so different?'

#### 5.2.2.3 Properties of ionic compounds
Ionic compounds have high melting and boiling points because a large amount of energy is needed to break the strong electrostatic forces between ions. They do not conduct when solid because ions are fixed in position, but do conduct when molten or dissolved in water because the ions are free to move and carry charge.

#### 5.2.2.4 Properties of small molecules
Small molecular substances have low melting and boiling points because weak forces between molecules (intermolecular forces) are overcome, not the strong covalent bonds within molecules. As molecules get bigger, the intermolecular forces increase, and so do boiling points. They do not conduct electricity because molecules have no overall charge.

#### 5.2.2.5 Polymers
Polymers have very large molecules with atoms linked by strong covalent bonds. The intermolecular forces between the molecules are relatively strong, so most polymers are solid at room temperature. This is why a plastic bag or bottle keeps its shape, unlike small hydrocarbon molecules such as methane that are gases.

#### 5.2.2.6 Giant covalent structures
Giant covalent substances (diamond, graphite, silicon dioxide) have all atoms linked by strong covalent bonds in giant networks. They have very high melting points because a lot of energy is needed to break these bonds.

#### 5.2.2.7 Properties of metals and alloys
Metals have layers of atoms that can slide over one another, so pure metals are malleable and soft. Alloys contain atoms of different sizes, which distort the layers and make it harder for them to slide, so alloys are harder than pure metals.

#### 5.2.2.8 Metals as conductors
Metals conduct electricity and heat because the delocalised electrons carry charge and energy through the structure. Metals have high melting and boiling points because of the strong metallic bonding. They are also usually shiny and dense.

#### 5.2.3.1 Diamond
In diamond each carbon atom forms four covalent bonds with other carbon atoms in a giant covalent structure. This makes diamond very hard with a very high melting point, and it does not conduct electricity because there are no free electrons. It is used in cutting tools and jewellery.

#### 5.2.3.2 Graphite
In graphite each carbon atom forms three covalent bonds, creating hexagonal layers with no covalent bonds between the layers; the layers can slide, so graphite is soft and slippery (a lubricant). One electron per carbon is delocalised, so graphite conducts electricity, which is why it is used for electrodes. It has a high melting point.

#### 5.2.3.3 Graphene and fullerenes
Graphene is a single layer of graphite, one atom thick, very strong and a good conductor, useful in electronics and composites. Fullerenes are hollow molecules of carbon atoms forming closed tubes or cages: Buckminsterfullerene (C60) is a sphere of 60 carbon atoms. Carbon nanotubes are cylindrical fullerenes with high tensile strength and electrical conductivity, used in nanotechnology, electronics and reinforcing materials such as tennis rackets.
