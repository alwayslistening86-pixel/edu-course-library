## How to teach this
Start with: 'Why does the sea take longer to warm than the sand on a beach?' Build heat capacity from particles.

#### 6.3.1.1 Density of materials
Density = mass / volume (kg/m3 or g/cm3). The particles in a solid are close together and have a higher density than a gas. To find density, measure mass on a balance and volume by a ruler (regular solid) or displacement of water (irregular solid). A block of 250 g and 100 cm3 has density 2.5 g/cm3.

#### 6.3.1.2 Changes of state
Melting, freezing, boiling, evaporation, condensation and sublimation are physical changes: the material recovers its original properties if the change is reversed. Mass is conserved because the number of particles does not change. Changes of state are different from chemical changes.

#### 6.3.2.1 Internal energy
The internal energy of a system is the total kinetic and potential energy of all its particles. Heating increases the internal energy by raising the temperature (particles move faster) or by causing a change of state (particles move further apart, changing potential energy). The kinetic energy of particles rises with temperature.

#### 6.3.2.2 Temperature changes in a system and specific heat capacity
The change in thermal energy = mass x specific heat capacity x temperature change. Higher specific heat capacity means more energy is needed per kilogram per degree, so the material warms more slowly. Heating 0.5 kg of water by 20 degrees Celsius needs 0.5 x 4200 x 20 = 42 000 J.

#### 6.3.2.3 Changes of state and specific latent heat
During a change of state the temperature stays constant while energy changes the potential energy of particles. Energy for a change of state = mass x specific latent heat (E = m L). Specific latent heat of fusion is for melting or freezing, of vaporisation for boiling or condensing. Melting 0.2 kg of ice (L = 334 000 J/kg) needs 66 800 J. On a heating curve the flat sections show changes of state.

#### 6.3.3.1 Particle motion in gases
Gas particles move randomly in all directions at high speed. The temperature of a gas is related to the average kinetic energy of the particles: higher temperature means faster particles. At absolute zero (-273 degrees Celsius, 0 K) the particles have the minimum internal energy. Kelvin temperature = Celsius + 273.

#### P-RP1 Required practical: specific heat capacity
Determine the specific heat capacity of a solid, such as an aluminium block, by heating with an electrical heater in an insulated block, using a joulemeter or a voltmeter and ammeter and stopwatch for the energy supplied, and a thermometer for temperature change. Calculate c = energy / (mass x temperature change). Insulate the block and take the highest temperature to reduce heat loss error.

#### P-RP4 Required practical: density of solids and liquids
For a regular solid, measure the dimensions with a ruler or micrometer and mass with a balance; volume = length x width x height. For an irregular solid, use a eureka can or a measuring cylinder with water and displacement. For a liquid, measure the mass of a measuring cylinder and then with a known volume of liquid, and subtract. Calculate density = mass / volume, and repeat.
