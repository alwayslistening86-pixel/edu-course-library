## How to teach this
Start with: 'Why are there so many kinds of finch, moth and bacteria, and how do we know?' Build the argument for natural selection.

#### 4.6.2.1 Variation
Differences between individuals of the same species result from genes inherited from parents, from the environment (for example diet, climate, accidents), or a combination of the two. Mutations happen continuously and occasionally lead to a new phenotype. Rarely, a mutation gives a new phenotype that is well suited to a changed environment.

#### 4.6.2.2 Evolution
Evolution is a change in the inherited characteristics of a population over time through natural selection, and may result in a new species. Individuals with characteristics that suit the environment are more likely to survive and breed successfully, and so pass those characteristics (alleles) to the next generation. Over many generations the proportion of individuals with these alleles increases. Explain in steps: variation, survival, reproduction, inheritance.

#### 4.6.2.3 Selective breeding
Humans choose parents with desired characteristics and breed them, selecting the best offspring over many generations. Examples: disease-resistant crops, animals producing more meat or milk, large or unusual flowers, gentle dogs. Selective breeding can cause inbreeding, giving a smaller gene pool, increased risk of inherited defects and susceptibility to disease.

#### 4.6.2.4 Genetic engineering
Genetic engineering changes an organism's genome by transferring a gene from another organism. Enzymes cut out the gene, a vector such as a plasmid or a virus carries it into the target cells, ideally at an early stage of development so all cells have it. Examples: crops resistant to insects or herbicides, bacteria making human insulin, and golden rice. Benefits include higher yields and improved nutrition; concerns include effects on wild flower populations, on insects, and on human health.

#### 4.6.3.1 Evidence for evolution
Evidence includes fossils, which show how organisms have changed over time, and the rapid evolution of antibiotic-resistant bacteria, which can be observed within a human lifetime. Both together show that species change as a result of natural selection.

#### 4.6.3.2 Fossils
Fossils form from the hard parts of organisms that do not decay (bones, shells), from parts replaced by minerals as they decay, or from traces such as footprints and burrows, preserved rock impressions of soft-bodied organisms. The fossil record is incomplete because many early life forms were soft-bodied and left few traces, and many fossils were destroyed by geological activity or have not yet been found.

#### 4.6.3.3 Extinction
Extinction may be caused by a catastrophic event such as a volcanic eruption or an asteroid impact, or by changes to the environment over geological time, new predators, new diseases, or new, more successful competitors. Species can become extinct when no individuals remain that can cope with a new environmental condition.

#### 4.6.3.4 Resistant bacteria
Bacteria can develop resistance to antibiotics by mutation. In the presence of an antibiotic, resistant bacteria survive and reproduce, so resistant strains such as MRSA become common. To reduce resistance, doctors should avoid prescribing antibiotics for viral infections, patients should complete courses, and agricultural use of antibiotics should be restricted. The development of new antibiotics is costly and slow, and cannot keep up.

#### 4.6.4 Classification of living organisms
Linnaeus classified organisms into kingdom, phylum, class, order, family, genus and species, based on structure and characteristics, and named organisms with a binomial system (genus and species, for example Homo sapiens). With microscopes and biochemical evidence, Carl Woese proposed the three-domain system: archaea, bacteria and eukaryota. Evolutionary trees show how related organisms are, using evidence from both living species and fossils.
