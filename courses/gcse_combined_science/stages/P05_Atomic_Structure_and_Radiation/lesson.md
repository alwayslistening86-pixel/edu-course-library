#### 6.4.1.1 The structure of an atom
Atoms have a radius of about 1 x 10 to the power -10 m. They have a positively charged nucleus, containing protons and neutrons, with a radius of about 1 x 10 to the power -15 m, surrounded by negatively charged electrons in energy levels (shells). Electrons can move to a higher energy level by absorbing electromagnetic radiation and fall to a lower one by emitting it. The number of electrons equals the number of protons, so atoms are neutral. An atom that loses or gains electrons becomes an ion.

#### 6.4.1.2 Mass number, atomic number and isotopes
Atomic number is the number of protons; mass number is the number of protons plus neutrons. Isotopes are atoms of the same element with the same protons but different numbers of neutrons: carbon-12 has 6 neutrons and carbon-14 has 8. Isotopes have the same chemical properties but some, such as carbon-14, are unstable and radioactive. Nuclei are written as, for example, carbon with mass number 14 above and atomic number 6 below.

#### 6.4.1.3 The development of the model of the atom
Dalton pictured solid spheres. Thomson discovered the electron and proposed the plum pudding model. Rutherford's alpha scattering experiment (Geiger and Marsden) found that most alpha particles passed straight through but a few were deflected through large angles, so the positive charge and most mass must be concentrated in a tiny nucleus. Bohr adapted the model, with electrons in fixed orbits (energy levels), supported by evidence on emitted light. Later Chadwick's experiments provided evidence for the neutron.

#### 6.4.2.1 Radioactive decay and nuclear radiation
Radioactive decay is the random emission of radiation from unstable nuclei. Alpha particles are helium nuclei (2 protons, 2 neutrons): strongly ionising, stopped by paper or a few centimetres of air. Beta particles are fast electrons from the nucleus (a neutron turns into a proton): moderate ionisation, stopped by thin aluminium. Gamma rays are electromagnetic waves: weakly ionising but very penetrating, reduced by thick lead or concrete. Neutrons can also be emitted. Activity is measured in becquerels (Bq), and detected with a Geiger-Muller tube.

#### 6.4.2.2 Nuclear equations
In equations, the total mass number and total atomic number must balance. Alpha decay: the nucleus loses 2 protons and 2 neutrons, so mass number falls by 4 and atomic number by 2: uranium-238 (92) gives thorium-234 (90) + alpha (4, 2). Beta decay: the mass number is unchanged and the atomic number rises by 1: carbon-14 (6) gives nitrogen-14 (7) + beta (0, -1). Gamma emission changes neither number.

#### 6.4.2.3 Half-lives and the random nature of radioactive decay
Radioactive decay is random: we cannot predict when a particular nucleus will decay, but for a large number the rate is predictable. The half-life is the time for the number of nuclei of an isotope in a sample to halve, or for the count rate to fall to half its initial value. A source with a count rate of 800 counts per minute and a half-life of 3 hours has 400 after 3 h, 200 after 6 h, 100 after 9 h. Higher tier: the net decline is the ratio of the final to initial activity, after n half-lives (1/2 to the power n).

#### 6.4.2.4 Radioactive contamination
Contamination is the unwanted presence of materials containing radioactive atoms on other materials; the hazard depends on the radiation type. Irradiation is exposure to radiation from a source without the source touching, and does not make the object radioactive. Contamination continues to irradiate until the source decays, so it is more dangerous if ingested. Precautions include gloves, tongs, protective clothing, limiting exposure time and shielding.
