## How to teach this
Start with: 'When a candle burns it seems to lose mass. Where did it go?' Lead to conservation of mass.

#### 5.3.1.1 Conservation of mass and balanced chemical equations
No atoms are lost or made in a chemical reaction, so the mass of products equals the mass of reactants, and a symbol equation must be balanced: the same number of each type of atom on each side. Balance by changing the large numbers in front of formulae, never the small subscripts: Mg + O2 gives 2MgO after writing 2Mg + O2 gives 2MgO.

#### 5.3.1.2 Relative formula mass
The relative formula mass (Mr) of a compound is the sum of the relative atomic masses of the atoms in the formula. For CaCO3: 40 + 12 + (3 x 16) = 100. In a balanced equation, the sum of Mr of reactants equals the sum of Mr of products. The percentage by mass of an element in a compound = (Ar x number of atoms) / Mr x 100.

#### 5.3.1.3 Mass changes when a reactant or product is a gas
If a reaction seems to lose mass, a gas has escaped, such as carbon dioxide from thermal decomposition of a carbonate. If mass appears to increase, a gas such as oxygen has joined the solid, such as when magnesium burns in air. In a sealed container the total mass remains constant.

#### 5.3.1.4 Chemical measurements
Repeat measurements to see how spread they are. The mean of repeats is the best estimate; the range shows the uncertainty, and half the range is used as the uncertainty of the mean. Readings give a range within which the true value probably lies. Use the resolution of the apparatus to judge how many significant figures to record.

#### 5.3.2.1 Moles (Higher tier)
The amount of substance is measured in moles. One mole contains the Avogadro constant of particles, 6.02 x 10 to the power 23. The mass of one mole in grams equals the relative formula mass. Number of moles = mass in g divided by Mr: 4.4 g of CO2 (Mr 44) is 0.1 mol.

#### 5.3.2.2 Amounts of substances in equations (Higher tier)
The coefficients in a balanced equation give the ratio of moles. 2Mg + O2 gives 2MgO: 1 mole Mg reacts with 0.5 mole O2 to give 1 mole MgO. To find a mass: mass of A to moles of A, use the ratio to find moles of B, then convert to mass of B. Example: 12 g of magnesium (0.5 mol) gives 0.5 mol MgO = 20 g.

#### 5.3.2.3 Using moles to balance equations (Higher tier)
Convert the given masses to moles and divide by the smallest number to find the simplest ratio, which gives the coefficients. For example, 4.8 g of Mg (0.2 mol) reacting fully with 3.2 g of O2 (0.1 mol) shows Mg : O2 = 2 : 1, so the equation is 2Mg + O2 gives 2MgO.

#### 5.3.2.4 Limiting reactants (Higher tier)
In many reactions one reactant is in excess to ensure the other is used up completely; the limiting reactant is the one that is used up first and determines the amount of product. Find moles of each reactant, divide by the coefficient, and the smaller value shows the limiting reactant.

#### 5.3.2.5 Concentration of solutions
Concentration = mass of solute (g) divided by volume of solution (dm3). Convert cm3 to dm3 by dividing by 1000. 5 g in 250 cm3 (0.25 dm3) is 20 g/dm3. A more concentrated solution has more solute in the same volume.
