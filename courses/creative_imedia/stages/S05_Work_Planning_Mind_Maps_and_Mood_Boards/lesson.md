# S05_Work_Planning_Mind_Maps_and_Mood_Boards - Lesson: Work planning, mind maps and mood boards

## Goal
The learner explains the components and advantages of a workplan and the purpose, conventions, users and effectiveness of mind maps and mood boards.

## Syllabus items taught here
- R093-3.1 - Work planning and workplans: know the components of a workplan and explain how it manages time, tasks and resources
- R093-3.2 - Mind maps and mood boards for ideas generation: know the purpose, conventions, tools, users and effectiveness of mind maps and mood boards

## How to teach this
Ask: 'You have three weeks to produce a short film with a team of five. What would go wrong if you just started filming, and what would you write down first?' Then show a simple workplan and a mood board side by side and ask what each helps you decide.

#### R093-3.1 Work planning and workplans
A workplan sets out how a project will be completed. Its components are phases (pre-production, production, post-production), tasks and activities within each phase, workflow (the order tasks must happen in, for example scripts before filming), timescales, milestones (key points such as script approved or rough cut complete), contingencies (backup plans such as a spare location or extra time for problems) and resources: hardware, people and software. The purpose is to organise work so a deadline is met and the budget controlled. Advantages: everyone knows what to do and when; problems and delays are spotted early; resources are not double-booked; the client can see progress. For individuals a workplan manages their own time; for large teams it coordinates many people so that dependent tasks are not held up. Exam habit: link each component to the scenario ('a contingency such as booking a second recording day protects the deadline if it rains').

#### R093-3.2 Mind maps and mood boards for ideas generation
Both documents support ideas generation. A mind map starts with a central topic (the brief or theme) and branches into linked ideas, sub-ideas and details; it can be hand drawn or digital, and is used by the creator to explore content, options and connections quickly. A mood board is a collage of images, colours, textures, typography and other examples that communicate the look, feel and style of a product; it can be physical (board and cut-outs) or digital (slides or an app), and is often shared with the client and team to agree a visual direction. The specification asks learners to know the purpose, components and conventions, the hardware and software used, the users, when each is appropriate and what makes each effective. A mind map is effective when branches are clear, colour-coded and cover all the brief; a mood board is effective when everything on it supports the same intended mood and audience. Creating them from scratch is not examined but recognising and improving them is.

## Explicitly not here
Storyboards, wireframes, visualisation diagrams, asset logs, flow charts and scripts (S06).
