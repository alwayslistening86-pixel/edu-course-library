# BT_S06_Leadership_Management_and_Teams - Lesson: Leadership, management, teams and motivation

## Goal
The learner distinguishes leadership, management and supervision, explains individual and group behaviour at work, how teams form and develop, theories of motivation, and how learning, training and appraisal support performance.

## Syllabus items taught here
- BT.D1 - Leadership, management and supervision
- BT.D2 - Individual and group behaviour in business organisations
- BT.D3 - Team formation, development and management
- BT.D4 - Motivating individuals and teams
- BT.D5 - Learning and training at work
- BT.D6 - Review and appraisal of individual performance

## How to teach this
Ask: 'Is every good manager automatically a good leader? Is every good leader automatically a good manager?' Use the answers to separate the two concepts. Have the learner predict the result of every example before running it, and run code for real whenever they can.

#### BT.D1 Leadership, management and supervision
**Leadership** is about setting direction, inspiring and influencing people towards a vision, often associated with change. **Management** is about planning, organising, coordinating and controlling resources to achieve defined objectives efficiently, often associated with stability and existing systems. **Supervision** is first-line, day-to-day oversight of a team's routine work. **Leadership styles**: autocratic (the leader decides alone, fast but can demotivate and loses staff input), democratic/participative (the team is consulted, improving buy-in and quality of decisions but slower), and laissez-faire (minimal direction, staff largely self-manage - can work well with highly skilled, self-motivated teams but risks drift without it). The best style depends on the situation - the task, the team's competence and the time available (situational/contingency leadership) - rather than one style always being correct.

#### BT.D2 Individual and group behaviour in business organisations
**Individual behaviour at work** is shaped by personality, attitudes, perception (how people interpret situations, which can differ from the objective facts) and learning. **Group behaviour**: groups can be formal (created by the organisation, e.g. a department) or informal (arising socially). Groups develop **norms** (unwritten expected behaviours) and can exhibit **groupthink** (a desire for harmony overriding realistic appraisal of alternatives, leading to poor group decisions) or **social loafing** (individuals exerting less effort in a group than they would alone, because individual contribution is harder to observe). Understanding these dynamics helps a manager get the best, rather than the worst, from group working.

#### BT.D3 Team formation, development and management
**Team formation and development**: Tuckman's model describes four (later five) stages - **forming** (members are polite, unsure of roles, little real progress), **storming** (conflict emerges over roles, approach and power), **norming** (the team agrees ways of working and roles settle), **performing** (the team works effectively towards its goal), and **adjourning/mourning** (the team disbands once its task is complete). Teams do not always move through the stages smoothly or in strict sequence, and new members or major change can push a team back to an earlier stage. Belbin's team roles describe the different behavioural contributions people tend to make in a team (e.g. the ideas-generator, the completer-finisher, the coordinator); a well-balanced mix of roles, not just of technical skills, tends to make a stronger team.

#### BT.D4 Motivating individuals and teams
**Motivating individuals and teams.** Content theories explain *what* motivates: **Maslow's hierarchy of needs** (physiological, safety, social/belonging, esteem, self-actualisation - lower needs generally must be satisfied before higher ones motivate strongly); **Herzberg's two-factor theory** distinguishes **hygiene factors** (pay, working conditions, company policy - their absence causes dissatisfaction, but their presence alone doesn't create strong motivation) from **motivators** (achievement, recognition, responsibility, the work itself - these genuinely drive motivation and job satisfaction). Process theories explain *how* motivation happens: e.g. expectancy theory (people are motivated when they believe effort will lead to performance, and performance to a valued reward). In practice, pay alone (a hygiene factor) rarely sustains high motivation; job design, recognition and development opportunities matter too.

#### BT.D5 Learning and training at work
**Learning and training at work**: training improves current job performance (e.g. induction training, on-the-job coaching, technical skills courses); **development/education** builds broader, longer-term capability (e.g. management development, further qualifications). Methods include on-the-job training (learning while doing the actual job, cheap and relevant but variable in quality) and off-the-job training (courses, e-learning, away from normal duties - more consistent and can cover material unavailable on the job, but costs more and risks poor transfer back to the workplace). Kolb's learning cycle (concrete experience, reflective observation, abstract conceptualisation, active experimentation) describes how adults learn effectively from experience when they also reflect on and generalise from it.

#### BT.D6 Review and appraisal of individual performance
**Review and appraisal of individual performance**: a formal, usually periodic process comparing an individual's actual performance against agreed objectives, to recognise good performance, identify development needs, and (often) inform pay or promotion decisions. Good appraisal is two-way (the appraisee's own view is heard), based on evidence rather than recent-events bias, sets SMART (Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Relevant, Time-bound) objectives for the next period, and is followed up - an appraisal that happens once a year with no ongoing feedback in between tends to be far less effective than one supported by regular informal check-ins.
