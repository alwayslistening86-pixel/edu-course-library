# BT_S02_Micro_Social_Technological_Environmental_and_Competitive_Factors - Lesson: Microeconomic, social, technological, environmental and competitive factors

## Goal
The learner explains microeconomic influences on a business (demand, supply, competition structures), social/demographic trends, technological change, environmental/sustainability pressures, and competitive analysis.

## Syllabus items taught here
- BT.A5 - Microeconomic factors
- BT.A6 - Social and demographic factors
- BT.A7 - Technological factors
- BT.A8 - Environmental and sustainability factors
- BT.A9 - Competitive factors

## How to teach this
Ask why a business selling ice cream needs to think differently about 'the economy' (national interest rates) versus 'its market' (how many competitors sell ice cream nearby, and how loyal customers are). Have the learner predict the result of every example before running it, and run code for real whenever they can.

#### BT.A5 Microeconomic factors
**Microeconomic factors** concern a business's own market rather than the whole economy: **demand** (how much customers want to buy at a given price, affected by price, income, tastes, and the price of substitutes/complements) and **supply** (how much businesses are willing to sell at a given price); together they set the **market price**. **Market structure** ranges from **perfect competition** (many small firms, identical products, no pricing power) through **monopolistic competition** and **oligopoly** (a few dominant firms, e.g. supermarkets or airlines, interdependent pricing decisions) to **monopoly** (a single dominant supplier, significant pricing power, often regulated). A firm's own pricing and output decisions, costs and profitability are shaped by which structure it competes in.

#### BT.A6 Social and demographic factors
**Social and demographic factors**: changes in population size and structure (an ageing population increases demand for healthcare and pensions products, a growing working-age population increases labour supply), household composition, income distribution, education levels, and cultural attitudes and lifestyle trends (e.g. rising health-consciousness, remote working, changing attitudes to home ownership). These shape both the demand for products/services and the labour market a business recruits from.

#### BT.A7 Technological factors
**Technological factors**: innovation changes what businesses can produce, how efficiently they produce it, and how they reach customers - automation and robotics (reducing labour cost, raising output consistency), e-commerce and digital marketing (new sales channels, global reach), and data/analytics and artificial intelligence (better forecasting, personalised marketing, faster decision-making). Technological change can be a threat (disruption to an existing business model, e.g. streaming displacing physical media) or an opportunity (new products, new markets, lower costs), and the pace of change means businesses must monitor and adapt continually or risk obsolescence.

#### BT.A8 Environmental and sustainability factors
**Environmental and sustainability factors**: growing regulatory, investor and customer pressure for businesses to manage their environmental impact - carbon emissions and climate change, resource use and waste, pollution, and broader **sustainability** (meeting present needs without compromising future generations' ability to meet theirs, covering environmental, social and economic dimensions - the 'triple bottom line' of people, planet and profit). Businesses respond through environmental policies, sustainability reporting, cleaner production processes and product redesign, partly from genuine commitment and partly because regulators, investors and customers increasingly demand it.

#### BT.A9 Competitive factors
**Competitive factors**: analysed using tools such as **Porter's Five Forces** - the threat of new entrants (deterred by high barriers to entry, e.g. capital requirements, economies of scale, brand loyalty), the threat of substitute products, the bargaining power of suppliers, the bargaining power of buyers/customers, and the intensity of rivalry among existing competitors (price wars, advertising battles, innovation races). Together these forces determine how attractive (potentially profitable) an industry is, and inform a firm's competitive strategy.
