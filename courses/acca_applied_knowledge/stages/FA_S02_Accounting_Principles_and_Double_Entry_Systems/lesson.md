# FA_S02_Accounting_Principles_and_Double_Entry_Systems - Lesson: Accounting principles and double-entry systems

## Goal
The learner applies key accounting concepts, the qualitative characteristics of useful financial information, and the principles of double-entry bookkeeping including ledger accounts and journal entries.

## Syllabus items taught here
- FA.B1 - Key principles and concepts of accounting
- FA.B2 - Qualitative characteristics of useful financial information
- FA.C1 - Double-entry bookkeeping principles and maintenance of records
- FA.C2 - General ledger accounts and journal entries

## How to teach this
Ask: 'If a business buys a machine for £10,000 cash, has it got poorer?' Use the answer to introduce double entry - one asset simply becomes another. Have the learner predict the result of every example before running it, and run code for real whenever they can.

#### FA.B1 Key principles and concepts of accounting
**Key accounting principles and concepts**: the **business entity concept** (the business is accounted for separately from its owner(s), even for a sole trader with no separate legal existence); **going concern** (financial statements assume the business will continue operating for the foreseeable future, unless there is evidence otherwise); the **accruals (matching) concept** (income and expenses are recognised when earned/incurred, not when cash is received/paid - see FA_S06); **materiality** (information is material if omitting or misstating it could influence users' decisions; immaterial items need not be shown separately); **consistency** (the same accounting treatment is applied to similar items period after period, so results are comparable); **prudence** (a degree of caution when making judgements under uncertainty, so assets/income are not overstated and liabilities/expenses are not understated); and **substance over form** (transactions are accounted for according to their economic substance, not merely their legal form).

#### FA.B2 Qualitative characteristics of useful financial information
**Qualitative characteristics** of useful financial information (per the IASB's Conceptual Framework): the two **fundamental** characteristics are **relevance** (information capable of making a difference to users' decisions, having predictive and/or confirmatory value) and **faithful representation** (information that is complete, neutral and free from error, genuinely depicting what it purports to represent). Four **enhancing** characteristics support these: **comparability** (across time and between entities, supported by consistency), **verifiability** (different knowledgeable observers could reach consensus that the information is faithfully represented), **timeliness** (available to users in time to influence decisions), and **understandability** (clearly and concisely presented, for users with reasonable business/accounting knowledge).

#### FA.C1 Double-entry bookkeeping principles and maintenance of records
**Double-entry bookkeeping**: every transaction affects at least two accounts, recorded as one **debit (Dr)** and one equal **credit (Cr)** entry, so total debits always equal total credits. The rule for which side to use follows the accounting equation and account type: **debit** an increase in an asset or an expense, or a decrease in a liability, equity or income; **credit** an increase in a liability, equity or income, or a decrease in an asset or an expense. *Worked example*: a business buys inventory for £2,000 cash - Dr Inventory £2,000, Cr Cash £2,000 (one asset increases, another decreases; total debits £2,000 = total credits £2,000). A sale of goods for £3,500 cash (cost of the goods is a separate entry) - Dr Cash £3,500, Cr Sales revenue £3,500 (an asset increases, income increases).

#### FA.C2 General ledger accounts and journal entries
**General ledger accounts and journal entries**. The **general ledger** holds a separate T-account for every asset, liability, equity, income and expense item; postings from the day books (sales day book, purchases day book, cash book, journal) are summarised into it. A **journal entry** records a transaction as a debit and credit pair (or several, still balancing) before/instead of posting directly, often used for less routine transactions (e.g. correcting an error, recording depreciation) and always including a brief narrative explaining the entry. *Worked example*: a business takes out a £15,000 bank loan - journal: Dr Bank £15,000, Cr Loan payable £15,000, narrative 'being loan received from the bank'. The **trial balance** (FA_S08) is a listing of every ledger account's balance, used to check that total debits equal total credits before preparing the financial statements.
