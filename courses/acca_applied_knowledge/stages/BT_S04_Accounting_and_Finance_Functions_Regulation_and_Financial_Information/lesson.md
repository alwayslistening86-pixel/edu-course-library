# BT_S04_Accounting_and_Finance_Functions_Regulation_and_Financial_Information - Lesson: Accounting/finance functions, regulation and financial information

## Goal
The learner explains how accounting relates to other business functions, the main roles within an accounting/finance function, the regulatory environment (including financial crime), and the financial information a business produces and why.

## Syllabus items taught here
- BT.C1 - Relationship between accounting and other business functions
- BT.C2 - Accounting and finance functions within business organisations
- BT.C3 - Regulation and financial crime
- BT.C4 - Financial information provided by business

## How to teach this
Ask: 'Marketing wants to spend more on advertising; production wants a new machine; finance says no to both this quarter.' Why does finance often have the final say? Have the learner predict the result of every example before running it, and run code for real whenever they can.

#### BT.C1 Relationship between accounting and other business functions
**The accounting and finance function** supports every other function by providing information for decisions and control: it advises marketing on pricing and budget limits, tells production the cost implications of a proposed change, provides HR with payroll and headcount cost data, and reports to senior management on whether the whole organisation is meeting its financial targets. Because virtually every business decision has a financial consequence, accounting acts as a common language and a control point across the organisation, though it must balance its own priorities (accuracy, cost control, compliance) against the different priorities of the functions it supports.

#### BT.C2 Accounting and finance functions within business organisations
Within the accounting/finance function, **financial accounting** records transactions and prepares the statutory financial statements for external users (shareholders, tax authorities, lenders) under applicable accounting standards; **management accounting** provides internal information for planning, control and decision-making (budgets, costing, variance analysis - see the MA syllabus); **treasury** manages cash, working capital, borrowing and financial risk (e.g. interest rate and currency risk); **internal audit** independently reviews the effectiveness of internal controls and risk management, reporting typically to those charged with governance (e.g. an audit committee) rather than to operational management, to preserve its objectivity; **tax** manages compliance with, and planning around, the organisation's tax obligations.

#### BT.C3 Regulation and financial crime
**Regulation** of business includes company law (setting out directors' duties and reporting requirements), accounting standards (ensuring financial statements are comparable and reliable), and sector-specific regulation (e.g. financial services). **Financial crime** includes fraud (dishonestly obtaining a financial advantage, or causing loss, through deception), money laundering (disguising the origin of criminal proceeds so they appear legitimate, typically through placement, layering and integration), bribery and corruption. Businesses and accountants have legal duties to prevent and report suspected financial crime (e.g. under money-laundering regulations, a duty to report suspicious activity to the relevant authority), and to maintain internal controls that make fraud harder to commit and easier to detect.

#### BT.C4 Financial information provided by business
Businesses produce **financial information** for a range of users and purposes: statutory **financial statements** (statement of profit or loss, statement of financial position, statement of cash flows) for external users, required by law and accounting standards; **management information** (budgets, variance reports, KPI dashboards) for internal decision-making, not governed by external standards and tailored to management's needs; and regulatory/statistical returns for government bodies (e.g. tax returns, national statistics filings). Good financial information should be relevant, reliable, timely, comparable and understandable to the intended user - the same underlying data may need to be presented very differently for a shareholder, a bank manager and an operations manager.
