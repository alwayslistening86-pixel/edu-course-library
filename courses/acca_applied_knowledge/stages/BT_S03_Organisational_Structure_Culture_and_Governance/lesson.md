# BT_S03_Organisational_Structure_Culture_and_Governance - Lesson: Organisational structure, culture, governance and sustainable practice

## Goal
The learner distinguishes formal and informal organisation, compares structural forms, explains organisational culture (Handy's typology) and how it changes, and outlines corporate governance and sustainable business practice.

## Syllabus items taught here
- BT.B1 - Formal and informal business organisation
- BT.B2 - Business organisational structure
- BT.B3 - Organisational culture
- BT.B4 - Governance in business organisations
- BT.B5 - Sustainable business practices

## How to teach this
Ask: 'Two companies have an identical organisation chart. Could working there still feel completely different?' Use the answer to separate structure from culture. Have the learner predict the result of every example before running it, and run code for real whenever they can.

#### BT.B1 Formal and informal business organisation
The **formal organisation** is the officially sanctioned structure: defined roles, reporting lines, rules and procedures, shown on an organisation chart. The **informal organisation** is the network of relationships, social groupings and unofficial communication ('the grapevine') that exists alongside it, arising naturally from personal relationships rather than job design. The informal organisation can speed up communication and build cooperation, but can also spread rumour, resist formal change, and create cliques that undermine the official structure; effective managers recognise and work with it rather than only relying on the formal structure.

#### BT.B2 Business organisational structure
**Organisational structure** describes how tasks are divided and coordinated. **Functional structure**: departments organised by specialism (production, marketing, finance, HR) - efficient use of specialist skills, but can create silos and slow cross-functional coordination. **Divisional structure**: organised around products, geographic regions, or customer groups, each division fairly self-contained - responsive to local needs, but can duplicate resources. **Matrix structure**: staff report to both a functional manager and a project/product manager - flexible and good for cross-disciplinary projects, but creates dual reporting lines and potential conflict over priorities. Structures are also described by **height** (tall, many layers of management, narrow spans of control, slower decisions) versus **flat** (few layers, wide spans of control, faster decisions but each manager oversees more people), and by degree of **centralisation** (decisions concentrated at the top, consistent but slower and less responsive locally) versus **decentralisation** (decision-making pushed to lower levels/local units, faster and more responsive, but risking inconsistency).

#### BT.B3 Organisational culture
**Organisational culture** is 'the way things are done around here' - shared values, beliefs, assumptions and norms of behaviour. **Handy's typology** identifies four cultures: **power culture** (control concentrated in one person or small group, e.g. a founder-led small firm - fast decisions, but very dependent on that individual); **role culture** (bureaucratic, defined by job descriptions, procedures and hierarchy, e.g. a large civil service body - stable and predictable, but can be slow to adapt); **task culture** (organised around getting a specific job done, flexible project teams, expertise valued over hierarchy, e.g. a consultancy - adaptable, but can lack clear central control); **person culture** (individuals' own expertise is central and the organisation exists to serve them, e.g. a barristers' chambers or a partnership of doctors - rare, minimal hierarchy). Culture is shaped by leadership, history, the industry, and national/regional culture, and changing an established culture is slow and difficult, usually requiring sustained change in leadership behaviour, structures, rewards and communication, not just a new mission statement.

#### BT.B4 Governance in business organisations
**Corporate governance** is the system by which companies are directed and controlled, balancing the interests of shareholders, management and other stakeholders. Core principles typically include: an effective, balanced **board of directors** (with independent non-executive directors providing scrutiny of executive management), clear division of responsibilities (e.g. separating the roles of Chair and Chief Executive to avoid excessive power in one person), robust **risk management and internal control**, transparent and accurate financial reporting and disclosure, fair remuneration linked to performance, and accountability to shareholders (e.g. through the AGM and voting rights). Good governance aims to protect shareholders and other stakeholders from mismanagement, fraud and excessive risk-taking, and to maintain trust and confidence in the business.

#### BT.B5 Sustainable business practices
**Sustainable business practices** integrate environmental, social and governance (ESG) considerations into strategy and operations, not just compliance: reducing environmental impact (energy, waste, emissions, supply chain sourcing), fair treatment of employees and communities (the social dimension), and sound governance and ethical conduct (linking back to BT.B4). Businesses increasingly publish **sustainability/ESG reports** alongside financial statements, reflecting pressure from investors, regulators, customers and employees, and because a poor sustainability record can damage reputation, raise the cost of capital, and create long-term operational and legal risk.
