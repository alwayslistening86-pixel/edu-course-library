# MA_S05_Budgeting_Fundamentals_and_Preparation - Lesson: Budgeting fundamentals and preparation

## Goal
The learner explains why organisations budget, prepares functional budgets (including a cash budget) from given data, and flexes a budget to a different activity level.

## Syllabus items taught here
- MA.D1 - Nature and purpose of budgeting
- MA.D2 - Statistical techniques in budgeting
- MA.D3 - Functional budgets

## How to teach this
Ask what could go wrong for a business that never plans its cash inflows and outflows month by month, even if it is profitable on paper. Have the learner predict the result of every example before running it, and run code for real whenever they can.

#### MA.D1 Nature and purpose of budgeting
**Budgets** are the quantified plan for a future period, used for **planning** (forcing managers to think ahead and coordinate departments), **control** (comparing actual results with budget to spot and act on variances), **communication and coordination**, **motivation** (a target to aim for) and **evaluation** (a benchmark for performance). The **budget committee** approves budgets and resolves conflicts between departments; the **master budget** (budgeted income statement, statement of financial position and cash budget) is the summary of all the functional budgets, and the **principal budget factor** (often sales demand, sometimes a scarce resource) is identified first because every other budget depends on it.

#### MA.D2 Statistical techniques in budgeting
**Budget preparation** works from the principal budget factor outward: e.g. the sales budget (units x price) drives the production budget (production = budgeted sales + desired closing inventory - opening inventory), which drives the materials usage and purchases budgets, the labour budget and the overhead budget; these feed into a **cash budget** (forecast receipts and payments by period, to spot cash shortfalls or surpluses in time to arrange finance or investment) and the budgeted income statement and statement of financial position. *Worked example, production budget*: budgeted sales 5,000 units; desired closing inventory 600 units; opening inventory 400 units. Production required = 5,000 + 600 - 400 = {5000 + 600 - 400} units.

#### MA.D3 Functional budgets
A **flexible budget** restates the original (fixed) budget at the actual level of activity achieved, so that like is compared with like: fixed costs stay the same in total, variable costs are scaled in proportion to activity, and semi-variable costs are split (e.g. by the high-low method) before flexing. Comparing actual results against a flexed budget (rather than the original static budget) isolates genuine efficiency/cost variances from the effect of simply producing a different volume. *Worked example*: original budget for 1,000 units: variable cost £8,000 (£8/unit), fixed cost £5,000. Actual output was 1,200 units. Flexed budget variable cost = £8 x 1,200 = £{8 * 1200:,}; flexed fixed cost stays £5,000; total flexed budget cost = £{8 * 1200 + 5000:,}.
