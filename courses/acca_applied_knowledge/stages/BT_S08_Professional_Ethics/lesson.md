# BT_S08_Professional_Ethics - Lesson: Professional ethics

## Goal
The learner explains the fundamental principles of ethical behaviour (IESBA/ACCA Code), the role of regulatory and professional bodies, corporate codes of ethics, and how to identify and resolve ethical conflicts and dilemmas.

## Syllabus items taught here
- BT.F1 - Fundamental principles of ethical behaviour
- BT.F2 - Role of regulatory and professional bodies
- BT.F3 - Corporate codes of ethics
- BT.F4 - Ethical conflicts and dilemmas

## How to teach this
Ask: 'Your manager asks you to backdate an invoice so this quarter's figures look better. No law is obviously broken yet. Is that still a problem?' Use the answer to introduce professional ethics beyond mere legality. Have the learner predict the result of every example before running it, and run code for real whenever they can.

#### BT.F1 Fundamental principles of ethical behaviour
**Fundamental principles of ethical behaviour**, as set out in ACCA's Code of Ethics and Conduct (based on the IESBA International Code of Ethics for Professional Accountants): **integrity** (being straightforward and honest in all professional and business relationships); **objectivity** (not allowing bias, conflict of interest, or undue influence of others to override professional or business judgements); **professional competence and due care** (maintaining professional knowledge and skill at the level required, and acting diligently, in accordance with applicable technical and professional standards); **confidentiality** (respecting the confidentiality of information acquired through professional and business relationships, not disclosing it without proper authority, and not using it for personal advantage); and **professional behaviour** (complying with relevant laws and regulations, and avoiding any conduct that discredits the profession). These are principles-based (requiring judgement in context) rather than a fixed rulebook covering every situation.

#### BT.F2 Role of regulatory and professional bodies
**Regulatory and professional bodies**: professional accountancy bodies (such as ACCA) set and enforce ethical and technical standards for their members, provide education and continuing professional development, and can discipline members (up to expulsion) for serious breaches. National/international regulators (e.g. audit and financial reporting regulators, securities regulators) set and enforce legal requirements around financial reporting, audit quality, and market conduct, and can impose fines or other sanctions on firms and individuals. Together, professional self-regulation and external statutory regulation aim to maintain public trust in financial information and in the accountancy profession.

#### BT.F3 Corporate codes of ethics
**Corporate codes of ethics** are an organisation's own statement of the values and standards of behaviour expected of its staff (and often its suppliers), going beyond the legal minimum - covering areas such as anti-bribery, conflicts of interest, use of company resources, whistleblowing, and treatment of colleagues and customers. A code is most effective when genuinely embedded (senior management visibly living by it, training, and real consequences for breaches) rather than treated as a document filed away and forgotten; a mismatch between a stated code and actual behaviour ('ethics washing') can damage trust more than having no code at all.

#### BT.F4 Ethical conflicts and dilemmas
**Ethical conflicts and dilemmas** arise where duties or interests point in different directions, e.g. loyalty to an employer versus the duty of objectivity and integrity, or confidentiality versus a public interest in disclosure. A structured approach: identify the relevant facts and the fundamental principles at stake; identify affected parties; consider established internal procedures (e.g. an ethics helpline, raising the matter with a more senior colleague or the compliance/ethics function); consider alternative courses of action and their consequences; and, if the matter cannot be resolved internally and involves a serious breach, consider seeking advice from the relevant professional body, obtaining independent legal advice, or as a last resort declining to be associated with the matter (which can include resigning). **Threats** to the fundamental principles are commonly categorised as self-interest, self-review, advocacy, familiarity and intimidation threats; identifying which threat applies helps identify an appropriate safeguard (e.g. involving a second, independent reviewer to address a self-review threat).
