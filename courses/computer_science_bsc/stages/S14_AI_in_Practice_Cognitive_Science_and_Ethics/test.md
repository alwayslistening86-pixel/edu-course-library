## Test items
1. Explain Searle's Chinese Room argument in your own words, and state precisely what it is intended to show about the relationship between passing the Turing Test and genuine understanding. [6 marks]
2. Explain why symbolic (rule-based) AI is generally more interpretable than a modern deep neural network, but tends to be more brittle. [4 marks]
3. A production fraud-detection model's accuracy quietly drops over several months with no code changes. Explain how data drift could cause this, and describe one monitoring practice that would catch it. [4 marks]
4. A public authority proposes using an AI system to assign every citizen a 'trustworthiness score' influencing their access to public services, based on their online behaviour and social connections. Discuss the AI-specific ethical and regulatory issues this raises, referring to explainability, accountability for emergent bias, and the EU AI Act's risk-based approach. [20 marks]
5. Which statements about the Turing Test and the Chinese Room argument are correct? Choose every correct option.
   A. The Turing Test judges a machine's external conversational behaviour, not its internal experience
   B. Searle's Chinese Room argument is intended to support strong AI
   C. The Chinese Room argument targets the claim that passing a behavioural test is sufficient evidence of genuine understanding
   D. Weak AI claims a program can usefully simulate intelligent behaviour without necessarily possessing genuine understanding

## Answer key (for the tutor only)
1. [6] B1 describes the setup: a person with no Chinese understanding, inside a room, follows purely syntactic (symbol-manipulation) rules to produce correct Chinese responses to Chinese input; B1 from outside, the room's output is indistinguishable from a genuine Chinese speaker's, i.e. it would pass a Chinese-language Turing Test; B1 yet the person inside has no understanding of Chinese meaning (semantics) at any point, only mechanical rule-following; B1 Searle argues a digital computer running any program is in exactly the same position -- pure syntactic symbol manipulation; B1 conclusion: passing the Turing Test (producing behaviourally correct output) is not sufficient evidence of genuine understanding/thought, since the Chinese Room does exactly that while (Searle argues) understanding nothing; B1 correctly names this as a challenge specifically to 'strong AI' (the claim that a suitably programmed computer genuinely thinks), not to the usefulness of AI systems in general.
2. [4] B1 interpretability: a symbolic system's behaviour follows explicit, human-written IF-THEN rules, so a specific decision can be traced to the exact rule(s) that fired; B1 a neural network's decision emerges from millions of learned numeric weights with no single human-readable 'reason', making it comparatively opaque; B1 brittleness: a symbolic system can only handle situations its rules explicitly anticipate, and fails (or behaves unpredictably) outside that coverage; B1 a learned model instead generalises statistically from patterns in training data, often handling novel-but-similar situations more gracefully than a purely rule-based system would.
3. [4] B1 the real-world pattern of transactions gradually changes over time (e.g. new payment methods, changed customer spending habits, or fraudsters adapting their behaviour), so incoming data increasingly differs statistically from the data the model was originally trained on; B1 the model's learned patterns, though unchanged in code, no longer match the current real-world input distribution, degrading its accuracy silently; B1 a valid monitoring practice, e.g. continuously tracking the statistical distribution of key input features (or the model's live prediction distribution/performance against a ground-truth label when it later becomes available) and alerting when it diverges significantly from the training-time baseline; B1 explains this enables timely retraining before the degradation becomes severe, rather than discovering it only once real-world harm has already occurred.

Credit specifically: an accountability problem specific to AI (biased/harmful scoring behaviour can emerge from data patterns without any single identifiable rule-author, making it harder to locate responsibility than for a traditional hand-coded rule); an explainability concern (a citizen denied services on the basis of an opaque score arguably cannot meaningfully understand or challenge the decision); the EU AI Act's risk-based framing (a social-scoring system of this kind, applied by a public authority, sits at exactly the 'unacceptable risk' end the Act singles out for prohibition, distinct from merely 'high-risk' applications that instead face stricter obligations short of an outright ban); a strong answer distinguishes the general data-protection concerns (which would also apply to a non-AI scoring system) from what is genuinely distinctive about this being an AI/ML system specifically.
