## Goal

Work every algorithm trace, calculation and code example with the learner step by step before revealing the next stage; have the learner predict a program's output before it is run. #### 14a Symbolic AI versus connectionist AI: expert systems and knowledge representation versus neural approaches
**Symbolic AI vs connectionist AI.** **Symbolic AI** (the dominant paradigm from the 1950s-1980s, sometimes called 'Good Old-Fashioned AI') represents knowledge explicitly as symbols and rules (e.g. an **expert system**: a knowledge base of IF-THEN rules written by human experts, plus an inference engine that applies them to reach conclusions -- e.g. MYCIN, an early medical-diagnosis expert system). Symbolic AI is interpretable (you can read the exact rule that fired) but brittle (struggles with situations not explicitly anticipated by its rules, and building/maintaining a large rule base by hand does not scale well). **Connectionist AI** (neural networks, the dominant paradigm today) learns patterns statistically from data rather than being explicitly programmed with rules, and can generalise to situations not seen in training -- but is comparatively opaque (a 'black box', as covered in S13) and requires large amounts of data/compute. Modern AI systems increasingly explore **neuro-symbolic** approaches, combining learned pattern recognition with explicit symbolic reasoning, aiming to get the interpretability/reasoning strengths of symbolic AI alongside connectionist AI's ability to learn from raw data.

#### 14b The Turing Test and philosophy of mind: strong versus weak AI, Searle's Chinese Room argument
**The Turing Test and philosophy of mind.** Alan Turing's 1950 'imitation game' proposes: if a human judge, conversing by text with both a machine and another human, cannot reliably tell which is which, the machine may be said to 'think' -- reframing the unanswerable question 'can machines think?' as the empirically testable question 'can a machine's behaviour be indistinguishable from a human's?'. This is a **behaviourist** criterion: it judges only external behaviour, not internal experience. **Strong AI** claims a sufficiently sophisticated computer program could genuinely think/understand/have a mind, not merely simulate the appearance of one; **weak AI** claims a program can usefully simulate thinking/intelligent behaviour without actually possessing genuine understanding or consciousness. **John Searle's Chinese Room argument** (1980) challenges strong AI: imagine a person who does not understand Chinese, locked in a room, given English rules for manipulating Chinese symbols to produce correct Chinese responses to Chinese questions passed in; from outside, the room appears to understand Chinese (it passes a Chinese-language Turing Test), yet the person inside understands nothing -- they are just following syntactic rules with no grasp of meaning (*semantics*). Searle argues a digital computer running any program is in exactly this position: manipulating symbols syntactically can never, by itself, produce genuine understanding, however convincing the external behaviour -- a direct challenge to the idea that passing the Turing Test is sufficient evidence of genuine thought.

#### 14c Deploying AI in practice: monitoring, data drift, the deployment-pipeline idea (MLOps basics)
**Deploying AI in practice (MLOps basics).** Training a model is only part of putting it to real use: a **deployment pipeline** takes a trained model through validation, packaging (e.g. as a callable API service), staged rollout (e.g. testing on a small fraction of real traffic before full release), and integration with the surrounding software system. Once deployed, **monitoring** tracks the model's real-world performance (not just its performance on the original test set) and its inputs over time, watching for **data drift** (the real-world input data's statistical distribution gradually diverges from the data the model was trained on, e.g. customer behaviour changing after an economic shift) and **concept drift** (the actual relationship between inputs and the correct output changes, e.g. what counts as 'spam' evolving as spammers adapt) -- either can silently degrade a model's accuracy over time without any change to the model's code, which is why production ML systems need ongoing monitoring and periodic retraining, not a one-off training-and-deploy event.

#### 14d Ethics and risk of AI: bias and fairness, explainability, safety concerns, an outline of AI-specific regulation
**Ethics and risk of AI (beyond the general computing-ethics frameworks of S12).** **Bias and fairness** (covered technically in S13) raise a distinctly *AI-specific* ethical concern: because a model's biased behaviour can emerge indirectly from patterns in data, without any single identifiable person having written an explicitly discriminatory rule, responsibility and accountability become harder to locate than in a traditional hand-coded system. **Explainability** is an ethical as well as technical concern: a person affected by an automated decision (e.g. loan refusal, job-application rejection) arguably has a right to a meaningful explanation, which a genuinely opaque model struggles to provide. **Safety/existential concerns**: discussions range from near-term (an AI system deployed in a safety-critical context, e.g. autonomous vehicles, making an error with physical consequences; job displacement from automation) to long-term/speculative (concerns, debated among researchers, about the difficulty of ensuring a highly capable future AI system's goals remain reliably aligned with human intent -- the 'alignment problem'). **Regulation**: the EU AI Act (adopted 2024) takes a risk-based approach, imposing stricter obligations (e.g. mandatory risk assessment, transparency, human oversight) on 'high-risk' AI applications (e.g. those used in employment, credit-scoring, law enforcement) than on low-risk ones, and prohibits certain uses outright (e.g. social scoring by public authorities); this represents one real, current regulatory response to exactly the risks named above, distinct from general data-protection law (GDPR), which was not written specifically with AI systems in mind.
