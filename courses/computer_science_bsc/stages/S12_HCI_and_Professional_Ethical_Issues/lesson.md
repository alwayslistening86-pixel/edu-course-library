# S12_HCI_and_Professional_Ethical_Issues - Lesson: Human-computer interaction and professional/ethical issues

## Goal
The learner applies Nielsen's usability heuristics and the user-centred design process, explains WCAG accessibility principles, identifies the key duties in the BCS Code of Conduct, the Computer Misuse Act 1990 and UK GDPR, and applies named ethical frameworks to a professional computing dilemma.

## Syllabus items taught here
- 12a - HCI principles: Nielsen's usability heuristics, the user-centred design process
- 12b - Accessibility and inclusive design: the WCAG principles, assistive technology
- 12c - Professional and legal frameworks: the BCS Code of Conduct, the Computer Misuse Act 1990, UK GDPR/Data Protection Act 2018
- 12d - Ethics of computing: consequentialist and deontological frameworks applied to a professional computing dilemma

## How to teach this
Ask the learner to name one app or website they find genuinely hard to use, and why -- then map their complaint onto one of Nielsen's heuristics. Work every algorithm trace, calculation and code example with the learner step by step before revealing the next stage; have the learner predict a program's output before it is run. This is honours-degree material: insist on precise terminology and full justification, not just a right answer. Every computed value, algorithm trace and program output in these files was produced by actually running Python when the course was built, never hand-typed.

#### 12a HCI principles: Nielsen's usability heuristics, the user-centred design process
**Human-computer interaction (HCI) principles.** Jakob Nielsen's 10 usability heuristics (a widely used practical checklist) include: *visibility of system status* (the system keeps users informed, e.g. a loading spinner); *match between system and the real world* (use familiar language/concepts, not internal jargon); *user control and freedom* (an easy 'undo'/exit from a mistaken action); *consistency and standards* (don't make users guess whether different words/actions mean the same thing); *error prevention* (design that prevents a problem occurring, better than a good error message after it occurs); *recognition rather than recall* (make options visible, don't force the user to remember information across screens); *flexibility and efficiency of use* (accelerators for expert users without penalising novices); *help users recognise, diagnose and recover from errors* (plain language, precisely indicate the problem, suggest a solution). The **user-centred design process** iterates: understand the context of use and user requirements -> produce design solutions -> evaluate against requirements (e.g. usability testing with real users) -> refine, repeating until the product meets the users' actual needs, rather than designing from the developer's own assumptions.

#### 12b Accessibility and inclusive design: the WCAG principles, assistive technology
**Accessibility and inclusive design.** The **Web Content Accessibility Guidelines (WCAG)** are organised around 4 principles (POUR): **Perceivable** (information must be presentable in ways users can perceive, e.g. text alternatives for images, captions for video, sufficient colour contrast); **Operable** (interface components must be operable by all users, e.g. full keyboard navigation for users who cannot use a mouse, no content that flashes in a way that could trigger seizures); **Understandable** (information and UI operation must be understandable, e.g. readable text, predictable navigation, help with input errors); **Robust** (content must work reliably with a wide range of user agents, including assistive technology such as screen readers, e.g. correct semantic HTML/ARIA roles so a screen reader can correctly announce a button as a button). **Assistive technology** (screen readers, switch access devices, voice control) depends on software being built to expose its structure and state correctly (e.g. proper HTML semantics, alt text, labelled form fields) -- accessibility failures are frequently failures of the underlying code, not just visual design.

#### 12c Professional and legal frameworks: the BCS Code of Conduct, the Computer Misuse Act 1990, UK GDPR/Data Protection Act 2018
**Professional and legal frameworks.** The **BCS Code of Conduct** (the Chartered Institute for IT's professional code) sets out duties in four areas: *the public interest* (having regard to public health, privacy, security and wellbeing, and to the legitimate rights of third parties); *professional competence and integrity* (only undertaking work within one's competence, keeping skills up to date, rejecting bribery); *duty to relevant authority* (client/employer, avoiding conflicts of interest, respecting confidentiality); *duty to the profession* (upholding the reputation of the profession, not misrepresenting others' work as one's own). The **Computer Misuse Act 1990** (UK) creates offences including unauthorised access to computer material, unauthorised access with intent to commit a further offence, and unauthorised modification of computer material (later extended to cover impairing operation, e.g. DoS attacks, and making/supplying tools for such offences) -- it is this Act that makes hacking and writing/distributing malware criminal offences in UK law, regardless of whether any data is actually stolen. **UK GDPR / the Data Protection Act 2018** require processing personal data lawfully, fairly and transparently, only for a specified purpose, with data minimisation (collect no more than necessary), accuracy, storage limitation (not kept longer than necessary), and appropriate security -- and give data subjects rights including access to their data, rectification, erasure ('right to be forgotten') and to object to certain automated decision-making.

#### 12d Ethics of computing: consequentialist and deontological frameworks applied to a professional computing dilemma
**Ethics of computing.** A **consequentialist** framework judges an action by its outcomes/consequences (the right action is the one producing the best overall outcome, e.g. utilitarianism: maximise overall wellbeing/minimise harm); applied to computing, this asks e.g. whether deploying a system produces net benefit once all affected parties' costs and benefits are weighed. A **deontological** framework judges an action by whether it follows a moral duty/rule, regardless of consequences (e.g. a duty of honesty, a duty not to discriminate); applied to computing, this asks e.g. whether a system violates a user's rights (privacy, fairness, informed consent) even if it happens to produce good aggregate outcomes. Real professional dilemmas in computing (e.g. deploying a facial-recognition system, an automated decision-making tool, or a data-harvesting feature) often pit these frameworks against each other: a consequentialist case for net public-safety benefit versus a deontological case that individual privacy/consent rights are violated regardless of aggregate benefit -- honours-level analysis names and applies these frameworks explicitly rather than only expressing a personal opinion.
