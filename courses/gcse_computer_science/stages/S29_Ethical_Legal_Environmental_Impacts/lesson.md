# S29_Ethical_Legal_Environmental_Impacts - Lesson: Ethical, legal and environmental impacts of digital technology

## Goal
The learner explains the current ethical, legal and environmental impacts and risks of digital technology across cyber security, mobile technologies, wireless networking, cloud storage, hacking, wearable technologies, computer-based implants and autonomous vehicles, and the privacy tension between citizens and governments/security services, at the level of general principles.

## Syllabus items taught here
- 3.8 - Ethical, legal and environmental impacts of digital technology, including privacy

## How to teach this
Ask the learner: is it ever right for a government to read private messages if doing so might prevent a crime -- and who should get to decide? Have the learner predict the output of every Python example before running it, and run code for real whenever they can. AQA's own exams are set in AQA's pseudo-code, not any specific language; Python (AQA's widely-used reference teaching language) is used here so examples can be run for real and their output verified, but the learner should also be shown the equivalent pseudo-code form for exam-style tracing questions, since Paper 1 code-reading/writing questions are set in pseudo-code with only the constructs taught in 3.2. Binary/hex conversions, storage and sound/image size calculations, Huffman/RLE compression and logic-gate truth tables were computed/verified when this course was built.

#### 3.8 Ethical, legal and environmental impacts of digital technology, including privacy
Digital technology raises ethical, legal and environmental questions across many areas; AQA expects general principles and balanced discussion here, not detailed technical knowledge. **Cyber security**: measures that protect data (e.g. monitoring network traffic) can themselves raise privacy concerns about how much of a user's activity is being watched, and by whom. **Mobile technologies**: constant connectivity has wellbeing implications (always-on pressure, distraction) and location/usage data collected by phones raises privacy questions about who can access it and for what purpose. **Wireless networking**: convenience and flexibility have to be weighed against a wireless signal being easier to intercept than a wired one, and against the environmental cost of the infrastructure (masts, routers, power use) supporting it. **Cloud storage**: storing data on someone else's remote servers raises legal questions (which country's laws apply to data stored there, and who can be legally compelled to hand it over), ethical questions (how much a user can really trust a third party with personal data), and environmental questions (the energy and cooling large data centres consume). **Hacking**: unauthorised access to computer systems is illegal in the UK (under the Computer Misuse Act); there is ongoing ethical debate about "ethical"/authorised hacking (e.g. penetration testers or security researchers finding and reporting flaws) compared with unauthorised, malicious hacking. **Wearable technologies** (e.g. fitness trackers, smartwatches): continuously collect personal health/location data, raising privacy questions about who else can see or sell it, and environmental questions from frequent device turnover/replacement (e-waste). **Computer-based implants** (e.g. a pacemaker with wireless monitoring): offer real medical benefits, but raise serious questions about security (could such a device be hacked?), privacy (of the health data it generates) and informed consent. **Autonomous vehicles**: raise ethical questions about how a self-driving car's software should be programmed to behave in an unavoidable accident, legal questions about who is liable when something goes wrong (the manufacturer, the software developer, the "driver"), and potential environmental benefits from more efficient, optimised driving and journeys. Underlying many of these is a wider **privacy tension between citizens and governments/security services**: balancing collective security (e.g. surveillance intended to prevent crime or terrorism) against an individual's right to privacy, with reasonable people disagreeing about where that balance should sit.
