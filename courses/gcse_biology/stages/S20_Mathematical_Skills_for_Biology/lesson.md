# S20_Mathematical_Skills_for_Biology - Lesson: Mathematical requirements for GCSE Biology

## Goal
The learner applies the mathematical skills required in AQA Biology: number and standard form, data handling and statistics, simple algebra, graphs and areas and volumes.

- M2a - Maths: significant figures: use an appropriate number of significant figures

## How to teach this
Use worked biology contexts (magnification, percentage change, population estimates) so maths feels like a tool, not a separate topic.

#### M1a Maths: decimal form
Recognise and use numbers in decimal form: 0.05 g is five hundredths of a gram; 0.5 mm is 500 micrometres. Compare decimals by aligning place values, and round sensibly to match the precision of the measurements.

#### M1b Maths: standard form
Write large and small numbers as a x 10 to the power n, with a between 1 and 10: a bacterium 0.000002 m long is 2 x 10 to the power -6 m; a population of 3 400 000 is 3.4 x 10 to the power 6. When multiplying, multiply the numbers and add the powers.

#### M1c Maths: ratios, fractions and percentages
Use ratio, fraction and percentage: a cross giving a 3:1 ratio has 75 per cent dominant offspring; percentage change = change divided by original times 100; a fraction such as 1 in 4 is 25 per cent. Simplify a ratio by dividing both parts by their common factor.

#### M1d Maths: estimating results
Estimate answers by rounding to one significant figure: 4.8 x 1.9 is about 5 x 2 = 10. Use estimates to check that calculator answers are sensible, and to estimate populations, sizes and magnification.

#### M2a Maths: significant figures
Give answers to an appropriate number of significant figures, usually the same as the least precise data value. To round to 2 significant figures, count from the first non-zero digit: 0.004372 is 0.0044; 3728 is 3700.

#### M2b Maths: arithmetic means
The arithmetic mean is the sum of the values divided by the number of values. For 12, 14, 13, 17 the mean is 56 divided by 4 = 14. Exclude anomalous results before calculating the mean, and say so.

#### M2c Maths: frequency tables, bar charts and histograms
A frequency table records how many times each value or group occurs. Bar charts have gaps and show categories; histograms have no gaps and show continuous data grouped in classes of equal width. Read and draw them with scales and labelled axes.

#### M2d Maths: sampling principles
A sample should be random, large enough to be representative, and gathered without bias. Larger samples give more reliable estimates of a population, for example with quadrats across a field.

#### M2e Maths: simple probability
Probability = number of ways an outcome can happen divided by total number of outcomes. In a genetic cross, a 1 in 4 chance is 0.25 or 25 per cent. Express as a fraction, decimal or percentage; probabilities of independent events multiply.

#### M2f Maths: mean, mode and median
The mean is the sum divided by count; the mode is the most common value; the median is the middle value when data are ordered. For 2, 3, 3, 5, 12 the mean is 5, the mode 3 and the median 3. The median is less affected by outliers.

#### M2g Maths: scatter diagrams and correlation
A scatter diagram plots two variables. Points rising left to right show positive correlation; falling shows negative; no pattern means none. A line of best fit summarises the trend. Correlation alone does not prove one variable causes the other.

#### M2h Maths: order of magnitude
Order of magnitude compares sizes to the nearest power of ten. A cell of 10 micrometres and a bacterium of 1 micrometre differ by one order of magnitude; a human at 1.7 m compared with a cell at 10 micrometres is about five orders of magnitude.

#### M3a Maths: symbols
Understand and use the symbols: = (equals), < (less than), << (much less than), >> (much greater than), > (greater than), proportional to, and ~ (approximately). For example, biomass at level 1 >> level 4, and rate proportional to concentration.

#### M3d Maths: solving simple algebraic equations
Solve simple equations by doing the same to both sides. From magnification = image size divided by real size, real size = image size divided by magnification: if magnification is 400 and image is 20 mm, real size is 20 divided by 400 = 0.05 mm.

#### M4a Maths: translating information between graphical and numeric form
Read values off a graph to fill a table, and plot table values as a graph. Use the axis scale carefully, and state units, for example reading the enzyme rate at 37 degrees Celsius from a rate against temperature graph.

#### M4b Maths: y = mx + c
The equation y = mx + c describes a straight-line relationship: m is the gradient, c is the intercept on the y axis. If y is directly proportional to x then c = 0 and the line passes through the origin.

#### M4c Maths: plotting two variables
Plot the independent variable on the x-axis and the dependent variable on the y-axis, choose scales that fill the grid, use equal intervals, label axes with units, plot points with small crosses, and draw a smooth curve or straight line of best fit through the trend, not joining the dots.

#### M4d Maths: slope and intercept
Gradient = change in y divided by change in x, using two points far apart on the line. For rate against time, the gradient is the rate of change. The intercept is the y value where the line crosses the y-axis.

#### M5c Maths: areas, surface areas and volumes
Area of rectangle = length x width; area of triangle = one half x base x height; surface area of a cube = 6 x side squared; volume of a cube = side cubed. Use these in surface-area-to-volume ratio: a cube of side 2 cm has surface area 24 cm2, volume 8 cm3, ratio 3:1; side 4 cm gives 96 and 64, ratio 1.5:1, so larger organisms exchange less efficiently.

## Explicitly not here
Higher-tier maths outside these AQA Biology requirements (such as circle theorems) is not needed.
