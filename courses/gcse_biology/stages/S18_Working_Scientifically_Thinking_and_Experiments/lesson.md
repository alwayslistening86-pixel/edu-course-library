# S18_Working_Scientifically_Thinking_and_Experiments - Lesson: Working scientifically: scientific thinking and experimental skills

## Goal
The learner explains how science develops and its ethical limits, and plans, carries out and evaluates experiments safely and accurately.

## Syllabus items taught here
- WS1.1 - Development of scientific methods and theories: explain how scientific methods and theories develop over time
- WS1.2 - Use of models: use a variety of models to develop explanations
- WS1.3 - Power and limitations of science, ethical issues: evaluate what science can and cannot answer
- WS1.4 - Applications of science and implications: explain applications and evaluate implications
- WS1.5 - Risks in practical science and society: evaluate risks
- WS1.6 - Peer review and communicating results: explain the role of peer review
- WS2.1 - Using theories to develop hypotheses: write testable hypotheses
- WS2.2 - Planning experiments: plan a fair investigation identifying variables
- WS2.3 - Selecting apparatus and techniques: select appropriate apparatus and techniques
- WS2.4 - Carrying out experiments safely and accurately: carry out experiments with accuracy and safety
- WS2.5 - Sampling techniques: recognise representative sampling
- WS2.6 - Making and recording observations: record observations and measurements
- WS2.7 - Evaluating methods and improvements: evaluate methods and suggest improvements

## How to teach this
Use a biology practical the learner has already done, such as the amylase practical, and ask: 'What would you change if you had to convince a sceptic?' Draw the answers out before naming the skill.

#### WS1.1 Development of scientific methods and theories
Scientific theories develop over time as new evidence appears: for example, Lamarck's ideas gave way to Darwin's as evidence accumulated, and the accepted model of DNA improved through X-ray images. Ideas are tested, some are rejected, and new methods enable new discoveries such as the electron microscope showing organelles.

#### WS1.2 Use of models
Models simplify reality to explain observations and make predictions: physical (such as a model heart), diagrammatic (such as the lock and key model of enzymes), and mathematical (such as population growth curves). Every model has limits, so it is important to say what a model does not show.

#### WS1.3 Power and limitations of science and ethical issues
Science can answer questions that can be tested by evidence, such as how a drug works, but cannot decide values questions, such as whether using stem cells from embryos is right. Ethical, economic and social issues affect what is done with science, so the learner should present arguments on more than one side.

#### WS1.4 Applications of science and implications
The learner explains applications, such as vaccination, GM crops and genetic screening, and weighs up their benefits against implications including cost, safety, fairness, and effects on the environment and society.

#### WS1.5 Risks in practical science and society
A hazard is something that could cause harm; risk is the likelihood and severity. Practical risk assessments name a hazard, who is at risk and how to reduce it, such as using goggles, aseptic technique or water baths. In society, risks include side effects of drugs or the spread of resistant bacteria and are judged using evidence and the perception of risk.

#### WS1.6 Peer review and communicating results
New results are checked by other scientists before publication (peer review) and shared through journals and conferences. Other scientists can then repeat the work to see if they get the same results; reproducibility strengthens confidence. Media reports may be unreliable, so the source and evidence should be evaluated.

#### WS2.1 Using theories to develop hypotheses
A hypothesis is a testable prediction based on a theory, such as 'If enzymes have an optimum pH, then the rate will fall at pH values either side.' A good hypothesis states the expected relationship between the independent and dependent variable, with reasoning.

#### WS2.2 Planning experiments
Identify the independent variable (changed), the dependent variable (measured) and control variables (kept constant). Include a control experiment where appropriate, choose a suitable range and interval for the independent variable, decide how many repeats, and state how the results will be recorded and analysed.

#### WS2.3 Selecting apparatus and techniques
Match apparatus to purpose: a measuring cylinder or pipette for volumes, a balance for mass, a stopwatch for time, a thermometer, quadrats and transect lines for sampling, and a light microscope for observation. Choose apparatus with a suitable resolution (smallest division) for the measurement.

#### WS2.4 Carrying out experiments safely and accurately
Follow the method exactly, use apparatus correctly (read the meniscus at eye level, zero the balance), wear the right protective equipment, and keep the working area tidy. Carry out the risk assessment first.

#### WS2.5 Sampling techniques
A sample must be representative and large enough. Random sampling with random-number coordinates avoids bias; systematic sampling (such as along a transect) is used to study change across a gradient. Larger samples reduce the effect of chance.

#### WS2.6 Making and recording observations and measurements
Record data in a table with clear headings and units in the heading, not in the body. Give all readings to the same number of decimal places consistent with the apparatus resolution, and record repeats. Careful observations such as colour change and drawings are recorded in words.

#### WS2.7 Evaluating methods and suggesting improvements
Evaluate by looking for sources of random error (which give scatter) and systematic error (a consistent shift, for example a badly calibrated balance). Suggest specific improvements: use a more precise instrument, control the temperature with a water bath, increase the number of repeats or the range of values.
