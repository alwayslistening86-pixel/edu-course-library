# S13_Variation_Selection_and_Genetic_Technology - Lesson: Variation, natural selection, selective breeding, genetic engineering and cloning

## Goal
The learner explains variation and natural selection, and evaluates selective breeding, genetic engineering and cloning techniques.

## Syllabus items taught here
- 4.6.2.1 - Variation: distinguish genetic and environmental variation
- 4.6.2.2 - Evolution: explain natural selection
- 4.6.2.3 - Selective breeding: describe selective breeding and its risks
- 4.6.2.4 - Genetic engineering: describe genetic engineering and its benefits and risks
- 4.6.2.5 - Cloning: describe tissue culture, cuttings, embryo transplants and adult cell cloning (Biology-only)

## How to teach this
Ask: 'Why are all pedigree dogs one species, yet so different in size and shape?' Follow with: 'Who did the selecting, and how does that differ from the wild?'

#### 4.6.2.1 Variation
Differences between individuals of the same species result from genes inherited from parents, from the environment (for example diet, climate, accidents), or a combination of the two. Mutations happen continuously and occasionally lead to a new phenotype. Rarely, a mutation gives a new phenotype that is well suited to a changed environment.

#### 4.6.2.2 Evolution
Evolution is a change in the inherited characteristics of a population over time through natural selection, and may result in a new species. Individuals with characteristics that suit the environment are more likely to survive and breed successfully, and so pass those characteristics (alleles) to the next generation. Over many generations the proportion of individuals with these alleles increases. Explain in steps: variation, survival, reproduction, inheritance.

#### 4.6.2.3 Selective breeding
Humans choose parents with desired characteristics and breed them, selecting the best offspring over many generations. Examples: disease-resistant crops, animals producing more meat or milk, large or unusual flowers, gentle dogs. Selective breeding can cause inbreeding, giving a smaller gene pool, increased risk of inherited defects and susceptibility to disease.

#### 4.6.2.4 Genetic engineering
Genetic engineering changes an organism's genome by transferring a gene from another organism. Enzymes cut out the gene, a vector such as a plasmid or a virus carries it into the target cells, ideally at an early stage of development so all cells have it. Examples: crops resistant to insects or herbicides, bacteria making human insulin, and golden rice. Benefits include higher yields and improved nutrition; concerns include effects on wild flower populations, on insects, and on human health.

#### 4.6.2.5 Cloning
Tissue culture uses small groups of cells taken from the growing tips of a plant, placed on agar with hormones, to produce clones quickly. Cuttings are pieces of stem grown into new plants. Embryo transplants split a developing animal embryo into several before cells become specialised and implant them into host mothers. Adult cell cloning takes a nucleus from an adult cell and inserts it into an egg cell with the nucleus removed, then stimulates it with an electric shock to divide; the embryo is implanted in a womb. Clones reduce variation but preserve desirable traits.
