# S16_Decay_Biodiversity_and_Global_Warming - Lesson: Decomposition, environmental change, biodiversity and human impact

## Goal
The learner explains decomposition and its uses, environmental change, biodiversity, pollution, land use, deforestation, global warming and conservation, and investigates the rate of decay.

## Syllabus items taught here
- 4.7.2.3 - Decomposition: explain the factors affecting decay and compost (Biology-only)
- 4.7.2.4 - Impact of environmental change: explain how environmental change affects distribution (Biology-only)
- 4.7.3.1 - Biodiversity: explain biodiversity and human impact on it
- 4.7.3.2 - Waste management: describe pollution of water, air and land
- 4.7.3.3 - Land use: explain the effects of land use such as peat destruction
- 4.7.3.4 - Deforestation: explain the causes and effects of deforestation
- 4.7.3.5 - Global warming: explain the greenhouse effect and consequences of global warming
- 4.7.3.6 - Maintaining biodiversity: evaluate conservation programmes
- RP10 - Required practical 10: rate of decay: investigate temperature and decay of milk (Biology-only)

## How to teach this
Ask: 'Where does a fallen leaf go? And what would the woodland look like without whatever makes it disappear?'

#### 4.7.2.3 Decomposition
Microorganisms break down dead plants and animals, returning mineral ions to the soil for plants. Decay is faster with a warm temperature, plenty of water and plenty of oxygen. Compost uses decay to make fertiliser: heaps are kept warm, moist and aerated. Anaerobic decay in biogas generators produces methane that can be burned as fuel.

#### 4.7.2.4 Impact of environmental change
Changes in temperature, water availability and the composition of atmospheric gases affect where organisms live and their numbers. Seasonal, geographical or human-caused changes can shift the distribution of species; for example a warmer climate can change migration patterns and range.

#### 4.7.3.1 Biodiversity
Biodiversity is the variety of all living species on Earth or in an ecosystem. High biodiversity gives ecosystems stability because species depend on each other. Humans have reduced biodiversity by using land, generating waste, deforestation and adding greenhouse gases; there are now positive efforts to reduce the harm.

#### 4.7.3.2 Waste management
As the population and standard of living rise, more resources are used and more waste is produced. Water can be polluted by sewage, fertiliser and toxic chemicals; air by smoke and acidic gases from burning fuel; land by landfill and toxic chemicals, including pesticides. Unless carefully managed, pollution kills plants and animals and reduces biodiversity.

#### 4.7.3.3 Land use
Humans reduce land available for other animals and plants by building, quarrying, farming and dumping waste. Peat bogs are destroyed to provide compost and fuel; this releases carbon dioxide into the atmosphere and destroys habitats for specialised species.

#### 4.7.3.4 Deforestation
Large-scale deforestation in tropical areas provides land for cattle and rice fields, and for growing crops for biofuels. Consequences include reduced biodiversity, reduced photosynthesis removing less CO2 from the air, and CO2 released by burning and decay of the trees.

#### 4.7.3.5 Global warming
Greenhouse gases such as carbon dioxide and methane absorb thermal energy, maintaining Earth's temperature; increasing levels contribute to global warming. Consequences include rising sea levels from melting ice, changes in the distribution of species, loss of habitat and extreme weather, and less biodiversity.

#### 4.7.3.6 Maintaining biodiversity
Actions include breeding programmes for endangered species, protection and regeneration of rare habitats, reintroduction of field margins and hedgerows in agricultural areas where farmers grow only one crop, reducing deforestation and CO2 emissions by some governments, and recycling resources rather than dumping waste. Conservation may conflict with economic needs of local people, so evaluations weigh costs and benefits.

#### RP10 Required practical 10: rate of decay
Investigate the effect of temperature on the rate of decay of fresh milk by measuring pH change. Milk with lipase and sodium carbonate solution contains an indicator; as fat is broken down, fatty acids lower the pH and the indicator changes colour (phenolphthalein pink to colourless). Measure the time to change at several temperatures in water baths. Control volumes and concentrations, and repeat for a mean.
