# S17_Trophic_Levels_and_Food_Security - Lesson: Trophic levels, biomass transfer, food security and farming

## Goal
The learner describes trophic levels, constructs pyramids of biomass, calculates biomass transfer, and evaluates factors affecting food security, farming techniques, fishing and biotechnology.

## Syllabus items taught here
- 4.7.4.1 - Trophic levels: describe trophic levels (Biology-only)
- 4.7.4.2 - Pyramids of biomass: construct and interpret pyramids of biomass (Biology-only)
- 4.7.4.3 - Transfer of biomass: calculate biomass transfer efficiency (Biology-only)
- 4.7.5.1 - Factors affecting food security: list the factors affecting food security (Biology-only)
- 4.7.5.2 - Farming techniques: evaluate intensive farming techniques (Biology-only)
- 4.7.5.3 - Sustainable fisheries: describe sustainable fishing (Biology-only)
- 4.7.5.4 - Role of biotechnology: describe biotechnology in food production (Biology-only)

## How to teach this
Ask: 'Why are there many more grass plants than lions in a savanna, and why do food chains rarely have more than five links?'

#### 4.7.4.1 Trophic levels
Trophic levels are the feeding positions in a food chain. Level 1 is producers (photosynthetic organisms), level 2 primary consumers (herbivores), level 3 secondary consumers, and so on; apex predators are at the top and have no predators. Decomposers break down dead plant and animal matter and are not counted as a trophic level in the chain.

#### 4.7.4.2 Pyramids of biomass
A pyramid of biomass shows the mass of living material (dry mass) at each trophic level, drawn to scale: each bar is the biomass, the first level being the widest at the bottom. Pyramids are constructed from data, with the producer at the base, and are usually a pyramid shape because biomass decreases up the levels.

#### 4.7.4.3 Transfer of biomass
Biomass is lost between trophic levels because not all of it is eaten, not all of what is eaten is absorbed (waste, faeces), and some is lost through respiration and in excretion (urine). Efficiency = biomass transferred divided by biomass available, times 100. About 10 per cent is transferred at each stage. Such losses explain why food chains have few trophic levels and why eating plants is more efficient than eating animals.

#### 4.7.5.1 Factors affecting food security
Food security means having enough food to feed a population. Factors include rising world population and birth rate, changing diets in developed countries (increasing demand for meat), new pests and pathogens, environmental change such as drought, the cost of agricultural inputs, and conflicts that affect availability.

#### 4.7.5.2 Farming techniques
Efficiency can be improved by reducing energy transfer losses: limit movement of livestock and control their temperature so less energy is lost in respiration; feed them high-protein food. Intensive methods raise output but raise ethical concerns about animal welfare, and can also raise costs and environmental impact.

#### 4.7.5.3 Sustainable fisheries
Fish stocks in the oceans are declining, so it is important to maintain them at a level where breeding continues and future generations can catch fish. Methods include fishing quotas and controlling net size so that young fish are not caught. These measures preserve populations and biodiversity while allowing fishing.

#### 4.7.5.4 Role of biotechnology
The fungus Fusarium is grown on glucose syrup in aerobic conditions in large fermenters to produce mycoprotein for human consumption. Genetically modified bacteria produce human insulin, and GM crops such as golden rice give higher yields or added nutrients. Biotechnology can raise food security, but people question risks and costs.
