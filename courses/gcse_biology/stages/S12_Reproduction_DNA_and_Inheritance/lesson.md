# S12_Reproduction_DNA_and_Inheritance - Lesson: Reproduction, meiosis, DNA structure, the genome and genetic inheritance

## Goal
The learner compares sexual and asexual reproduction, describes meiosis, DNA and the genome, completes genetic crosses, and explains inherited disorders and sex determination.

## How to teach this
Ask: 'Why are you similar to your parents but not identical to them?' It leads to meiosis, alleles and random fertilisation.

#### 4.6.1.1 Sexual and asexual reproduction
Sexual reproduction involves the joining of male and female gametes (sperm and egg cells in animals; pollen and egg cells in flowering plants), formed by meiosis, and produces variation in offspring. Asexual reproduction involves one parent, no gametes and no fusion, and produces genetically identical clones by mitosis.

#### 4.6.1.2 Meiosis
Meiosis takes place in the reproductive organs and produces gametes. The DNA is copied and the cell divides twice, forming four gametes, each with a single set of chromosomes (haploid) and genetically different from each other. At fertilisation the diploid number is restored and the new cell divides by mitosis, with cells differentiating as the embryo develops.

#### 4.6.1.3 Advantages and disadvantages of sexual and asexual reproduction
Sexual: variation helps a species survive changing environments and allows selective breeding. Asexual: only one parent is needed, it is faster and uses less energy, and many identical offspring appear quickly in favourable conditions, though a disease can then wipe out a whole clone. Some organisms use both: malarial parasites reproduce asexually in the human host and sexually in the mosquito; fungi reproduce by spores; strawberry plants by runners; daffodils by bulb division.

#### 4.6.1.4 DNA and the genome
The genome is the entire genetic material of an organism. The Human Genome Project mapped all the human genes. Understanding the genome helps identify genes linked to disease, understand and treat inherited disorders, and trace human migration patterns from the past.

#### 4.6.1.5 DNA structure
DNA is a polymer made of repeating units called nucleotides. Each nucleotide has a common sugar and phosphate group and one of four bases: A, C, G or T. Two strands form a double helix, with A pairing with T and C with G. A gene is a sequence of bases; three bases code for one amino acid, and the order of bases determines the order of amino acids in a protein. Ribosomes assemble proteins; messenger RNA carries the code from the nucleus. Mutations change the base sequence, usually with little effect, but may change the protein shape.

#### 4.6.1.6 Genetic inheritance
Alleles are different versions of a gene. A dominant allele is expressed if present; a recessive allele only if two copies are present. Homozygous means two identical alleles, heterozygous two different. Genotype is the alleles present; phenotype is the characteristic expressed. In a monohybrid cross use a Punnett square: two heterozygous parents (Bb x Bb) give 1 BB, 2 Bb, 1 bb, a 3:1 ratio of dominant to recessive phenotype. Most characteristics are controlled by several genes.

#### 4.6.1.7 Inherited disorders
Polydactyly (extra fingers or toes) is caused by a dominant allele. Cystic fibrosis is a disorder of cell membranes caused by a recessive allele, so two carrier parents (Ff) have a 1 in 4 chance of each child having it. Embryo screening allows selection of embryos without a disorder, raising ethical issues about destroying embryos, cost and the potential for choosing traits.

#### 4.6.1.8 Sex determination
Humans have 23 pairs of chromosomes; one pair are the sex chromosomes. Females have XX, males XY. A Punnett square of XX x XY shows a 50 per cent chance of each sex at every birth.
