# S01_Cells_and_Microscopy - Lesson: Cell structure, microscopy and culturing microorganisms

## Goal
The learner describes prokaryotic and eukaryotic cells, links structure to function in specialised cells, uses the magnification formula, and describes aseptic culturing of microorganisms.

## Syllabus items taught here
- 4.1.1.1 - Eukaryotes and prokaryotes: distinguish eukaryotic and prokaryotic cells by structure
- 4.1.1.2 - Animal and plant cells: name and explain the function of sub-cellular structures
- 4.1.1.3 - Cell specialisation: link the structure of specialised cells to their function
- 4.1.1.4 - Cell differentiation: explain differentiation in animals and plants
- 4.1.1.5 - Microscopy: use magnification formula and compare light and electron microscopes
- 4.1.1.6 - Culturing microorganisms: describe aseptic culture technique and calculate colony growth (Biology-only)
- RP1 - Required practical 1: microscopy: observe, draw and label plant and animal cells
- RP2 - Required practical 2: antiseptics and bacterial growth: use agar plates and zones of inhibition (Biology-only)

## How to teach this
Start with: 'A bacterium and a liver cell are both alive. What must every living cell have in common, and what might differ?' Let the learner draw and label before any lists are given.

#### 4.1.1.1 Eukaryotes and prokaryotes
Eukaryotic cells (animals, plants, fungi, protists) have a nucleus enclosed by a membrane that holds the genetic material. Prokaryotic cells (bacteria) have no nucleus: their genetic material is a single loop of DNA free in the cytoplasm, with extra small rings of DNA called plasmids. Prokaryotes are much smaller (about 1 to 5 micrometres against 10 to 100 micrometres). Both types have cytoplasm, a cell membrane and ribosomes; bacteria also have a cell wall.

#### 4.1.1.2 Animal and plant cells
Animal cells have a nucleus (controls the cell and holds DNA), cytoplasm (where reactions happen), a cell membrane (controls what enters and leaves), mitochondria (aerobic respiration) and ribosomes (protein synthesis). Plant cells have all of these plus a cellulose cell wall (support), a permanent vacuole containing cell sap, and chloroplasts containing chlorophyll for photosynthesis. Root cells have no chloroplasts because they are underground.

#### 4.1.1.3 Cell specialisation
Always link structure to function. A sperm cell has a streamlined head, a tail, many mitochondria and an acrosome holding enzymes. A nerve cell has a long axon and branched dendrites to carry and connect impulses. Muscle cells contain protein fibres that shorten and many mitochondria. Root hair cells have a large surface area for water uptake. Xylem is hollow, lignified dead tissue; phloem has sieve plates and companion cells.

#### 4.1.1.4 Cell differentiation
As an organism develops, cells change to become specialised: this is differentiation. Most animal cells differentiate early and then lose the ability to change, though many keep the ability to divide to repair or replace tissue. Plant cells in meristems can differentiate throughout life. In mature animals differentiation is mainly limited to repair and replacement, for example in bone marrow producing blood cells.

#### 4.1.1.5 Microscopy
Light microscopes let us see nuclei and cell walls; electron microscopes have higher magnification and resolving power, revealing mitochondria, ribosomes and plasmids in detail. Use magnification = image size divided by real size, keeping units the same: an image of 12 mm for a cell of 0.02 mm gives magnification 600 times. Convert mm to micrometres by multiplying by 1000, and use standard form for very small sizes.

#### 4.1.1.6 Culturing microorganisms
Bacteria are grown in nutrient broth or on agar plates. Aseptic technique prevents contamination: sterilise Petri dishes and culture media, pass inoculating loops through a flame, lift the lid only slightly, tape the lid on with two pieces of tape, store upside down, and incubate at 25 degrees Celsius in schools to avoid growing human pathogens. Population growth: bacteria divide by binary fission about every 20 minutes, so one cell becomes 2 to the power of n after n divisions. Colony area is pi r squared, and antibiotic effectiveness is compared using the area of the clear zone.

#### RP1 Required practical 1: microscopy
Use a light microscope to observe, draw and label plant and animal cells, for example onion epidermis stained with iodine. Start with the lowest power objective, focus with the coarse then fine focus wheel, and only then increase magnification. Draw with a sharp pencil, in clear continuous lines, no shading, label lines not crossing, and include a title and the magnification worked out as objective lens times eyepiece lens.

#### RP2 Required practical 2: antiseptics and bacterial growth
Investigate the effect of antiseptics or antibiotics on bacterial growth using agar plates. Spread a bacterial lawn, place discs soaked in different antiseptics on it, incubate at 25 degrees Celsius and measure the diameter of each zone of inhibition. The larger the clear zone, the more effective the antiseptic. Control variables: concentration, volume, disc size, bacterial species, incubation time and temperature. Areas compare via pi r squared. Include a control disc soaked in sterile water.
