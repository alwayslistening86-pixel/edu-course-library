# S08_Defence_Vaccines_and_Drugs - Lesson: Defence systems, vaccines, drugs, monoclonal antibodies and plant defence

## Goal
The learner explains human defences and vaccination, antibiotics and drug development, monoclonal antibody production and uses, and plant disease detection and defence.

## Syllabus items taught here
- 4.3.1.6 - Human defence systems: explain non-specific defences and the immune response
- 4.3.1.7 - Vaccination: explain vaccination and herd immunity
- 4.3.1.8 - Antibiotics and painkillers: explain antibiotic action and resistance
- 4.3.1.9 - Discovery and development of drugs: describe drug sources, testing and clinical trials
- 4.3.2.1 - Producing monoclonal antibodies: describe production of monoclonal antibodies (Biology-only)
- 4.3.2.2 - Uses of monoclonal antibodies: describe uses in diagnosis, research and treatment (Biology-only)

## How to teach this
Ask: 'Why do you catch chickenpox once but a cold many times?' It leads to antigens, antibodies and memory cells.

#### 4.3.1.6 Human defence systems
Non-specific defences include skin, hairs and mucus in the nose, cilia and mucus in the trachea and bronchi, and stomach acid. Inside the body white blood cells act: phagocytes engulf pathogens, lymphocytes produce antibodies specific to the pathogen's antigens, and some produce antitoxins. Memory cells remain so a second infection is met with a faster, stronger response.

#### 4.3.1.7 Vaccination
A vaccine contains dead or inactive pathogens carrying antigens. The immune system makes antibodies and memory cells, so if the live pathogen enters later it is destroyed quickly. If enough of a population is vaccinated, the pathogen cannot spread easily and unvaccinated people are protected: herd immunity.

#### 4.3.1.8 Antibiotics and painkillers
Antibiotics such as penicillin kill bacteria inside the body but do not work on viruses, which live inside cells. Painkillers relieve symptoms but do not kill pathogens. Different antibiotics work on different bacteria. Overuse leads to resistant strains through mutation and natural selection, such as MRSA, so antibiotics should not be prescribed for viral infections.

#### 4.3.1.9 Discovery and development of drugs
Drugs come from plants and microorganisms: digitalis (heart disease) from foxgloves, aspirin from willow, penicillin from Penicillium mould. New drugs are tested in preclinical trials on cells, tissues and live animals, then in clinical trials on healthy volunteers (low doses to test safety) and patients (to find the optimum dose). Trials are double blind and use a placebo; results are peer reviewed.

#### 4.3.2.1 Producing monoclonal antibodies
Monoclonal antibodies are identical antibodies produced from a single clone of cells. A mouse is immunised with an antigen; lymphocytes making the specific antibody are taken and fused with a tumour cell to form a hybridoma. The hybridoma divides rapidly and produces the antibody, which is collected and purified in large quantity.

#### 4.3.2.2 Uses of monoclonal antibodies
Because each antibody binds to only one antigen, it is used to diagnose (pregnancy tests detect hCG), in laboratories to locate or identify molecules by attaching a fluorescent dye, and in treatment, for example carrying a radioactive substance or drug to cancer cells only. They have caused more side effects than first expected.

#### 4.3.3.1 Detection and identification of plant diseases
Signs include stunted growth, spots on leaves, areas of decay, growths, malformed stems or leaves, discolouration, and the presence of pests. The disease can be identified using a gardening manual or website, by analysis in a laboratory, or by testing kits that contain monoclonal antibodies.

#### 4.3.3.2 Plant defence responses
Physical defences include cellulose cell walls, a waxy cuticle and layers of dead cells (bark) around stems. Chemical defences are antibacterial chemicals and poisons that deter herbivores. Mechanical adaptations include thorns and hairs, and leaves that droop or curl when touched. Mimicry can also deter herbivores.
