# S09_Nervous_System_and_Homeostasis - Lesson: Homeostasis, the nervous system, the brain, the eye and temperature control

## Goal
The learner defines homeostasis, describes the nervous system and reflex arc, the brain and the eye, explains temperature control, and investigates reaction time.

## Syllabus items taught here
- 4.5.1 - Homeostasis: define homeostasis and its control components
- 4.5.2.1 - Structure and function: describe the nervous system and the reflex arc
- 4.5.2.2 - The brain: describe brain regions and methods of investigating the brain (Biology-only)
- 4.5.2.3 - The eye: describe eye structure, accommodation and defects (Biology-only)
- 4.5.2.4 - Control of body temperature: explain thermoregulation (Biology-only)
- RP7 - Required practical 7: human reaction time: plan an investigation into a factor affecting reaction time

## How to teach this
Ask: 'Why do you shiver in the cold and sweat when hot, without deciding to?' Build homeostasis from that.

#### 4.5.1 Homeostasis
Homeostasis is the regulation of internal conditions to maintain optimum conditions for enzymes and cell function, for example temperature, blood glucose and water content. Control systems have receptors (detect stimuli), coordination centres (brain, spinal cord, pancreas) that process information, and effectors (muscles or glands) that produce responses. Nervous and hormonal systems both take part.

#### 4.5.2.1 Structure and function
The central nervous system is the brain and spinal cord. Sensory neurones carry impulses from receptors to the CNS, relay neurones connect inside it, and motor neurones carry impulses to effectors. A synapse is a gap where a chemical transmitter diffuses across to trigger the next impulse. In a reflex arc: stimulus, receptor, sensory neurone, relay neurone, motor neurone, effector, response. Reflexes are rapid and automatic, protecting the body.

#### 4.5.2.2 The brain
The cerebral cortex controls consciousness, intelligence, memory and language; the cerebellum coordinates muscular activity and balance; the medulla controls unconscious activities such as heart rate and breathing. Methods of study include observing patients with brain damage, electrical stimulation of parts of the brain, and MRI scanning. The brain is complex and delicate, so damage and disease are difficult to treat.

#### 4.5.2.3 The eye
Parts: cornea (refracts light), iris (regulates pupil size), lens (focuses light), ciliary muscles and suspensory ligaments, retina (light receptors), optic nerve. In bright light the iris circular muscles contract and the pupil narrows. Accommodation: for near objects the ciliary muscles contract, suspensory ligaments loosen and the lens becomes thicker; for distant objects the ciliary muscles relax, ligaments tighten and the lens becomes thinner. Myopia is corrected with a concave lens, hyperopia with a convex lens; other treatments include contact lenses, laser surgery and replacement lenses.

#### 4.5.2.4 Control of body temperature
Body temperature is monitored and controlled by the thermoregulatory centre in the brain, which contains receptors sensitive to blood temperature; the skin also has temperature receptors. If too hot, sweat glands release sweat that evaporates to cool, and blood vessels near the skin surface dilate (vasodilation). If too cold, sweating stops, blood vessels constrict, and skeletal muscles contract rapidly (shivering) to release energy. Optimum is about 37 degrees Celsius.

#### RP7 Required practical 7: human reaction time
Plan and carry out an investigation into the effect of a factor on human reaction time, for example caffeine, practice, or distraction. Use the ruler-drop test: a partner drops a ruler without warning and the learner catches it; record the distance and convert to time. Control variables: same hand, same person, same ruler. Repeat several times to calculate a mean, identify anomalies, and note that human error in timing is why the ruler method is better than a stopwatch.
