# S14_Evolution_Evidence_and_Classification - Lesson: Theory of evolution, speciation, fossils, extinction, resistance and classification

## Goal
The learner compares theories of evolution, explains speciation, evidence from fossils and resistant bacteria, and classifies organisms.

## How to teach this
Ask: 'Why did it take so long for Darwin's idea to be accepted, given how obvious it seems now?' Steer toward evidence, mechanism and beliefs.

#### 4.6.3.1 Theory of evolution
Charles Darwin proposed that species evolve by natural selection, published in On the Origin of Species (1859). Earlier, Lamarck suggested that changes acquired during life, for example a giraffe stretching its neck, could be inherited; this was later rejected because the evidence did not support it. Darwin's theory was controversial because it challenged religious beliefs about creation, and at the time there was too little evidence to convince many scientists, and no known mechanism of inheritance.

#### 4.6.3.2 Speciation
If populations of one species become isolated, for example by geography, natural selection acts differently in each, and over time they may become so different that they can no longer interbreed to produce fertile offspring: new species have formed. Alfred Russel Wallace independently proposed natural selection and gathered evidence on speciation, especially through work on warning colouration in animals and the geographical distribution of species.

#### 4.6.3.3 The understanding of genetics
Mendel in the 19th century showed, by breeding pea plants, that characteristics are inherited as separate units, some dominant and some recessive, though genes were not known then. In the 20th century, scientists observed chromosomes dividing in cell division and identified DNA as the genetic material; by the mid-20th century the structure of DNA was described. This explained inheritance and mutations, which supported Darwin's theory.

#### 4.6.3.4 Evidence for evolution
Evidence includes fossils, which show how organisms have changed over time, and the rapid evolution of antibiotic-resistant bacteria, which can be observed within a human lifetime. Both together show that species change as a result of natural selection.

#### 4.6.3.5 Fossils
Fossils form from the hard parts of organisms that do not decay (bones, shells), from parts replaced by minerals as they decay, or from traces such as footprints and burrows, preserved rock impressions of soft-bodied organisms. The fossil record is incomplete because many early life forms were soft-bodied and left few traces, and many fossils were destroyed by geological activity or have not yet been found.

#### 4.6.3.6 Extinction
Extinction may be caused by a catastrophic event such as a volcanic eruption or an asteroid impact, or by changes to the environment over geological time, new predators, new diseases, or new, more successful competitors. Species can become extinct when no individuals remain that can cope with a new environmental condition.

#### 4.6.3.7 Resistant bacteria
Bacteria can develop resistance to antibiotics by mutation. In the presence of an antibiotic, resistant bacteria survive and reproduce, so resistant strains such as MRSA become common. To reduce resistance, doctors should avoid prescribing antibiotics for viral infections, patients should complete courses, and agricultural use of antibiotics should be restricted. The development of new antibiotics is costly and slow, and cannot keep up.

#### 4.6.4 Classification of living organisms
Linnaeus classified organisms into kingdom, phylum, class, order, family, genus and species, based on structure and characteristics, and named organisms with a binomial system (genus and species, for example Homo sapiens). With microscopes and biochemical evidence, Carl Woese proposed the three-domain system: archaea, bacteria and eukaryota. Evolutionary trees show how related organisms are, using evidence from both living species and fossils.
