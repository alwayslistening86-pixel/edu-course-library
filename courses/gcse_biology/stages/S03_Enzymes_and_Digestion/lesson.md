# S03_Enzymes_and_Digestion - Lesson: Principles of organisation, enzymes and digestion

## Goal
The learner explains levels of organisation, enzyme action, the human digestive system and the food tests, and can carry out and evaluate an enzyme investigation.

## Syllabus items taught here
- 4.2.1 - Principles of organisation: relate cells, tissues, organs and systems and explain enzyme action
- 4.2.2.1 - The human digestive system: describe digestion, enzymes, bile and the food tests
- RP4 - Required practical 4: food tests: use Benedict's, iodine and biuret reagents
- RP5 - Required practical 5: pH and amylase: investigate pH and rate of reaction

## How to teach this
Ask: 'Why does a piece of bread start to taste sweet if you chew it for a minute?' The answer leads to amylase, substrates, and products.

#### 4.2.1 Principles of organisation
Cells are organised into tissues (groups of similar cells), organs (tissues working together) and organ systems (organs working together), which together make an organism. Enzymes are biological catalysts made of protein: each has an active site whose shape matches only its substrate (lock and key). Rate depends on temperature and pH; beyond the optimum the active site changes shape and the enzyme is denatured. Enzymes speed up reactions without being used up.

#### 4.2.2.1 The human digestive system
Digestion breaks down large insoluble molecules so they can be absorbed. Amylase (salivary glands, pancreas, small intestine) breaks starch into sugars; proteases (stomach, pancreas, small intestine) break protein into amino acids; lipases (pancreas, small intestine) break lipids into fatty acids and glycerol. The stomach makes hydrochloric acid, giving the low pH protease needs. Bile, made in the liver and stored in the gall bladder, is alkaline to neutralise stomach acid and emulsifies fat into droplets, giving lipase a larger surface area. The products build carbohydrates, lipids and proteins, and some glucose is used in respiration.

#### RP4 Required practical 4: food tests
Test for sugars with Benedict's reagent (heat in a water bath: blue to green, yellow, orange or brick red, depending on concentration), starch with iodine (orange-brown to blue-black), and protein with biuret reagent (blue to purple). Lipids can be tested with the ethanol emulsion test (white cloudy emulsion) or Sudan III. Use safety goggles, water baths not naked flames, and test known samples as controls.

#### RP5 Required practical 5: pH and amylase
Investigate the effect of pH on the rate of reaction of amylase. Mix amylase, starch and a buffer solution of a chosen pH in a water bath at constant temperature. Every 30 seconds, take a drop of the mixture into iodine on a spotting tile; record the time when iodine stays orange-brown (starch gone). Rate is proportional to 1 divided by the time taken. Control temperature, volumes, and concentrations, and repeat to calculate a mean.
