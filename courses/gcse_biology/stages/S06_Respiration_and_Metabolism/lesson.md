# S06_Respiration_and_Metabolism - Lesson: Respiration, exercise and metabolism

## Goal
The learner writes respiration equations, compares aerobic and anaerobic respiration, explains the body's response to exercise and oxygen debt, and defines metabolism.

## Syllabus items taught here
- 4.4.2.1 - Aerobic and anaerobic respiration: write respiration equations and compare aerobic and anaerobic
- 4.4.2.2 - Response to exercise: explain the body's response to exercise and oxygen debt
- 4.4.2.3 - Metabolism: define metabolism and list its reactions

## How to teach this
Ask the learner to run on the spot for a minute and describe what changed in their body, then ask why each change happened.

#### 4.4.2.1 Aerobic and anaerobic respiration
Respiration transfers energy from glucose and is exothermic. Aerobic respiration uses oxygen and takes place in mitochondria: glucose + oxygen gives carbon dioxide + water (C6H12O6 + 6O2 gives 6CO2 + 6H2O). Anaerobic respiration in muscles gives glucose to lactic acid; in plants and yeast it gives ethanol + carbon dioxide (fermentation, used in brewing and baking). Anaerobic releases much less energy per glucose molecule.

#### 4.4.2.2 Response to exercise
During exercise the muscles need more energy, so heart rate, breathing rate and breath volume increase to supply more oxygen and remove more carbon dioxide. If oxygen supply is not enough, anaerobic respiration produces lactic acid, causing muscle fatigue. After exercise, extra oxygen is needed to react with the lactic acid: this is the oxygen debt. Blood transports lactic acid to the liver to be converted back to glucose.

#### 4.4.2.3 Metabolism
Metabolism is the sum of all the chemical reactions in a cell or the body. It includes conversion of glucose to starch, glycogen and cellulose, formation of lipid molecules from a molecule of glycerol and three fatty acids, use of glucose and nitrate ions to make amino acids and then proteins, respiration, and breakdown of excess protein to form urea for excretion.

Photosynthesis (previous stage) and homeostasis in exercise (later stage).
