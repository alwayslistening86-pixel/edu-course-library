# S15_Ecosystems_and_Adaptation - Lesson: Communities, abiotic and biotic factors, adaptations, food webs, sampling and material cycles

## Goal
The learner describes ecosystems and interdependence, abiotic and biotic factors, adaptations, sampling methods and the carbon and water cycles, and can plan a population sampling investigation.

## Syllabus items taught here
- 4.7.1.1 - Communities: define ecosystem, population, community and interdependence
- 4.7.1.2 - Abiotic factors: describe abiotic factors and their effects
- 4.7.1.3 - Biotic factors: describe biotic factors including competition and predator-prey cycles
- 4.7.1.4 - Adaptations: describe structural, behavioural and functional adaptations and extremophiles
- 4.7.2.1 - Levels of organisation: use food chains, webs and quadrat/transect sampling
- 4.7.2.2 - How materials are cycled: describe the carbon and water cycles
- RP9 - Required practical 9: population sampling: use quadrats to estimate population size and distribution

## How to teach this
Ask: 'If all the foxes in a woodland disappeared, what else changes?' Have the learner draw the connections before naming any terms.

#### 4.7.1.1 Communities
An ecosystem is the interaction of a community of living organisms with the non-living (abiotic) parts of the environment. A population is all the organisms of one species in a habitat; a community is all the populations living together. Organisms depend on each other for food, shelter and pollination (interdependence). In a stable community all species and environmental factors are in balance, so population sizes remain fairly constant, and materials are cycled.

#### 4.7.1.2 Abiotic factors
Abiotic factors that affect communities include light intensity, temperature, moisture levels, soil pH and mineral content, wind intensity and direction, carbon dioxide levels for plants, and oxygen levels for aquatic animals. Change in any of these can change which species can live there.

#### 4.7.1.3 Biotic factors
Biotic factors include availability of food, new predators arriving, new pathogens, and competition between species (for food, light, water, space, mates or territory). In predator-prey cycles, prey numbers rise, then predator numbers rise, then prey fall, then predators fall; the predator curve lags behind the prey curve.

#### 4.7.1.4 Adaptations
Structural adaptations are body features, such as a polar bear's thick fur; behavioural adaptations are ways of behaving, such as migration; functional adaptations are processes, such as sweating or hibernating. Some organisms, called extremophiles, live in extreme conditions such as high temperatures (deep-sea vents), high salt concentrations or high pressure.

#### 4.7.2.1 Levels of organisation
Food chains begin with a producer (usually a green plant) that makes glucose by photosynthesis, then primary, secondary and tertiary consumers; apex predators have no predators. Food webs show interlinked chains. Sampling: use random quadrat placement with random number coordinates to estimate population size (mean per square metre times total area), and belt transects to see how distribution changes across a gradient such as light or moisture.

#### 4.7.2.2 How materials are cycled
Materials such as carbon and water are recycled. In the carbon cycle photosynthesis removes CO2 from the air, respiration by plants, animals and decomposers returns it, feeding passes carbon along food chains, and combustion of fossil fuels releases CO2. In the water cycle, water evaporates from the sea and land, condenses to form clouds, falls as precipitation, and returns via rivers and groundwater; fresh water is supplied by this for drinking and industry.

#### RP9 Required practical 9: population sampling
Measure the population size of a common species in a habitat and use sampling techniques to investigate the effect of a factor on its distribution. Use a quadrat at random coordinates, count the organisms, repeat many times and calculate the mean per square metre; multiply by the area of the habitat. For distribution, lay a transect line from an area of the factor (for example shade) and sample at regular intervals. Sources of error include small samples and non-random placement.
