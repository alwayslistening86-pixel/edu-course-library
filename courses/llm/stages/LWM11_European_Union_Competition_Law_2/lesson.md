# LWM11_European_Union_Competition_Law_2 - Lesson: EU Competition Law C-D — Merger control and enforcement procedure

## Goal
The learner applies the EU Merger Regulation's SIEC test and the Regulation 1/2003 enforcement framework, engaging critically with jurisdictional fragmentation and decentralised enforcement.

## Syllabus items taught here
- LWM11-C1 - EU merger control: the SIEC test: Apply the EUMR one-stop-shop and the significant-impediment-to-effective-competition test
- LWM11-C2 - Joint ventures and jurisdictional thresholds: Apply the full-function joint venture rules and evaluate the EUMR threshold cliff-edge problem
- LWM11-D1 - Regulation 1/2003: modernisation and self-assessment: Apply the Article 101(3) self-assessment regime and decentralised enforcement under Regulation 1/2003
- LWM11-D2 - Private enforcement and the Damages Directive: Apply the Antitrust Damages Directive to a follow-on damages claim

## How to teach this
Move from prohibited conduct (A-B) to prohibited structural change (C) and then to how all of this is actually enforced (D) — ask the learner to explain, in one sentence each, what problem merger control and Regulation 1/2003 each solve that Articles 101/102 alone do not.

#### LWM11-C1 EU merger control: the SIEC test
**Council Regulation (EC) No 139/2004** (the "EU Merger Regulation" or EUMR) establishes a "one-stop shop" system: concentrations — mergers, acquisitions of control, certain full-function joint ventures — meeting specified EU-wide and Member-State turnover thresholds must be notified to the European Commission **before** implementation, and cannot be completed until cleared (the "standstill obligation," Article 7). The substantive test asks whether the concentration would **significantly impede effective competition (SIEC)** in the internal market or a substantial part of it, in particular through the creation or strengthening of a dominant position (Article 2(3)). This replaced the pre-2004 "dominance test" specifically to capture non-collusive "unilateral effects" in oligopolistic markets that could significantly impede competition without any single firm reaching a classic dominance threshold.

#### LWM11-C2 Joint ventures and jurisdictional thresholds
Full-function joint ventures — operating on a lasting basis, performing all the functions of an autonomous economic entity — are treated as concentrations under the EUMR and assessed under the SIEC test, though joint ventures with coordinative effects between the parent companies are also assessed for compatibility with Article 101 principles. Critical angle: evaluate the "one-stop shop" principle's efficiency benefits, avoiding multiple, potentially conflicting national merger reviews for genuinely EU-scale transactions, against its jurisdictional cliff-edge problems — transactions falling just below EUMR thresholds can still require multiple separate national merger filings across Member States, and, post-Brexit, separately to the UK CMA, reintroducing exactly the fragmentation the EUMR was designed to avoid for smaller cross-border deals.

#### LWM11-D1 Regulation 1/2003: modernisation and self-assessment
**Council Regulation (EC) No 1/2003** modernised the enforcement of Articles 101 and 102, most significantly by **abolishing the prior notification/exemption system** for Article 101(3) — previously, undertakings had to notify agreements to the Commission to obtain an individual exemption. Since 2004, Article 101(3) operates as a directly applicable legal exception that undertakings must self-assess, a fundamental shift in compliance burden onto businesses and their advisers. The Regulation empowers the Commission (investigative powers including dawn raids under Article 20, fines up to 10% of worldwide turnover) alongside **national competition authorities and national courts**, which now have full power to apply Articles 101 and 102 directly, creating a genuinely decentralised, network-based enforcement system (the European Competition Network) rather than exclusive centralisation in Brussels.

#### LWM11-D2 Private enforcement and the Damages Directive
Beyond public enforcement, the **Antitrust Damages Directive** (2014/104/EU) facilitates private actions for damages by parties harmed by competition law infringements, such as cartel victims, addressing disclosure of evidence, limitation periods, and the effect of a prior Commission infringement decision as binding proof of the infringement in follow-on damages claims. Critical angle: evaluate whether decentralised enforcement — national courts and authorities applying EU competition law directly — has genuinely improved enforcement effectiveness and access to justice for harmed parties, or risks inconsistent application of EU competition law across many different national legal systems, and consider how post-Brexit UK enforcement, now entirely domestic and coordinated with the EU only informally, fits into or diverges from this network.
