# LWM83_Advanced_Contract_Law_2 - Lesson: Advanced Contract Law B-D — Agency, sale of goods and insurance

## Goal
The learner applies agency, sale of goods and insurance doctrine at Master's depth, with critical evaluation of the underlying doctrinal debates.

## Syllabus items taught here
- LWM83-B1 - Creation of agency and actual/apparent authority: Apply Freeman & Lockyer and Armagas to a disputed-authority problem and evaluate the estoppel/independent-power debate
- LWM83-B2 - Agent's duties, undisclosed principal and termination: Apply an agent's fiduciary duties, the undisclosed principal doctrine and the Commercial Agents Regulations 1993
- LWM83-C1 - Sale of goods implied terms at advanced level: Critically apply ss.12-15 SGA 1979 to a commercial dispute, including the s.15A slight-breach limitation
- LWM83-C2 - Passing of property, risk and nemo dat exceptions: Apply ss.16-20 and the nemo dat exceptions to a disputed-title problem at advanced level
- LWM83-D1 - Utmost good faith and the Insurance Act 2015: Evaluate the Insurance Act 2015 reform from all-or-nothing avoidance to proportionate remedies
- LWM83-D2 - Insurable interest, indemnity and subrogation: Apply insurable interest, the indemnity principle and subrogation to an insurance claim

## How to teach this
Agency law governs when Party A (the agent) can bind Party B (the principal) in a legal relationship with Party C (the third party), even though B never dealt with C directly — hold this structure in mind throughout sub-module B, then move to the SGA 1979 framework already met at LLB level (L5-Commercial-1/2/3) but treated here more critically, and finally insurance.

#### LWM83-B1 Creation of agency and actual/apparent authority
Agency is created by express agreement, by conduct, or by **apparent (ostensible) authority** — where the principal's own representations lead a third party reasonably to believe the agent has authority, even if actual authority was withdrawn or never granted (*Freeman & Lockyer v Buckhurst Park Properties (Mangal) Ltd* [1964] 2 QB 480). An agent acting *within* actual authority binds the principal regardless of what the third party knew; an agent acting *without* actual authority but *within* apparent authority also binds the principal, though the principal then has a claim against the agent for breach of duty. *Armagas Ltd v Mundogas SA* [1986] AC 717 marks the limits of apparent authority where the third party's own counterpart agent is dishonestly colluding. Master's-level framing: is apparent authority best explained as an estoppel doctrine (the principal is estopped from denying authority) or as a genuinely independent source of legal power in the agent — the answer matters for remedies and for how the doctrine interacts with company law's "indoor management rule" on directors' authority.

#### LWM83-B2 Agent's duties, undisclosed principal and termination
An agent owes **fiduciary duties** to the principal: loyalty, no secret profit, no conflict of interest. The **undisclosed principal doctrine**, a genuinely distinctive feature of English and Commonwealth law, allows a third party who contracts with an agent not knowing of the principal's existence nonetheless, in some circumstances, to be sued by, or to sue, the undisclosed principal directly. Termination occurs by performance, agreement, or revocation, but for **commercial agents** specifically, the **Commercial Agents (Council Directive) Regulations 1993** override the common law default and can require compensation or an indemnity on termination — a genuinely EU-derived layer of mandatory protection still in force post-Brexit, and a frequent trap for commercial parties who assume they can simply terminate at will.

#### LWM83-C1 Sale of goods implied terms at advanced level
The Sale of Goods Act 1979, now overlapping significantly with the Consumer Rights Act 2015 for consumer transactions, implies: **s.12** right to sell/title, **s.13** correspondence with description, **s.14(2)** satisfactory quality, **s.14(3)** fitness for particular purpose, **s.15** correspondence with sample. At Master's level, the examinable edge is **s.15A**: for a non-consumer buyer, a breach so slight that rejection would be unreasonable is treated as a breach of warranty only, not a condition — a statutory override of the ordinarily strict condition/warranty classification, and a genuinely contested point in commercial practice where buyers routinely attempt rejection for technical, low-materiality breaches.

#### LWM83-C2 Passing of property, risk and nemo dat exceptions
Property passes when the parties intend it to (s.17), subject to the s.18 default rules for unascertained and specific goods; risk generally passes with property (s.20) unless agreed otherwise — a point of real importance in the export sales context met again in LWM36-A1, where FOB and CIF terms displace these default rules by trade custom. The **nemo dat** default (you cannot pass better title than you have) is qualified by commercially vital exceptions: sale by a mercantile agent (Factors Act 1889 s.2), sale under a voidable title before rescission (SGA s.23; *Car & Universal Finance Co Ltd v Caldwell* [1965] 1 QB 525), and sale by a buyer in possession (s.25) or seller in possession after sale (s.24). Critical angle: evaluate whether this 19th-century statutory patchwork is fit for modern high-volume, often digital or dematerialised, commercial sales.

#### LWM83-D1 Utmost good faith and the Insurance Act 2015
Insurance contracts were historically governed by *uberrimae fidei* (utmost good faith), requiring the insured to disclose all material facts even without being asked, with non-disclosure entitling the insurer to avoid the policy entirely regardless of proportionality. The **Insurance Act 2015** substantially reformed this for non-consumer (business) insurance, replacing the old all-or-nothing avoidance regime with a duty of "fair presentation of the risk" and **proportionate remedies** for breach, tied to what the insurer would actually have done had proper disclosure been made (charging a higher premium, adding a term, or declining the risk entirely). Critical angle: this shift mirrors the wider discomfort seen in *Cavendish*'s proportionality reasoning (LWM83-A2) with harsh, disproportionate secondary consequences for breach — a genuine cross-sub-module synthesis point about a broader theme in contemporary English commercial law reform.

#### LWM83-D2 Insurable interest, indemnity and subrogation
The insured must have a genuine, legally recognised **insurable interest** in the subject matter at the relevant time (for life insurance, at the time the policy is taken out; for indemnity/property insurance, generally at the time of loss), rooted in the historic Life Assurance Act 1774 and Marine Insurance Act 1906, aimed at preventing insurance being used as a wagering device. The **indemnity principle** puts the insured back in their pre-loss position, no better, preventing profit from insurance. **Subrogation** lets the insurer, having indemnified the insured, step into the insured's shoes to pursue any claim the insured has against a third party responsible for the loss. Third-party claimants generally have no direct right against a liability insurer at common law (privity), though the Third Parties (Rights against Insurers) Act 2010 now allows direct claims where the insured is insolvent.
