# LWM36_International_Trade_Law_2 - Lesson: International Trade Law C-D — Documentary credits and the CISG

## Goal
The learner applies the autonomy principle in documentary credits and the CISG's fundamental breach remedy scheme, comparing it critically with English law.

## Syllabus items taught here
- LWM36-C1 - Documentary credits and the autonomy principle: Apply the autonomy/independence principle from Hamzeh Malas to a documentary credit dispute
- LWM36-C2 - The fraud exception and standby credits: Apply the narrow fraud exception from United City Merchants and distinguish standby credits
- LWM36-D1 - CISG scope and Article 7 interpretation: Apply CISG Article 1 scope and the Article 7 uniform-interpretation mandate, noting UK non-ratification
- LWM36-D2 - CISG obligations, fundamental breach and remedies: Compare the CISG fundamental-breach threshold with English law's conditions/warranties/innominate-terms approach

## How to teach this
Continue the same connected transaction: having sold (A) and shipped (B) the goods, sub-module C covers how the seller actually gets paid, and sub-module D introduces an entirely separate international sales-law regime that the UK has deliberately never joined, for comparison with everything taught in sub-module A and at LLB level.

#### LWM36-C1 Documentary credits and the autonomy principle
In an international sale, seller and buyer are strangers, often in different legal systems, and neither wants to perform first without assurance of payment or delivery. A **documentary credit** (letter of credit) solves this by substituting the buyer's bank's independent payment promise for the buyer's own promise: the issuing bank undertakes to pay the seller (beneficiary) on presentation of documents that conform strictly to the credit's terms, regardless of disputes about the underlying sale contract. The doctrinal heart of this sub-module is the **autonomy (independence) principle**: the bank's obligation to pay is independent of, and unaffected by, disputes under the underlying sale contract — a bank must pay on strictly conforming documents even if the buyer alleges the goods themselves are defective (*Hamzeh Malas & Sons v British Imex Industries Ltd* [1958] 2 QB 127).

#### LWM36-C2 The fraud exception and standby credits
The one recognised exception to autonomy is clear evidence of **fraud** in the documents — the seller/beneficiary knew the documents were false, or the underlying transaction was a sham — deliberately kept narrow to protect the commercial utility of the credit mechanism (*United City Merchants (Investments) Ltd v Royal Bank of Canada (The American Accord)* [1983] 1 AC 168). A **standby letter of credit** functions more like a guarantee, payable on presentation of a simple demand or default certificate rather than shipping documents, and is used across a much broader range of transactions, including construction and performance bonds, beyond goods sales. Critical angle: evaluate the tension at the core of documentary credit law — strict compliance protects the bank, which must be able to check documents mechanically without investigating facts, and protects the seller, who is assured of payment on compliant documents, but can seem harsh to a buyer facing a seller who has technically complied on paper while shipping defective or non-conforming goods. Is the fraud exception's narrowness the correct trade-off?

#### LWM36-D1 CISG scope and Article 7 interpretation
The **United Nations Convention on Contracts for the International Sale of Goods (CISG)** applies, subject to opt-out, to contracts for the sale of goods between parties whose places of business are in different Contracting States (Article 1), avoiding the need to govern international sales by any single national sale-of-goods law with all its idiosyncrasies, through a uniform, internationally-negotiated regime. Importantly, **the UK has never ratified the CISG** — a genuinely important fact for Master's-level comparison, meaning English-law export sales (LWM36-A1) exist in deliberate contrast to, not under, this Convention. Article 7(1) requires that in interpreting the Convention, regard is to be had to its international character and the need to promote uniformity in application — a strikingly different interpretive instruction from the common law's usual domestically-anchored, precedent-based method; Article 7(2) directs gaps to be filled by the Convention's own general principles, not by reference to any single domestic legal system.

#### LWM36-D2 CISG obligations, fundamental breach and remedies
The CISG imposes obligations on the seller (to deliver goods of the required quantity, quality and description, Articles 30-34) and the buyer (to pay the price and take delivery, Articles 53-60), and provides a structured remedy scheme organised around **fundamental breach** (Article 25 — a breach is fundamental if it results in such detriment as substantially to deprive the other party of what they were entitled to expect, unless the breaching party did not foresee and a reasonable person would not have foreseen that result). Only a fundamental breach permits avoidance (termination) of the contract (Articles 49/64); lesser breaches are remedied by damages, price reduction, or requiring cure. Critical angle, the module's central comparative question: compare the CISG's fundamental-breach threshold for termination with English law's more fragmented approach — conditions versus warranties versus innominate terms (*Hong Kong Fir Shipping Co v Kawasaki Kisen Kaisha* [1962] 2 QB 26). Does the CISG's single, functional test produce more predictable or more uncertain outcomes than English law's categorical approach? Also evaluate the CISG's Article 7(1) good-faith-adjacent interpretive principle against English law's continuing resistance to a general good-faith doctrine (LWM83-A1) — a genuine, examinable point of doctrinal contrast.
