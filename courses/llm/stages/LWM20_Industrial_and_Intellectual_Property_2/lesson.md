# LWM20_Industrial_and_Intellectual_Property_2 - Lesson: Industrial and Intellectual Property C-D — Trade marks, passing off and integrated theory

## Goal

## How to teach this
Sub-module D deliberately revisits A-C at a theoretical level, so treat it as a synthesis point rather than new doctrine — ask the learner, for each earlier topic, "which theoretical justification best explains this rule?"

#### LWM20-C1 Registered trade marks: registrability and grounds for refusal
Under the **Trade Marks Act 1994** (historically implementing the EU Trade Marks Directive), a sign is registrable if capable of distinguishing the goods/services of one undertaking from another and capable of clear representation (s.1), subject to **absolute grounds for refusal** (s.3: descriptive, non-distinctive, or generic marks, and marks contrary to public policy) and **relative grounds** (s.5: conflict with an earlier registered mark or earlier unregistered right protectable by passing off).

#### LWM20-C2 Trade mark infringement and the average consumer
Infringement under s.10 turns heavily on the likelihood of confusion, assessed from the perspective of the **average consumer** — reasonably well-informed, observant and circumspect, but who rarely has the chance to make a direct comparison and must rely on imperfect recollection. This test derives from CJEU jurisprudence, notably *Lloyd Schuhfabrik Meyer & Co GmbH v Klijsen Handel BV* (C-342/97), and remains highly influential post-Brexit even though not strictly binding on UK courts.

#### LWM20-C3 Passing off
The common law tort of **passing off** protects unregistered goodwill through the "classic trinity" from *Reckitt & Colman Products Ltd v Borden Inc* (the "Jif Lemon" case) [1990] 1 WLR 491: (1) goodwill attached to goods or services, (2) misrepresentation leading the public to believe the defendant's goods or services are the claimant's, and (3) damage to goodwill resulting from that misrepresentation. Critical angle: passing off is often described as filling gaps left by registered trade mark law, including, in some cases, celebrity endorsement/character merchandising (*Fenty v Arcadia Group Brands Ltd (Rihanna/Topshop)* [2015] EWCA Civ 3) — evaluate whether extending passing off into personality/image rights is a principled development of the misrepresentation requirement, or judicial overreach into territory Parliament has never legislated for, unlike jurisdictions with a distinct statutory image right.

#### LWM20-D1 Theoretical justifications for IP protection
Master's-level IP study expects fluency in the competing theoretical accounts of why IP protection should exist at all: **utilitarian/incentive theory** (IP rights incentivise costly investment in innovation/creation that would otherwise be under-supplied given information's public-good, non-rivalrous nature — the dominant justification in UK/US law and policy); **natural rights/Lockean labour theory** (a person acquires property rights in what they create through their labour); **personality theory** (more prominent in continental European, especially German and French, copyright thinking, treating creative works as an extension of the author's personality, justifying strong moral rights); and **social planning/cultural theory** (IP should be shaped to produce a desirable cultural/information ecosystem, not merely to reward creators).

#### LWM20-D2 IP enforcement and remedies
Civil remedies (injunctions, damages/account of profits, delivery up/destruction) sit alongside criminal offences for the most serious infringements — for example CDPA 1988 s.107 for copyright and Trade Marks Act 1994 s.92 for counterfeiting — reflecting Parliament's judgment that some IP infringement, particularly commercial-scale counterfeiting, merits criminal sanction, not merely civil compensation.

#### LWM20-D3 UK design law post-Brexit
UK design law offers a genuinely complex, overlapping patchwork: UK registered designs, UK unregistered design right (CDPA 1988 Part III, a sui generis UK-only right protecting the shape/configuration of functional articles), and, until the end of the Brexit transition period, EU-wide registered and unregistered Community designs, now replaced for the UK by a domestic "supplementary unregistered design" right. Critical angle: evaluate the post-Brexit landscape — has losing automatic access to EU-wide unregistered Community design protection created a genuine gap for UK designers whose work is often first disclosed at EU trade shows, or has the domestic supplementary unregistered design right adequately filled it?
