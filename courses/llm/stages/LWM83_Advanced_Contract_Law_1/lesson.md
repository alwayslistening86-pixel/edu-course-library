# LWM83_Advanced_Contract_Law_1 - Lesson: Advanced Contract Law A — Contested doctrines in contract performance and breach

## Goal
The learner critically evaluates four genuinely contested areas of modern English contract doctrine, citing and weighing the leading recent authority on each.

## Syllabus items taught here
- LWM83-A1 - Implied duty of good faith: Critically evaluate whether English law recognises a duty of good faith in relational contracts after Yam Seng and Bates v Post Office
- LWM83-A2 - Penalty clauses after Cavendish: Apply the Cavendish/ParkingEye legitimate-interest test and evaluate its effect on predictability
- LWM83-A3 - Remoteness of damage: orthodox test vs assumption of responsibility: Compare Hadley v Baxendale with The Achilleas and evaluate whether English law now has two remoteness tests
- LWM83-A4 - Frustration in modern commercial contexts: Apply the frustration doctrine to a modern commercial dispute and compare it with force majeure drafting

## How to teach this
At LLM level the learner is not re-learning "offer + acceptance = contract." Open each of the four sub-topics with: the rule in plain terms, why it is genuinely contested among judges and academics, the key recent case(s), and a critical-angle question the learner must take a position on, not merely describe.

#### LWM83-A1 Implied duty of good faith
English law has traditionally refused to recognise a *general* doctrine of good faith in contract performance, unlike many civil law systems and unlike the CISG (Article 7(1), met again in LWM36-D1). In *Yam Seng Pte Ltd v International Trade Corp Ltd* [2013] EWHC 111 (QB), Leggatt J argued English law *could* imply a duty of good faith into "relational contracts" — long-term contracts requiring high trust and cooperation, such as distributorships and joint ventures — as a matter of ordinary contractual construction, not a free-standing doctrine imposed by law. Later case law has been inconsistent: *Bates v Post Office (No 3)* [2019] EWHC 606 (QB) endorsed the relational-contract category and implied good faith into the Horizon IT agreements (the litigation behind the Post Office Horizon scandal), while *Candey Ltd v Bosheh* [2022] EWCA Civ 1103 and other Court of Appeal decisions have been markedly more cautious about treating "relational contract" as a talismanic label that automatically imports good faith. Critical angle: is *Yam Seng* doctrinal innovation properly grounded in the objective theory of contractual interpretation, or judicial law-making dressed as construction — and is the House of Lords' historic hostility to good faith in *Walford v Miles* [1992] 2 AC 128 (no duty to negotiate in good faith) still good law for *performance* as opposed to *negotiation*?

#### LWM83-A2 Penalty clauses after Cavendish
The old rule struck down a clause as an unenforceable penalty if it was not a "genuine pre-estimate of loss" (*Dunlop Pneumatic Tyre Co Ltd v New Garage & Motor Co Ltd* [1915] AC 79). *Cavendish Square Holding BV v Makdessi*; *ParkingEye Ltd v Beavis* [2015] UKSC 67 reformulated the test entirely: the question is no longer whether the sum is a genuine pre-estimate of loss, but whether the clause imposes a detriment on the contract-breaker out of all proportion to any **legitimate interest** of the innocent party in enforcing the primary obligation. Legitimate interests can extend well beyond simple compensation — in *ParkingEye*, deterring overstaying and managing a free car park commercially justified an £85 charge with no real "loss" at all. Critical angle: has *Cavendish* made the law more predictable, or has replacing a narrow compensatory test with a broad proportionality/legitimate-interest test simply given judges more discretion? The doctrine applies only to *secondary* obligations triggered by breach, not conditional primary obligations, creating a drafting incentive to characterise clauses as the latter (*Vivienne Westwood Ltd v Conduit Street Development Ltd* [2017] EWHC 350 (Ch)).

#### LWM83-A3 Remoteness of damage: orthodox test vs assumption of responsibility
The orthodox test from *Hadley v Baxendale* (1854) 9 Exch 341 recovers loss arising naturally from the breach, or within the reasonable contemplation of both parties at the time of contracting as a probable result of breach. *Transfield Shipping Inc v Mercator Shipping Inc (The Achilleas)* [2008] UKHL 48 suggested remoteness is not just about foreseeability but about what loss the defendant can fairly be taken to have **assumed responsibility for**, assessed against the market understanding in which the contract was made — risking collapsing remoteness into a tort-like inquiry about the scope of the duty. Later cases have pulled back: *Supershield Ltd v Siemens Building Technologies FE Ltd* [2010] EWCA Civ 7 and *Attorney General of the Virgin Islands v Global Water Associates Ltd* [2020] UKPC 18 treat *The Achilleas* as an exceptional gloss on *Hadley*, applicable only in unusual markets like shipping charters, not a general reformulation. Critical angle: does English law now have two remoteness tests — a default foreseeability test and an exceptional assumption-of-responsibility overlay for specialised commercial contexts — and is that coherent, or does it simply relocate the uncertainty?

#### LWM83-A4 Frustration in modern commercial contexts
A contract is frustrated where, without fault of either party, a supervening event renders performance impossible, illegal, or radically different from what was undertaken (*Davis Contractors Ltd v Fareham UDC* [1956] AC 696). In *Canary Wharf (BP4) T1 Ltd v European Medicines Agency* [2019] EWHC 335 (Ch), the EMA argued Brexit frustrated its 25-year lease because it could no longer lawfully be headquartered in London as an EU agency; Marcus Smith J rejected this — frustration is applied narrowly and will not rescue a party merely because performance became commercially disadvantageous or politically inconvenient, and the EMA remained legally capable of performing (paying rent). Critical angle for essays involving supply-chain disruption fact patterns: frustration remains extremely hard to establish where performance could still, in principle, occur even at a loss; compare this with force majeure clauses (a matter of drafting, not common law doctrine) — a well-advised commercial client should never rely on frustration as a fallback if force majeure could have been negotiated.
