# LWM11_European_Union_Competition_Law_1 - Lesson: EU Competition Law A-B — Anti-competitive agreements and abuse of dominance

## Goal
The learner applies Articles 101 and 102 TFEU at Master's depth, engaging critically with the object/effect distinction and the as-efficient-competitor test.

## Syllabus items taught here
- LWM11-A1 - Article 101 TFEU: agreements and object vs effect: Apply Article 101(1)-(3) and the object/effect distinction, evaluating Cartes Bancaires
- LWM11-A2 - Vertical agreements, block exemptions and cartels: Apply the vertical block exemption safe harbour and the cartel leniency programme
- LWM11-B1 - Article 102 TFEU: dominance: Apply the Hoffmann-La Roche dominance test and identify categories of abuse
- LWM11-B2 - The as-efficient-competitor test and post-Brexit relevance: Evaluate the Post Danmark II/Intel effects-based approach and its status in UK CMA practice

## How to teach this
Fixed structure throughout: legal text, the economic idea behind it, leading CJEU/General Court case, critical evaluation, and a note on post-Brexit relevance, since this module studies EU law the UK no longer directly applies but that still shapes UK "retained EU law" competition doctrine via the Competition and Markets Authority (CMA).

#### LWM11-A1 Article 101 TFEU: agreements and object vs effect
**Article 101(1) TFEU** prohibits agreements between undertakings, decisions by associations of undertakings, and concerted practices which may affect trade between Member States and which have as their **object or effect** the prevention, restriction or distortion of competition within the internal market. Article 101(2) renders such agreements automatically void; **Article 101(3)** provides an exemption where the agreement improves production/distribution or promotes technical/economic progress, allows consumers a fair share of the benefit, and is indispensable without eliminating competition substantially. An agreement infringes Article 101(1) if it has **either** an anti-competitive object **or** effect — not cumulative requirements: if an agreement has an anti-competitive object (a restriction so obviously harmful its effects need not be examined, such as price-fixing, market-sharing or output limitation), no proof of actual anti-competitive effect is needed. Critical angle: *Groupement des Cartes Bancaires (CB) v Commission* (C-67/13 P) is the leading modern authority cautioning that the "object" category must be interpreted restrictively, applied only to agreements revealing, by their very nature, a sufficient degree of harm to competition — evaluate whether *CB* has meaningfully constrained Commission enforcement practice, or whether "object" restrictions remain, in practice, an easy shortcut to establishing infringement.

#### LWM11-A2 Vertical agreements, block exemptions and cartels
Not all agreements between different levels of the supply chain (manufacturer-distributor, licensor-licensee) are equally suspect: vertical agreements are generally assessed under a **block exemption regulation** (currently the Vertical Block Exemption Regulation), granting safe-harbour treatment below certain market-share thresholds, reflecting the economic view that vertical restraints are typically less harmful, and sometimes pro-competitive, than horizontal collusion. Horizontal price-fixing, market-sharing and bid-rigging **cartels** are treated as the most serious Article 101 infringements, attracting the Commission's highest fines, and since 2006 a **leniency programme** has incentivised cartel members to self-report in exchange for fine reductions or full immunity.

#### LWM11-B1 Article 102 TFEU: dominance
**Article 102 TFEU** prohibits any abuse by one or more undertakings of a **dominant position** within the internal market, or a substantial part of it, in so far as it may affect trade between Member States. Unlike Article 101, there is no exemption mechanism: either conduct is abusive or it is not, though objective justification and efficiency defences exist. **Dominance** is classically defined in *Hoffmann-La Roche & Co AG v Commission* (Case 85/76) as a position of economic strength enabling an undertaking to behave, to an appreciable extent, independently of competitors, customers and ultimately consumers. High market share is strong evidence of dominance but not conclusive by itself; the assessment also considers barriers to entry, countervailing buyer power, and the dynamism of the market.

#### LWM11-B2 The as-efficient-competitor test and post-Brexit relevance
The most doctrinally live area concerns how to assess whether pricing conduct — rebates, discounts, margin squeeze — by a dominant firm is abusive. *Post Danmark A/S v Konkurrencerådet (Post Danmark II)* (C-23/14) and *Intel Corp v Commission* (C-413/14 P) together mark a significant modernisation: the CJEU moved away from a rigid, formalistic approach (certain rebate categories treated as abusive "by object" almost regardless of actual economic effect) towards requiring the Commission, where the dominant undertaking submits evidence its conduct was incapable of restricting competition, to also analyse the actual or likely exclusionary effects — including whether an **equally efficient competitor** could have withstood the pricing practice. Critical angle: evaluate whether this "more economic approach" improves the accuracy and legitimacy of Article 102 enforcement, or makes enforcement excessively costly and unpredictable, since authorities must now run detailed economic analysis in cases that used to be resolved by categorisation alone. Post-Brexit relevance: the UK's Competition and Markets Authority continues to apply UK domestic competition law (Chapter II Competition Act 1998, modelled directly on Article 102), and, as "retained EU case law," pre-Brexit CJEU jurisprudence like *Intel* remains persuasive, though the UK Supreme Court and Court of Appeal now have greater freedom to depart from it.
