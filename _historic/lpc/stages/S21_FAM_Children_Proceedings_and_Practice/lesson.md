# S21_FAM_Children_Proceedings_and_Practice - Lesson: Elective, Family Law: private law children proceedings, the welfare principle, parental responsibility and practice points

## Goal
The learner applies the welfare principle and checklist, identifies who has parental responsibility, and advises appropriately on safeguarding and non-court dispute resolution.

## How to teach this
Ask: "Two parents disagree about where their child should live. What single factor is the court required to treat as more important than anything else on the statutory checklist?" Use the welfare principle as paramount to anchor this stage, then move through the checklist, parental responsibility, and finally the practical, client-facing side of family work.

#### FAM-5 Private law children proceedings and the welfare principle
Where parents cannot agree arrangements for their children, either can apply to the court for an order under the Children Act 1989. Historically this included residence and contact orders; the framework was later reformed into Child Arrangements Orders, which set out with whom a child is to live, spend time, or otherwise have contact, so again watch which terminology fits the era of a given fact pattern. The welfare principle, set out in Children Act 1989 s.1, provides that the child's welfare is the court's paramount consideration: not merely one factor among several to be weighed, but the single overriding consideration that determines the outcome of the case, to which every other consideration is subordinate.

#### FAM-6 The welfare checklist
The statutory welfare checklist sets out the factors the court must consider when applying the welfare principle, including the child's own ascertainable wishes and feelings, considered in light of their age and understanding; the child's physical, emotional and educational needs; the likely effect on the child of any change in their circumstances; the child's age, sex, background and any other characteristics the court considers relevant; any harm the child has suffered or is at risk of suffering; the capability of each parent, and of any other relevant person, to meet the child's needs; and the range of powers available to the court in the proceedings in question. Applying this checklist to specific facts, rather than reciting it, is what distinguishes a strong answer, since different factors will carry different weight depending on the child's age and the nature of the dispute.

#### FAM-7 Parental responsibility
Parental responsibility is the bundle of rights, duties, powers, responsibilities and authority a parent has in relation to a child and the child's property. A mother automatically has parental responsibility from birth. A father has it automatically if married to the mother at the time of the child's birth, or otherwise acquires it through specific routes, such as being registered as the father on the birth certificate under the applicable regime, entering into a formal parental responsibility agreement with the mother, or obtaining a court order granting it. Courts generally start from a presumption that the involvement of both parents in a child's life is in the child's welfare interests, unless there is evidence that such involvement would put the child at risk, which links directly to the safeguarding practice point covered next.

#### FAM-8 Practice points for family clients
Family clients are very often in genuine emotional crisis when they first instruct a solicitor, which links directly to the structured approach taught in the Interviewing and Advising skill: give realistic, non-adversarial advice wherever possible, and flag mediation or other forms of non-court dispute resolution where appropriate, since courts and the profession generally encourage resolving arrangements for children and finances without contested litigation wherever it is safe and sensible to do so. Always screen for domestic abuse and other safeguarding concerns early and sensitively, since this can change both the appropriate process, for example mediation may not be suitable at all where genuine abuse is in issue, and the substantive legal analysis, for example being directly relevant to the welfare checklist's "harm" factor, or, in exceptional cases, to conduct in financial remedy proceedings.
