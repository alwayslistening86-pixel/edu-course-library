# S14_Skills_Legal_Writing - Lesson: Course Skills: professional legal writing conventions

## Goal
The learner drafts an attendance note and a client letter using the correct structure and conventions, applies plain-English drafting rules, and matches register to the intended reader.

## Syllabus items taught here
- SK-LW-1 - Attendance note conventions: learn and apply: heading, body, action points, neutral third-person style
- SK-LW-2 - Client letter and email structure: learn and apply: the five-part client letter scaffold, headline-first structure
- SK-LW-3 - Plain-English drafting conventions: learn and apply: avoiding jargon, short sentences, active voice, no double negatives
- SK-LW-4 - Matching register to reader: learn and apply: adjusting tone and technicality for lay client, supervisor, or opposing solicitor

## How to teach this
Explain: "Research is worthless to a client or fee-earner if it is not communicated clearly. This stage teaches the specific document conventions solicitors use daily, and the discipline of writing for the actual reader in front of you rather than for yourself." Have the learner draft a short attendance note and a short client letter based on the same simple facts, then compare the two directly to see how structure and register change.

#### SK-LW-1 Attendance note conventions
An attendance note is an accurate, contemporaneous record of what happened in a meeting or call, kept for the file. The heading records the date, time, who was present, the method (in person, phone or video), and the matter reference. The body sets out what was discussed in a logical order, which is not necessarily the order it actually came up in conversation, covering the facts given, the advice given, and any instructions received from the client. Action points are clearly separated out at the end, stating who is doing what and by when. Style should stay factual, neutral and third person throughout: "the client seemed upset" is acceptable if genuinely relevant to instructions given, but "the client was being unreasonable" is not appropriate for a professional file note.

#### SK-LW-2 Client letter and email structure
A client letter or email exists to inform, advise or update a lay client in a way they can genuinely understand and act on. Use a reliable five-part scaffold: an opening that references the matter and briefly explains why you are writing; a summary of the position or advice given up front, since lay clients want the headline first and the detail after, which is the opposite of how academic legal writing is usually structured; an explanation of the reasoning in plain English, broken into short paragraphs or a numbered list; clearly flagged next steps, ideally as a short bulleted list, including anything the client needs to do and by when; and a sign-off explaining how to get in touch with any questions.

#### SK-LW-3 Plain-English drafting conventions
Avoid unexplained jargon: where a legal term is genuinely unavoidable, such as "completion" or "exchange of contracts," define it in plain terms the first time it is used. Keep sentences and paragraphs short, aiming for one idea per sentence and one theme per paragraph wherever possible. Prefer active voice over passive where it aids clarity, for example "we have reviewed the contract" rather than "the contract has been reviewed by us." Avoid double negatives and hedged, vague language, turning something like "it is not unlikely that..." into the clearer "it is likely that...". Use headings and numbered lists for anything with more than two or three distinct points, which helps every reader scan for the part relevant to them rather than parsing one dense block of prose.

#### SK-LW-4 Matching register to reader
To a lay client, use plain English throughout, minimal jargon, a headline-first structure, and a warm but professional tone. To a fee-earner or supervisor, for example in an attendance note or a research memo, more technical language is acceptable and expected, but structure and clarity still matter enormously, since a busy supervisor should be able to skim the conclusion and action points without reading the entire note in full. To the other side's solicitor in formal correspondence, be precise and professional without unnecessary hostility, and be careful about the distinction between "without prejudice" correspondence, which is protected from being shown to a court as an admission, and open correspondence, which is not.
