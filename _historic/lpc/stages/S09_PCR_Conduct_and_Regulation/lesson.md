# S09_PCR_Conduct_and_Regulation - Lesson: Professional Conduct and Regulation: Principles, conflicts, confidentiality, client care, AML and accounts

## Goal
The learner applies the SRA Principles and Code of Conduct, correctly classifies conflicts of interest, resolves confidentiality/disclosure clashes, explains client care and AML obligations, and applies basic solicitors' accounts rules.

## Syllabus items taught here
- PCR-1 - SRA Principles and Code of Conduct: learn and apply: the SRA Principles, the Code of Conduct, and their hierarchy
- PCR-2 - Conflicts of interest: learn and apply: own-interest conflict vs client conflict
- PCR-3 - Confidentiality and disclosure: learn and apply: duty of confidentiality and its clash with duty of disclosure
- PCR-4 - Client care and complaints handling: learn and apply: client care information, complaints procedure, the Legal Ombudsman
- PCR-5 - Anti-money laundering: learn and apply: client due diligence, suspicious activity reports, no tipping off
- PCR-6 - Solicitors' accounts basics: learn and apply: client account vs office account, no commingling, billing before transfer

## How to teach this
Ask: "Every other module teaches you what to do for a client. This one asks a different question: are you even allowed to act, and are you handling their money properly while you do? Why might precision matter more here than anywhere else on the course?" Use the answer (it is assessed entirely by MCQ, so exact triggers matter more than essay flair) to frame the whole stage.

#### PCR-1 SRA Principles and Code of Conduct
The SRA Principles are the overarching principles a solicitor must uphold at all times, including acting with integrity, acting in the best interests of each client, upholding the rule of law and the proper administration of justice, and maintaining the trust the public places in solicitors and in legal services generally. The SRA Code of Conduct then sets out more detailed conduct rules that sit beneath, and must be read consistently with, the Principles. A classic exam trap tests the hierarchy directly: where a specific Code rule and a Principle appear to conflict, the Principles take priority, since they represent the more fundamental, overarching obligation a solicitor owes.

#### PCR-2 Conflicts of interest
Distinguish two types of conflict carefully, since MCQs are designed to test whether you can tell them apart quickly. An own-interest conflict arises where the solicitor's own interest conflicts, or might conflict, with the client's interest, for example acting for a client in a transaction where the solicitor personally has a financial stake. A client conflict arises where a solicitor or firm acts for two or more clients whose interests conflict, or might conflict, and is generally prohibited unless a specific exception applies, such as the clients having a substantially common interest and appropriate safeguards and informed consent being in place. Rule of thumb: a solicitor personally benefiting points to an own-interest conflict; two clients on opposite, or potentially opposite, sides of the same matter points to a client conflict.

#### PCR-3 Confidentiality and disclosure
A solicitor owes a duty of confidentiality to every client, subject only to narrow exceptions, such as the client's own consent, or where disclosure is required or permitted by law, for example under anti-money-laundering reporting obligations. A related but distinct duty of disclosure requires a solicitor to disclose to a current client all information material to that client's own matter. These two duties can clash where information relevant to Client A is confidential to a different client, Client B: generally, the duty of confidentiality owed to Client B takes priority over the duty of disclosure owed to Client A, and if the resulting conflict cannot be properly managed, the firm cannot continue to act for both clients.

#### PCR-4 Client care and complaints handling
Client care information must be given to every client at the outset of a matter, covering who is dealing with the matter, how the firm's complaints procedure works, costs information, and how the client can challenge their bill if they think it is wrong. Firms must maintain a written complaints procedure and must tell clients, both at the outset and again if a complaint is not resolved internally, of their right to escalate an unresolved complaint to the Legal Ombudsman. Good client care is not merely good service; it is a specific, examinable regulatory obligation with defined minimum content that must be communicated clearly and in writing.

#### PCR-5 Anti-money laundering
Before acting, a solicitor must carry out client due diligence, verifying the client's identity using reliable, independent documentation, as part of the firm's anti-money-laundering compliance obligations. If a solicitor knows or suspects that a transaction involves money laundering, they must report this, typically to the firm's Money Laundering Reporting Officer, who assesses whether to make a Suspicious Activity Report to the National Crime Agency. A solicitor who has made, or is considering making, such a report must not "tip off" the client that a report has been or may be made, since doing so is itself a serious offence that can undermine the entire purpose of the reporting regime.

#### PCR-6 Solicitors' accounts basics
Client money, meaning money held for or on behalf of a client, must be kept in a separate client account, entirely distinct from the firm's own office account, which holds the firm's own money. Client money must never be mixed with the firm's own money, since commingling client and office money is treated as a serious regulatory breach. The basic accounting logic follows double-entry principles: money received on behalf of a client is credited to that client's own ledger within the client account, and the firm's own fees can only be transferred from the client account to the office account once a proper bill has been delivered to the client, not before. Even an accidental shortfall on client account is treated as a serious, reportable matter by the regulator, reflecting how central client money protection is to public trust in the profession.
