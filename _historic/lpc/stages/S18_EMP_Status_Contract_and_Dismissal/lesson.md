# S18_EMP_Status_Contract_and_Dismissal - Lesson: Elective, Employment Law: employment status, contract terms, wrongful dismissal and unfair dismissal

## Goal
The learner classifies employment status, identifies the sources of contract terms, and distinguishes wrongful dismissal from unfair dismissal including the fair-reason and reasonableness tests.

## Syllabus items taught here
- EMP-1 - Employment status: learn and apply: employee vs worker vs self-employed, the three-factor test
- EMP-2 - Terms of the contract of employment: learn and apply: express, implied and incorporated terms
- EMP-3 - Wrongful dismissal: learn and apply: the contractual claim for dismissal without proper notice
- EMP-4 - Unfair dismissal: learn and apply: qualifying period, potentially fair reasons, reasonableness and procedure

## How to teach this
Ask: "A business calls someone 'self-employed' in their paperwork, but controls exactly how, when and where they work, and will not let them send someone else to do the job instead. Does that label actually settle the question of their employment rights?" Use the answer (no, the tribunal looks behind the label) to open on employment status, since it gates every other right in this module.

#### EMP-1 Employment status
Distinguish an employee, who works under a contract of service and has the greatest statutory protection; a worker, who has some protections, such as minimum wage and paid holiday, but not full employee rights such as unfair dismissal protection; and a genuinely self-employed contractor, to whom essentially no employment-protection statute applies. This classification question is a favourite exam opener, since it gates which rights are even potentially in play for the rest of the answer. Courts and tribunals weigh three key factors: mutuality of obligation, meaning an ongoing obligation to offer and accept work; personal service, meaning whether the individual can send a substitute to do the work instead of doing it themselves; and control, meaning who directs how, when and where the work is actually done.

#### EMP-2 Terms of the contract of employment
Contract terms come from three sources. Express terms are those actually agreed, whether in writing or orally, including the particulars an employer must provide in a written statement within the statutory deadline after employment begins. Implied terms arise even without express agreement, including the mutual duty of trust and confidence owed by both employer and employee, and the employee's duty of fidelity, broadly a duty not to act against the employer's interests. Terms can also be incorporated from other documents, such as collective agreements negotiated with a trade union, or from staff handbooks and policies, where the contract expressly or by clear custom and practice incorporates them. Identifying which source a disputed term comes from is often the first step in resolving a contractual employment dispute.

#### EMP-3 Wrongful dismissal
Wrongful dismissal is a contractual claim, arising from a breach of the employment contract itself, most commonly a dismissal carried out without giving the employee their proper contractual notice period. Because it is a contract claim, damages are usually limited to what the employee would have earned during the notice period they should have been given, rather than any wider assessment of loss. Wrongful dismissal exists alongside, and is analytically distinct from, unfair dismissal: an employer can dismiss lawfully at contract law by giving full notice, and yet still act unfairly under the separate statutory unfair dismissal regime, and equally can breach the contract on notice while still having a fair, statutorily sound reason for the dismissal itself.

#### EMP-4 Unfair dismissal
Unfair dismissal is a statutory claim requiring the employee to have the qualifying period of continuous service, subject to specific exceptions where no qualifying period is needed at all, such as certain automatically unfair reasons including whistleblowing or pregnancy-related dismissals. The employer must show a potentially fair reason for the dismissal, drawn from the standard statutory categories: capability, conduct, redundancy, statutory illegality, or "some other substantial reason." Even where a potentially fair reason is shown, the tribunal then asks whether the employer acted reasonably in treating that reason as sufficient to dismiss in all the circumstances, including whether a fair procedure was followed, covering proper investigation, a genuine right for the employee to be heard, and a right of appeal, broadly following an ACAS-style process. The primary remedy is compensation, made up of a basic award and a compensatory award, with reinstatement or re-engagement available but rarely ordered in practice.
