# S16_CLIP_Commercial_Contracts_and_Trade_Marks - Lesson: Elective, Commercial Law and IP: commercial contracts, and trade marks and passing off

## Goal
The learner advises on sale of goods, agency, distribution and franchising arrangements, and applies the registered trade mark infringement test and the elements of passing off.

## Syllabus items taught here
- CLIP-1 - Sale of goods and retention of title: learn and apply: SGA 1979 implied terms, passing of risk and title, retention of title clauses
- CLIP-2 - Agency: learn and apply: actual vs apparent authority, agent's fiduciary duties
- CLIP-3 - Distribution and franchising: learn and apply: distinguishing distribution and franchising from agency; competition law issues
- CLIP-4 - Trade marks and passing off: learn and apply: registered trade mark infringement test and the passing-off elements

## How to teach this
Ask: "A manufacturer sells goods to a retailer through an intermediary, and later discovers a rival is using a confusingly similar brand name. How many different areas of commercial law does that one sentence actually touch?" Use the answer (sale of goods, agency or distribution, and trade marks) to introduce this stage's four items in order.

#### CLIP-1 Sale of goods and retention of title
Business-to-business sales of goods are governed largely by the Sale of Goods Act 1979, as amended, with separate consumer protection legislation applying to business-to-consumer sales. Key implied terms include that the goods must correspond with their description, be of satisfactory quality, and be fit for any particular purpose made known to the seller. Risk generally passes with property, meaning ownership, unless the parties agree otherwise, but commercial contracts frequently include a retention of title clause, under which the seller keeps ownership of the goods until paid in full, even after the goods have been delivered to the buyer. Retention of title is a key protective drafting tool for suppliers, particularly valuable if the buyer later becomes insolvent before paying.

#### CLIP-2 Agency
An agent has authority to bind their principal to contracts made with third parties. Actual authority, whether express, expressly given by the principal, or implied, arising from the agent's position or the circumstances, binds the principal in the ordinary way. Apparent, or ostensible, authority arises where the principal's own conduct leads a third party reasonably to believe the agent has authority to act, even where that authority has in fact been withdrawn internally; the principal can still be bound to the third party in that situation, since the third party's reasonable reliance is protected. Agents also owe fiduciary duties to their principal, including avoiding conflicts of interest, accounting for any profits made through the agency, and acting within their authority at all times.

#### CLIP-3 Distribution and franchising
A distribution agreement involves a distributor buying goods from the supplier and reselling them in its own name and at its own risk; distinguish this carefully from agency, where the agent never takes title to the goods at all and acts throughout on the principal's behalf. Franchising involves the franchisee operating under the franchisor's brand and business system, typically paying an initial fee plus ongoing royalties, under a detailed franchise agreement covering the licensing of intellectual property, territory, quality control standards, and termination provisions. Both arrangements raise live competition law issues that examiners like to flag, such as resale price maintenance clauses or overly restrictive exclusive territory provisions, which can render parts of an otherwise sensible commercial arrangement unenforceable.

#### CLIP-4 Trade marks and passing off
A registered trade mark, registered via the UK Intellectual Property Office, protects signs, such as words or logos, that distinguish one trader's goods or services from another's. Infringement broadly requires use of an identical or similar sign on identical or similar goods or services, causing a likelihood of confusion, or, for marks with an established reputation, taking unfair advantage of, or causing detriment to, that reputation even without confusion. Unregistered marks can still be protected through the common-law tort of passing off, which requires proof of three elements: goodwill built up in the mark or get-up, a misrepresentation by the defendant likely to lead the public to believe the defendant's goods or services are those of, or connected with, the claimant, and damage caused to the claimant's goodwill as a result.
