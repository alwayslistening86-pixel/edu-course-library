# S07_LIT_Criminal_PoliceStation_to_Bail - Lesson: Criminal litigation: the police station stage, interview, charge and bail

## Goal
The learner advises a suspect at the police station, explains the adverse inference risk, and applies the presumption of bail and grounds to displace it.

## Syllabus items taught here
- LIT-CR1 - Police station procedure: learn and apply: right to legal advice, PACE Code C, the detention clock
- LIT-CR2 - Interview and adverse inference: learn and apply: advising on silence, s.34 CJPOA 1994 adverse inference risk
- LIT-CR3 - Charge and bail: learn and apply: Bail Act 1976 presumption of bail and grounds to displace it

## How to teach this
Ask: "Your client has just been arrested and is about to be interviewed. What is the single most important right you need to make sure they understand before that interview starts?" Build out the police station stage from there, then move to what happens once the police decide to charge.

#### LIT-CR1 Police station procedure
A person detained at a police station has the right to free and independent legal advice, under PACE 1984 and its accompanying Codes of Practice, and this right should be positively exercised, not merely mentioned. PACE Code C governs detention, treatment and questioning in detail. The basic detention clock allows an initial 24 hours of detention without charge, extendable by a superintendent to 36 hours, and then, for indictable offences, by magistrates up to a maximum of 96 hours. Advise clients arriving at the police station to always ask for legal advice before any interview, since waiving it is rarely in the client's interest and can affect later evidential and inference issues.

#### LIT-CR2 Interview and adverse inference
A central part of advising a client before interview is deciding, with them, whether to answer questions, give a prepared statement, or remain silent, since each carries different risks. The key legal risk of silence is an adverse inference under the Criminal Justice and Public Order Act 1994 s.34: if a fact later relied on at trial was not mentioned when questioned under caution, and it would have been reasonable in the circumstances to mention it then, the court or jury may draw an adverse inference from that earlier silence. Advising on this requires weighing the strength of the evidence already held by police, the client's ability to explain themselves coherently under pressure, and whether a "no comment" interview realistically protects or harms the client's position at any eventual trial.

#### LIT-CR3 Charge and bail
After charge, a defendant is either bailed or remanded in custody pending the next stage. The Bail Act 1976 creates a presumption in favour of bail, which can be displaced on specific grounds, such as a real risk the defendant would abscond, commit further offences, or interfere with witnesses or otherwise obstruct the course of justice. The court weighs factors such as the nature and seriousness of the offence, the defendant's character and community ties, and their previous record when deciding whether the presumption is displaced. Conditions can be attached to bail, such as reporting to a police station, a curfew, or a residence condition, as a proportionate alternative to remand where an unconditional grant of bail is not appropriate.

## Explicitly not here
Classification of offences, trial process and sentencing follow in the next stage.
