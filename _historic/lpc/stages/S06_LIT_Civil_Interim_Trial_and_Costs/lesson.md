# S06_LIT_Civil_Interim_Trial_and_Costs - Lesson: Civil litigation: disclosure, interim applications, Part 36 offers, trial, costs and enforcement

## Goal
The learner explains the disclosure duty, applies the tests for summary judgment and interim injunctions, advises on Part 36 costs consequences, and explains costs and enforcement after judgment.

## Syllabus items taught here
- LIT-C5 - Disclosure: learn and apply: standard disclosure duty and its scope
- LIT-C6 - Interim applications: learn and apply: summary judgment and interim injunctions
- LIT-C7 - Part 36 offers: learn and apply: formal settlement offers and their costs consequences
- LIT-C8 - Trial, costs and enforcement: learn and apply: costs follow the event, enforcement methods against a judgment debtor

## How to teach this
Ask: "The claim has been issued and defended. Before trial, what tools does each side have to strengthen their position, end the case early, or protect themselves on costs?" Work through disclosure, interim applications and Part 36 as the answer, then finish with what actually happens at trial and after judgment.

#### LIT-C5 Disclosure
Standard disclosure requires each party to disclose documents on which it relies, and documents which adversely affect its own case, adversely affect another party's case, or support another party's case, following a reasonable and proportionate search. Disclosure is a continuing duty up to the end of the proceedings, not a one-off exercise at a fixed point, so a party who later finds a relevant document must disclose it even after the initial disclosure exercise is complete. Advise clients early that disclosure includes documents unhelpful to their own case, not only helpful ones, since this is a common misunderstanding and can cause serious professional conduct problems if a client tries to withhold an adverse document.

#### LIT-C6 Interim applications
Two interim applications come up repeatedly. Summary judgment lets a party ask the court to decide a claim or issue without a full trial, where the respondent has no real prospect of succeeding on the claim or defence, and there is no other compelling reason why the case should go to trial. An interim injunction restrains a party from doing something pending trial, and the court applies an American Cyanamid-style balancing test: is there a serious issue to be tried; would damages be an adequate remedy if the injunction were refused; and where does the balance of convenience lie between the parties if the injunction is granted or refused. Both applications are powerful but risky tools, since losing one can itself carry adverse costs consequences.

#### LIT-C7 Part 36 offers
A Part 36 offer is a formal settlement offer made under a specific procedural regime carrying automatic costs consequences if the offer is not beaten at trial. If a claimant fails to beat their own Part 36 offer, or a defendant's Part 36 offer is not beaten by the claimant at trial, the party who should have accepted the offer typically faces adverse costs and, in some circumstances, additional interest or an uplift. This creates real tactical pressure to make and respond to offers carefully throughout the litigation, and advising a client on the costs risk of rejecting a well-pitched Part 36 offer is a core, frequently examined skill.

#### LIT-C8 Trial, costs and enforcement
The general costs rule is that costs follow the event: the losing party usually pays a proportion of the winning party's reasonably and proportionately incurred costs, though the court retains discretion and can depart from this based on conduct, including compliance with pre-action protocols and Part 36 offers made along the way. If a judgment debtor still does not pay after judgment, enforcement methods include a warrant or writ of control, allowing goods to be seized and sold; a third party debt order, freezing and redirecting money a third party owes the debtor, such as a bank; a charging order, secured against the debtor's property; and an attachment of earnings order, deducting sums directly from the debtor's wages. Choice of enforcement method depends on what the debtor actually has and where it can be reached.
