# S20_FAM_Divorce_and_Financial_Remedies - Lesson: Elective, Family Law: divorce/dissolution procedure and financial remedies on relationship breakdown

## Goal
The learner explains divorce/dissolution procedure across eras, applies the financial remedies checklist and the needs/sharing/compensation approaches, and explains the clean break principle.

## Syllabus items taught here
- FAM-1 - Divorce and dissolution procedure: learn and apply: application, conditional order, final order, fault vs no-fault eras
- FAM-2 - Financial remedies: the statutory checklist: learn and apply: the section 25-style checklist factors and the court's discretion
- FAM-3 - Financial remedies: approaches and orders: learn and apply: needs, sharing and compensation approaches; lump sum, property adjustment, pension sharing, maintenance orders
- FAM-4 - The clean break principle: learn and apply: achieving financial independence between the parties where fair

## How to teach this
Ask: "A client's divorce papers use the word 'grounds' and refer to a 'decree nisi.' Does that tell you anything about which set of rules actually applies to their case?" Use the answer (yes, it signals the fault-based, pre-reform regime rather than the later no-fault one) to introduce the need to identify the correct era's rules before advising, then build out financial remedies on top of that.

#### FAM-1 Divorce and dissolution procedure
Divorce ends a marriage, and dissolution ends a civil partnership. Historically, ending the relationship required proving one of a set of specific facts or grounds; a later no-fault reform removed the need to attribute blame or prove any fact at all, replacing it with a simple statement of irretrievable breakdown. Always check which regime a given fact pattern sits under, since this is exactly the kind of "which era's rules apply" issue a historical course like this one should flag honestly rather than silently assume the current rule always applied. The broad procedural stages are an application, then a conditional order, historically called a decree nisi, then a final order, historically called a decree absolute, with a minimum statutory time period required between the stages. A divorce or dissolution order ends the marriage or civil partnership itself; it does not automatically resolve either financial matters or arrangements for children, which are separate processes covered by the remaining items in this elective.

#### FAM-2 Financial remedies: the statutory checklist
The court has wide discretion when making financial remedy orders on relationship breakdown, guided by a statutory checklist of factors. These include the welfare of any minor child of the family, treated as the first consideration; the income, earning capacity, property and other financial resources each party has or is likely to have; the financial needs and obligations each party has; the standard of living enjoyed by the family before the breakdown; the ages of the parties and the duration of the marriage; contributions each party has made, including non-financial contributions such as homemaking and childcare; and conduct, which is only taken into account in genuinely exceptional cases. Applying this checklist accurately to a given set of facts, rather than reciting it in the abstract, is what the module actually tests.

#### FAM-3 Financial remedies: approaches and orders
Courts commonly use three broad approaches when deciding financial remedy outcomes. Needs focuses on meeting each party's reasonable needs, especially housing needs, and is the most commonly applied approach in cases without substantial surplus assets. Sharing focuses on a fair division of the matrimonial assets, often starting from a yardstick of equality for longer marriages, particularly where assets exceed what is needed simply to meet both parties' needs. Compensation is comparatively rare, applying only for a genuine, provable economic disadvantage caused by the marriage itself, such as one party giving up a demonstrably lucrative career for the family. The available orders include lump sum orders, property adjustment orders, such as transferring the family home, pension sharing orders, and periodical payments, sometimes called spousal maintenance, which can be time-limited or, less commonly, open-ended.

#### FAM-4 The clean break principle
Courts are encouraged, wherever it is fair to do so, to achieve financial independence between the parties, avoiding ongoing financial ties such as open-ended periodical payments where a fairer, more final outcome, such as a lump sum or a time-limited maintenance order, can achieve the same result. This does not mean a clean break is always appropriate or fair; where one party genuinely cannot achieve financial independence in the near term, for example due to caring responsibilities for young children, ongoing periodical payments may remain the fairer outcome despite the general policy preference for finality. Advise clients that the clean break principle is a strong steer towards finality, not an absolute rule that overrides the needs-based and sharing-based analysis in every case.
