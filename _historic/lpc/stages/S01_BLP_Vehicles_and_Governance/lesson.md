# S01_BLP_Vehicles_and_Governance - Lesson: Business vehicles, company formation and internal decision-making

## Goal
The learner compares business vehicles, forms and constitutes a company, and correctly identifies who has power to make a given corporate decision and by what procedure.

## Syllabus items taught here
- BLP-1 - Choosing and comparing business vehicles: learn and apply: compare sole trader, partnership, LLP and private limited company on legal personality and liability
- BLP-2 - Company formation and constitutional documents: learn and apply: CA 2006 incorporation procedure, articles of association, PSC register
- BLP-3 - Board decision-making procedure: learn and apply: board meetings, quorum, board resolutions, written resolutions
- BLP-4 - Shareholder decision-making procedure: learn and apply: ordinary and special resolutions, general meetings, written resolutions
- BLP-5 - Directors' general duties (CA 2006 ss.171-177): learn and apply: the seven statutory directors' duties and spotting breaches

## How to teach this
Open with: "A client walks in wanting to start a business with a friend. Before you can advise on anything else, what is the very first question you need answered?" Guide the learner to see that vehicle choice is the gateway question everything else depends on, then work through the checklist below in order: vehicle, formation, board power, shareholder power, directors' duties.

#### BLP-1 Choosing and comparing business vehicles
Every BLP problem question starts with identifying the vehicle. A sole trader has no separate legal personality and unlimited personal liability; there is no incorporation statute, just ordinary contract and tort law. A partnership (Partnership Act 1890) also has no separate legal personality in England and Wales, and partners are jointly and severally liable for partnership debts. A limited liability partnership (LLPs Act 2000) does have separate legal personality, with liability generally limited to capital contributed, combining partnership flexibility with limited liability. A private limited company (Companies Act 2006) has separate legal personality and liability limited to unpaid share capital. Rule of thumb: no incorporation means no separate legal person, so the humans behind the business are personally on the hook for its debts. Always establish the vehicle before answering any other question in a fact pattern.

#### BLP-2 Company formation and constitutional documents
A private limited company is formed by delivering an application for registration to Companies House under CA 2006 s.9: a memorandum of association (a short, one-off statement that the subscribers wish to form the company and become members), proposed articles of association (if not adopting the Model Articles unamended), a statement of proposed officers, a statement of capital and initial shareholdings, a statement of compliance, and the registration fee. On registration, the registrar issues a certificate of incorporation, and the company acquires separate legal personality from that date. Companies must also keep a register of people with significant control (PSC register), identifying anyone holding more than 25% of shares or voting rights, or who otherwise exercises significant influence or control, and file this information at Companies House.

#### BLP-3 Board decision-making procedure
Day-to-day management is delegated to the board of directors (Model Articles, art. 3). Directors decide by board resolution: an ordinary majority vote at a validly convened, quorate board meeting, or by written resolution if the articles permit it. Check three things in any board-decision fact pattern: was the meeting properly called with the notice the articles require; was it quorate (the minimum number of directors the articles specify); and did the resolution pass by the required majority. A decision taken without a validly constituted board meeting, or by a director acting alone without authority, is vulnerable to challenge and may not bind the company.

#### BLP-4 Shareholder decision-making procedure
Some decisions are reserved to the shareholders rather than the board. Ordinary resolutions (a simple majority of those voting) cover matters such as removing a director (CA 2006 s.168) or approving certain related-party or substantial property transactions. Special resolutions (at least 75% of those voting) are needed for more fundamental changes, most importantly altering the articles of association (CA 2006 s.21). Shareholder decisions can be made at a general meeting or, for private companies, by written resolution circulated to all eligible members. Always check the articles for any variation on these statutory defaults, and confirm the resolution type matches what the decision actually requires.

#### BLP-5 Directors' general duties (CA 2006 ss.171-177)
Learn the seven statutory duties as a checklist, since exam problem questions describe a director doing something and ask which duty (if any) is breached: act within powers (s.171); promote the success of the company for the benefit of members as a whole (s.172); exercise independent judgment (s.173); exercise reasonable care, skill and diligence (s.174); avoid conflicts of interest (s.175); not accept benefits from third parties (s.176); and declare any interest in a proposed transaction or arrangement with the company (s.177). Worked example: a sole director personally guarantees a company loan and also takes a fee from the lender for introducing the deal. This raises a possible s.175 conflict unless authorised, a possible s.176 benefit from a third party unless it cannot reasonably be regarded as likely to give rise to a conflict, and requires the director to keep considering s.172 when structuring arrangements that also benefit them personally.
