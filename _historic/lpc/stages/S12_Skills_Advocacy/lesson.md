# S12_Skills_Advocacy - Lesson: Course Skills: oral advocacy on an unseen brief

## Goal
The learner structures and delivers a short oral submission, handles judicial interventions correctly, observes courtroom manner, and prepares an unseen brief efficiently under time pressure.

## Syllabus items taught here
- SK-ADV-1 - Structuring a short oral submission: learn and apply: introduction, stating the order sought, one-sentence case theory
- SK-ADV-2 - Ordering and supporting arguments: learn and apply: strength-first structure, state-support-explain for each point
- SK-ADV-3 - Handling judicial interventions: learn and apply: answering questions directly rather than ploughing on with a script
- SK-ADV-4 - Courtroom manner and etiquette: learn and apply: forms of address, referring to the other side, pace and manner
- SK-ADV-5 - Preparing an unseen brief under time pressure: learn and apply: the skeleton-note preparation method

## How to teach this
Explain: "Solicitor-advocates regularly appear at short hearings such as bail applications, case management hearings and interim applications; this skill trains structured, persuasive oral argument under time pressure, not deep substantive legal knowledge, though you must still get the applicable rule right." Give the learner an unseen short brief and have them prepare and deliver a two to three minute submission using the structure below.

#### SK-ADV-1 Structuring a short oral submission
Open by stating who you appear for and what application or submission you are making, for example: "I appear for the Claimant and make this application for summary judgment." State the order you are asking the court to make up front, since judges want the ask before the argument, not buried at the end. Follow this immediately with your case theory: one clear, memorable sentence the judge could repeat back, for example "this is a straightforward case of a contract broken and never remedied, and my client is entitled to the outstanding sum plus interest." Everything else in the submission should support and return to this single sentence.

#### SK-ADV-2 Ordering and supporting arguments
Take each point in order of strength, strongest first, unless procedural logic genuinely requires a different order. For each point, follow a consistent pattern: state it, support it with the relevant rule, authority or evidence, explain why it supports your case theory, then move on to the next point rather than lingering. Anticipate the obvious counter-argument and address it briefly rather than ignoring it, since ignoring an evident weakness in your case looks evasive to a judge and undermines your credibility on the points you do make well. A well-ordered submission of two to four points, each properly supported, is far stronger than a longer, unstructured list.

#### SK-ADV-3 Handling judicial interventions
If the judge interrupts with a question, stop your prepared script immediately and answer the question directly; this is the single biggest differentiator between weak and strong advocates. Ploughing on with a rehearsed script while ignoring a judge's question signals that you are not actually listening, and a judge's question is a direct signal of exactly what they need resolved before they can decide in your favour. If you genuinely do not know the answer, say so honestly rather than guessing, and offer to address it in writing or come back to it once you have checked, since a confident wrong answer damages your case far more than an honest "I will confirm that."

#### SK-ADV-4 Courtroom manner and etiquette
Stand where the format requires it, and address the judge using the correct form, such as "Your Honour," "Sir" or "Madam," or "Judge," checking which form the specific tribunal or fact pattern specifies rather than assuming. Refer to the other side as "my friend" if they are a solicitor, or "my learned friend" if the other advocate is a barrister, rather than by name, in formal proceedings, and never address the other advocate directly; always address the court instead. Speak at a measured, controlled pace, and pause after key points rather than rushing through the submission, since a rushed delivery undermines even a well-structured argument.

#### SK-ADV-5 Preparing an unseen brief under time pressure
Skim-read the whole brief once first, for the overall shape of the problem, before making any notes. Identify the order you want and your one-sentence case theory first, since everything else in your preparation is built to support these two things. Build a skeleton, bullet points only rather than full sentences, of two to four points in strength order, each with its supporting rule or fact noted alongside it. Finally, rehearse the opening and closing lines so they become automatic, the same technique used in the Interviewing and Advising skill, freeing your working memory during preparation and delivery for the substantive middle section, which is where the real skill is actually tested.
