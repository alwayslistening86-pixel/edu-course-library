# S05_LIT_Civil_PreAction_and_Starting_a_Claim - Lesson: Civil litigation: pre-action conduct, starting a claim, limitation and track allocation

## Goal
The learner applies pre-action protocol requirements, drafts and sequences statements of case correctly, identifies the applicable limitation period, and allocates a claim to the correct track.

## Syllabus items taught here
- LIT-C1 - Pre-action conduct and protocols: learn and apply: pre-action protocols, the Practice Direction on Pre-Action Conduct, costs sanctions
- LIT-C2 - Starting a claim and statements of case: learn and apply: claim form, particulars of claim, defence, statement of truth
- LIT-C3 - Limitation periods: learn and apply: standard limitation periods for contract, tort, personal injury and deeds
- LIT-C4 - Track allocation: learn and apply: small claims, fast track and multi-track allocation

## How to teach this
Ask: "Your client wants to sue someone tomorrow morning. Can they just issue a claim form straight away?" Use the answer (usually not, without first following the relevant pre-action protocol) to introduce the civil procedure sequence from before a claim is even issued through to how it is tracked once it is.

#### LIT-C1 Pre-action conduct and protocols
Before issuing proceedings, parties must generally comply with the relevant pre-action protocol, of which there are specific protocols for matters such as personal injury, construction and professional negligence, plus a general Practice Direction on Pre-Action Conduct covering claims with no specific protocol. Protocols typically require parties to exchange sufficient information about the claim, consider alternative dispute resolution, and try to settle before proceedings are issued. Failing to comply with the applicable protocol does not usually stop a claim being brought, but can lead to costs sanctions later in the litigation, even for the party who ultimately wins, so pre-action compliance should always be checked and advised on at the outset of any new instruction.

#### LIT-C2 Starting a claim and statements of case
A claim is started using a claim form (form N1), accompanied by, or followed shortly by, the Particulars of Claim, which set out the facts relied on, the cause or causes of action, and the remedy sought. The usual sequence of statements of case is Particulars of Claim, then the Defence, then, if needed, a Reply from the claimant. Every statement of case must be verified by a statement of truth, confirming the party believes the facts stated in it are true, and a false statement of truth can expose the signatory to contempt of court proceedings. Advise clients that statements of case fix the issues in the case; a party generally cannot run a case at trial that was never pleaded.

#### LIT-C3 Limitation periods
Limitation periods bar a claim if it is not issued in time, so this should be checked at the very start of any new instruction, not left until later. The standard periods under the Limitation Act 1980 are six years for a simple contract claim (s.5) and most torts (s.2), three years for personal injury claims (s.11), and twelve years for a claim under a deed (s.8). Time generally runs from the date the cause of action accrued, though special rules can extend this in some circumstances, such as for personal injury claims involving a later date of knowledge. Missing a limitation period is usually fatal to a claim, so flag it early in any advice rather than as an afterthought.

#### LIT-C4 Track allocation
Once a claim is defended, the court allocates it to a track based broadly on value and complexity. The small claims track suits lower-value, straightforward claims, is relatively informal, and costs are not generally recoverable by the winning party beyond fixed amounts. The fast track suits mid-value, reasonably straightforward claims, with a fixed trial length and limited disclosure and expert evidence. The multi-track suits higher-value or more complex cases, which the court case-manages individually, often through a series of directions hearings. Track allocation affects costs recovery, the procedural steps required, and how much court management the case receives, so identifying the likely track early helps set realistic client expectations about cost and timescale.
