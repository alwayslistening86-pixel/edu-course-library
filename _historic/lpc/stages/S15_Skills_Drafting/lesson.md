# S15_Skills_Drafting - Lesson: Course Skills: drafting operative legal documents

## Goal
The learner applies core drafting principles to produce precise operative clauses, and drafts a contractual clause, particulars of claim, a simple will clause, and a conveyancing special condition.

## Syllabus items taught here
- SK-DR-1 - Core drafting principles: learn and apply: precision, cutting redundant words, defined terms, logical structure
- SK-DR-2 - Operative clauses: learn and apply: who does what by when, and the consequence of non-performance
- SK-DR-3 - Drafting a contractual clause: learn and apply: drafting a termination or limitation of liability clause
- SK-DR-4 - Drafting particulars of claim: learn and apply: parties, chronological facts, legal basis, remedy sought
- SK-DR-5 - Drafting a simple will clause: learn and apply: executor appointment, specific legacy, residuary clauses
- SK-DR-6 - Drafting conveyancing special conditions: learn and apply: precise transaction-specific special conditions

## How to teach this
Explain: "Drafting is a distinct skill from legal writing: it is about producing operative documents that will actually be relied on, interpreted literally, and potentially fought over. Precision beats elegance every time." Practise the core principles first on a single clause, then apply them across the four practical document types the module actually tests.

#### SK-DR-1 Core drafting principles
Every word in a drafted document should be doing work: cut redundant legal doubling that adds nothing, such as "null and void" or "each and every," unless there is a specific reason for it, since some doubling is deliberate for legal certainty, such as "sole and exclusive." Ask, for every word, whether removing it changes the meaning; if not, cut it. Be equally deliberate about vague qualifiers a court would have to interpret, such as "reasonable," "promptly" or "substantial": only use them where you genuinely intend to leave flexibility, and recognise that doing so builds in real interpretive discretion rather than simply saving you the effort of being precise.

#### SK-DR-2 Operative clauses
Structure the document for clarity: capitalise and define terms used repeatedly, such as "the Property," "the Purchase Price" or "the Completion Date," at first use, then use the defined term consistently throughout, never switching between the defined term and a plain description of the same thing. Use numbered clauses and sub-clauses, consistently formatted, so any clause can be referred to precisely elsewhere in the document or later in correspondence or litigation, following a logical order with parties and definitions first, substantive obligations next, and boilerplate, such as governing law and notices, last. A good operative clause specifies who does what, by when, and what happens if they do not: a clause stating only an obligation, with no consequence for breach, is often weaker in practice than one that builds the consequence directly into the drafting, for example "the Seller shall deliver the goods by 1 March; if the Seller fails to do so, the Buyer may terminate this agreement by written notice and shall be entitled to a full refund of any sums paid."

#### SK-DR-3 Drafting a contractual clause
Apply the operative-clause template directly to common commercial clauses, such as a termination clause or a limitation of liability clause: identify precisely who must do what, by what deadline, and what remedy or consequence follows if they do not. Keep defined terms consistent with the rest of the document, and check cross-references to other clause numbers are accurate, since a wrong cross-reference is a classic, easily avoided drafting error that examiners specifically look for. Avoid ambiguous pronouns, such as "it" or "they," wherever it is unclear which party is actually meant, replacing them with the defined term instead.

#### SK-DR-4 Drafting particulars of claim
Particulars of Claim should state the parties, then set out the facts giving rise to the claim in chronological order, then the legal basis for the claim, then the remedy sought, each in short, numbered paragraphs that can be referred to precisely by number later in the litigation. Precision matters as much here as in a commercial clause: state exactly what happened and when, exactly which legal duty or term was breached, and exactly what the claimant wants the court to order, since vague or poorly sequenced Particulars of Claim make it harder for the defendant, and later the court, to understand and respond to the case being brought.

#### SK-DR-5 Drafting a simple will clause
Practise drafting an executor appointment clause, a specific legacy clause, and a residuary clause, each requiring precise, unambiguous identification of the person and the gift concerned. An executor appointment clause should name the executor clearly, ideally with an alternative in case the first choice cannot or will not act. A specific legacy clause must identify both the specific item being given and the intended beneficiary without ambiguity, since an unclear description can cause the gift to fail. A residuary clause disposes of everything not otherwise specifically given, and should be drafted to cover what happens if a named residuary beneficiary predeceases the testator, to avoid an unintended partial intestacy.

#### SK-DR-6 Drafting conveyancing special conditions
Special conditions in a conveyancing contract must be precise and tied to the specific facts of the transaction rather than generic boilerplate, for example a special condition requiring a specifically identified defect in title to be remedied, or a specifically named piece of documentation to be produced, before completion can take place. Apply the same core principles used throughout this stage: define any term used more than once, state clearly who must do what and by when, and build in the consequence if the condition is not satisfied by completion, such as a right to rescind or to withhold completion monies until the condition is met.
