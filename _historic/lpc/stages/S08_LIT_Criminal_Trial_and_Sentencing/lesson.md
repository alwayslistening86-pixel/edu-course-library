# S08_LIT_Criminal_Trial_and_Sentencing - Lesson: Criminal litigation: classification of offences, mode of trial, trial process and sentencing

## Goal
The learner classifies an offence and identifies the correct venue, explains the trial process and burden/standard of proof, and applies the basic sentencing framework.

## Syllabus items taught here
- LIT-CR4 - Classification of offences and mode of trial: learn and apply: summary, either-way and indictable offences; plea before venue and allocation
- LIT-CR5 - Trial process: learn and apply: Magistrates' and Crown Court process, burden and standard of proof
- LIT-CR6 - Sentencing basics: learn and apply: seriousness, aggravating/mitigating factors, range of disposals

## How to teach this
Ask: "Your client has been charged with an either-way offence. Who decides where this case is heard, and why would that choice matter to your client?" Use this to build out offence classification and venue, then trial process, then finish with what happens if the client is convicted.

#### LIT-CR4 Classification of offences and mode of trial
Offences fall into three classes. Summary-only offences can only be tried in the Magistrates' Court. Either-way offences can be tried in either the Magistrates' Court or the Crown Court, depending on a plea-before-venue and allocation procedure. Indictable-only offences, generally the most serious, such as serious violence, can only be tried in the Crown Court. For either-way offences, the defendant first indicates a plea; on an indication of guilty, the Magistrates proceed to sentence or commit for sentence if their powers are insufficient; on an indication of not guilty, the Magistrates decide, considering the Magistrates' Court's sentencing powers and the seriousness of the case, whether to accept jurisdiction, but even where they do, the defendant retains the right to elect trial by jury in the Crown Court instead.

#### LIT-CR5 Trial process
In the Magistrates' Court, trial is by lay magistrates, or a single District Judge, with no jury; the magistrates or judge decide both fact and law. In the Crown Court, trial for indictable and elected either-way offences is by judge and jury, with committal or sending procedures moving a case up from the Magistrates' Court once it is clear the Crown Court has jurisdiction. Throughout, the prosecution bears the burden of proving guilt, and the standard of proof is beyond reasonable doubt, sometimes phrased to a jury as being sure of guilt; the defence never has to prove innocence, only, at most, raise a genuine issue the prosecution must then disprove to that standard.

#### LIT-CR6 Sentencing basics
Sentencing follows a structured approach: the court first assesses the seriousness of the offence by reference to culpability, how blameworthy the offender's conduct was, and harm, the actual or intended harm caused, then adjusts for aggravating factors, such as planning or vulnerability of the victim, and mitigating factors, such as genuine remorse or a guilty plea entered promptly, and applies any relevant sentencing guideline for that offence. The range of available disposals runs, roughly in ascending order of seriousness, from an absolute or conditional discharge, through a fine, a community order, a suspended sentence, up to immediate custody for the most serious offending, and advising a client realistically on where their case is likely to sit on that scale is a core part of the criminal advocate's role.
