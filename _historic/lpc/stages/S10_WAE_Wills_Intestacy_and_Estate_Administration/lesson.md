# S10_WAE_Wills_Intestacy_and_Estate_Administration - Lesson: Wills and Administration of Estates: valid wills, intestacy, obtaining the grant, IHT and administration

## Goal
The learner tests will validity and revocation, applies the intestacy rules, identifies the correct grant, calculates basic inheritance tax reliefs, and sequences estate administration correctly.

## Syllabus items taught here
- WAE-1 - Will validity formalities and capacity: learn and apply: Wills Act 1837 s.9, testamentary capacity, knowledge and approval, undue influence
- WAE-2 - Revocation of wills: learn and apply: revocation by later will, destruction, and by marriage/civil partnership
- WAE-3 - Intestacy rules: learn and apply: the statutory intestacy hierarchy and the cohabitant trap
- WAE-4 - Obtaining the grant: learn and apply: grant of probate vs letters of administration
- WAE-5 - Inheritance tax on death: learn and apply: nil-rate band, spouse and charity exemptions, transferable and residence nil-rate bands
- WAE-6 - Administering the estate: learn and apply: the personal representative's five-step sequence and duty of care

## How to teach this
Ask: "A client dies leaving a will their family says was signed on their deathbed with only one witness present. What is the very first thing you must check, and what happens to the estate if that check fails?" Use the answer (formal validity under the Wills Act 1837, and intestacy if it fails) to move through the module in the order a practitioner would actually face it: is there a valid will, if not what happens instead, how is the grant obtained, what tax is due, and how is the estate then administered.

#### WAE-1 Will validity formalities and capacity
Under the Wills Act 1837 s.9, a will is valid only if it is in writing and signed by the testator, or by someone else in their presence and at their direction; it appears the testator intended, by that signature, to give effect to the will; the signature is made or acknowledged by the testator in the presence of two witnesses present at the same time; and each witness then either attests and signs the will, or acknowledges their own earlier signature, in the testator's presence. Beyond these formalities, the testator needs testamentary capacity, meaning they understood the nature of the act and its effects, the extent of the property being disposed of, and the claims they ought to give effect to, without being affected by any disorder of mind that poisoned their affections or judgment. The testator must also have known and approved the contents of the will, and the will must be free of undue influence, meaning genuine coercion rather than mere persuasion.

#### WAE-2 Revocation of wills
A will can be revoked in several recognised ways: by a later valid will or codicil that expressly or impliedly revokes it; by physical destruction carried out with the intention to revoke, such as deliberately burning or tearing it up; and automatically by the testator's later marriage or civil partnership, subject to limited exceptions, such as a will expressly made in contemplation of that specific marriage. Advise clients clearly that simply writing "revoked" on a will, without either a later valid will or an act of destruction carried out with the necessary intent, does not effectively revoke it, and that a subsequent marriage can unexpectedly invalidate an otherwise perfectly valid existing will if it was not made in contemplation of that marriage.

#### WAE-3 Intestacy rules
Where there is no valid will, or the will does not dispose of the whole estate, the statutory intestacy rules apply a fixed order rather than the deceased's presumed wishes. A surviving spouse or civil partner takes priority, receiving the deceased's personal chattels, a statutory legacy, and a share of the remainder, with any surviving children sharing the rest of the estate under the statutory scheme. If there is no surviving spouse or civil partner, the estate passes down a fixed hierarchy: children first, then parents, then siblings of the whole blood, and so on, down to the Crown as bona vacantia if no qualifying relative survives. A frequently tested trap: unmarried cohabiting partners have no automatic entitlement whatsoever under the intestacy rules, however long the relationship lasted, which makes will-making advice particularly important for cohabiting clients.

#### WAE-4 Obtaining the grant
A grant of probate is obtained where there is a valid will appointing one or more executors who are able and willing to act; the grant confirms the executors' authority to administer the estate according to the will. Letters of administration are obtained instead where there is no valid will, or a will exists but has no proving executor able or willing to act; the person entitled to apply for letters of administration follows a statutory order of priority that broadly mirrors the intestacy beneficiary order. Before either grant can issue, the personal representative must complete the relevant inheritance tax account for the estate and either pay any inheritance tax due, or arrange funding for it, for example through a direct payment scheme drawing on the deceased's own bank accounts.

#### WAE-5 Inheritance tax on death
Inheritance tax is charged on the value of the estate above the nil-rate band, a fixed threshold reviewed periodically, at a rate of 40% on the value above that threshold, subject to a number of reliefs and exemptions. The spouse or civil partner exemption means transfers to a surviving spouse or civil partner are generally exempt from inheritance tax entirely. The charity exemption means gifts to charity are also exempt, and can additionally reduce the rate charged on the rest of the estate to 36% where a sufficient proportion of the estate is left to charity. The transferable nil-rate band allows an unused nil-rate band from a predeceasing spouse to be transferred to increase the surviving spouse's own available band, and the residence nil-rate band provides an additional band where a main residence passes to direct descendants, subject to conditions and tapering for larger estates.

#### WAE-6 Administering the estate
A personal representative follows a standard sequence: identify and value all the estate's assets and liabilities, which forms the basis of the inheritance tax account; obtain the grant, whether probate or letters of administration; collect in the assets, closing accounts and selling or transferring property as needed; pay the deceased's debts, funeral expenses, and any inheritance tax due; and finally distribute what remains to the beneficiaries under the will or the intestacy rules, obtaining proper receipts as each distribution is made. Personal representatives owe a duty to administer the estate properly and can be made personally liable for maladministration, such as distributing the estate before all debts and claims against it have been established, which is exactly why prudent personal representatives place statutory notices for creditors before making any distribution, to protect themselves from later, unexpected claims.
